## THE CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify at the vendor page. Where a fact is contested, say so.

1. A power of attorney (POA) is a document where the principal names an agent (attorney-in-fact) to act on their behalf. A DURABLE POA stays in effect even after the principal becomes incapacitated — that is the version most estate plans use.
2. A POA takes effect while the principal is ALIVE — either immediately on signing, or (a "springing" POA) only once incapacity is determined, per the document's own terms.
3. A POA's authority ENDS automatically at the principal's death. From that moment, the agent has no further legal authority — the will (and probate process) takes over.
4. A last will and testament has NO legal effect during the person's life. It only takes effect after death, once admitted to probate. It directs how assets pass, names an executor, and nominates a guardian for minor children.
5. A POA does not touch what happens to a person's property after death — that is the will's (or, for trust-titled assets, the trust's) job.
6. Typical attorney-drafted cost: a POA runs $150–$700, and is often bundled at no extra charge with an online will package. (Source: this site's own power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship comparison, /compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship/.)
7. Typical cost of a simple attorney-drafted will: $300–$800. A moderate-complexity will (married, with kids): $750–$2,500. Online will services: $0–$299 for the will itself (FreeWill is $0; Trust & Will individual is $199, couple $299; LegalZoom Basic $129/$229). (Source: this site's own online-will-vs-lawyer comparison, /compare/online-will-vs-lawyer/.)
8. Most attorney-drafted "estate planning package" pricing, and most online will services (Trust & Will, LegalZoom, FreeWill, Nolo Quicken WillMaker), bundle a will together with a durable financial POA and a healthcare directive in the same base price — you are not usually buying them as two separate purchases.
9. A POA requires no court filing and no court supervision to create or use. A will, by contrast, must go through PROBATE after death — a court proceeding to validate the will, pay creditors, and distribute assets. Probate typically takes 6–18 months and costs roughly 2–7% of estate value, depending on the state. (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison, /compare/living-trust-vs-will/.)
10. A POA can be revoked at any time, as long as the principal still has legal capacity — by signing a written revocation and notifying the agent and any institution relying on the document. A will can likewise be replaced or amended (via a codicil or a new will) at any time the person has capacity. Neither requires court involvement to revoke or replace.
11. If someone dies WITHOUT a valid will, state intestate succession law decides who inherits — not the person's own wishes. (General framing; do not cite a specific state's intestacy statute or invent a state-by-state list.)
12. If someone becomes incapacitated WITHOUT a valid POA, no family member — including a spouse, in most states — has automatic legal authority to manage that person's finances or make medical decisions. The family must petition a court for guardianship, which is slower and far more expensive than a POA. (Source: this site's own power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship comparison; do not restate its guardianship cost figures here beyond this general point — that is a different page's job. If you need a concrete guardianship cost figure, do not invent one; link to /compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship/ instead.)
13. A will nominates a guardian for minor children directly. A POA has no role in naming a child's guardian.
14. Valid will execution generally requires two witnesses present at signing (in essentially every U.S. state) and, in 48 states plus DC, a notarized self-proving affidavit. Louisiana additionally requires a notarial testament under La. Civ. Code art. 1577. (Source: this site's own online-will-vs-lawyer comparison.) A POA's execution requirement is simpler: signing and notarization (no witnesses requirement to state here beyond that — do not invent a specific witness count for POAs, since it varies by state and is not on this fact list).
15. Neither a POA nor a will avoids probate on assets held in the person's own name at death. Only assets that pass by beneficiary designation (401(k), IRA, life insurance), joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or that are titled into a living trust, skip probate. (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison.)
