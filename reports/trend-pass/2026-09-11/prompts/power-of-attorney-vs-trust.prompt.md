## THE CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify at the vendor page. Where a fact is contested, say so.

1. A power of attorney (POA) is a document where the principal names an agent (attorney-in-fact) to act on the principal's behalf, using accounts and property that stay titled in the PRINCIPAL'S OWN NAME. A durable POA stays in effect through incapacity.
2. A POA's authority ends automatically at the principal's death.
3. A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement where the person (as trustee, or naming a trustee) holds legal TITLE to assets that have been formally RETITLED into the trust's name. The trust continues seamlessly through incapacity (a successor trustee steps in) AND after death (the successor trustee distributes trust assets without probate) — there is no gap at death the way there is with a POA.
4. A trust only controls assets that were actually FUNDED into it — meaning the deed, account, or title was formally changed into the trust's name. An asset never retitled into the trust is NOT covered by the trust, no matter what the trust document says. (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison, /compare/living-trust-vs-will/.)
5. A POA's authority is not tied to any particular asset's title — it covers whatever the document's scope says (narrow or broad), on accounts still in the principal's own name.
6. Typical attorney-drafted POA cost: $150–$700, often bundled free with an online will package. (Source: this site's own power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship comparison, /compare/power-of-attorney-vs-guardianship/.)
7. Typical cost of a revocable living trust: $1,500–$5,000 attorney-drafted ($5,000–$10,000+ in California or other high-net-worth metros); $499–$599 online (Trust & Will); $139 online (Nolo Quicken WillMaker Plus, includes an RLT template). (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison.)
8. A revocable living trust does NOT reduce federal estate tax — assets in a revocable trust remain in the taxable estate because the person retained control (can amend or dissolve the trust). (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison.)
9. Assets titled into a living trust bypass probate at death. Assets left in the principal's own name at death — even with a valid POA in place while alive — still go through probate, because a POA's authority ends at death and does not affect how the decedent's remaining property is retitled. (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison.)
10. Setting up a trust requires an ongoing "funding" step — deed retitling, account beneficiary/ownership updates — every time new property is acquired. A POA requires no such ongoing maintenance once signed.
11. Financial institutions sometimes resist honoring a POA — requiring their own POA form, additional certification, or a delay — a friction point the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance on managing someone else's money specifically addresses. A successor trustee acting under a properly funded trust is typically dealing with an account already titled in the trust's name, which can avoid that same friction (do not claim it always avoids it — state this as a general pattern, not a guarantee, and do not invent a statistic for how often POAs get rejected).
12. A POA has no role in avoiding probate — it only authorizes someone to act while the principal is alive. A trust's main benefit IS probate avoidance (for whatever it holds).
13. The two documents are complementary, not substitutes: even someone with a fully funded living trust still needs a POA (or the trust's own incapacity provisions) to cover any asset never retitled into the trust, and to handle healthcare decisions, which a financial power of attorney or a trust does not cover — that requires a separate healthcare directive/healthcare POA.
14. A living trust does not need court supervision to operate, same as a POA — neither requires ongoing court filings the way a court-imposed guardianship does. (Do not conflate this with probate at death — the trust's benefit is skipping THAT proceeding; its lifetime operation was never court-supervised regardless, same as a POA.)

## What to cover, section by section
1. **What each document actually controls** — a POA authorizes someone to act on accounts still in YOUR name; a trust transfers legal title to assets you've actually retitled into it. This is the core mechanical difference behind the confusion.
2. **Why a trust doesn't replace a POA** — the funding gap (only retitled assets are covered) and the healthcare-decision gap (neither a plain POA-for-finances nor a trust covers medical decisions — that needs its own healthcare directive).
3. **Cost and setup** — the grounded cost figures above, framed as "cheap backstop" (POA) vs. "bigger, costlier commitment" (trust).
4. **Continuity through incapacity and at death** — the trust's no-gap continuity via successor trustee vs. the POA's authority ending at death; the institutional-friction point about banks and POAs, sourced to CFPB.

## Objectivity rule
Neither document is a universal winner. The verdict should state plainly that most people need a POA regardless (cheap, simple, covers incapacity), while a living trust is worth the extra cost mainly for probate avoidance, multi-state property, or privacy — and that the two are usually paired, not chosen between. At least one comparison-table row must favor each option on its own terms (e.g., a POA wins on "cost" and "no ongoing maintenance"; a trust wins on "probate avoidance" and "continuity at death").
