## CLOSED FACT LIST — anything not here, you do not know. Never invent a number, date, or URL.

1. Wealth-management minimums commonly start between $500,000 and $2 million in investable
   assets depending on the firm. Charles Schwab's Wealth Advisory service sets a $500,000
   enrollment minimum; its Private Client Services tier requires $1 million in household assets.
   Fidelity Wealth Management generally requires around $500,000 in general eligibility, and its
   Private Wealth Management tier is built for households with $10 million or more in investable
   assets. (These are the same figures already cited, with the same sources, on this site's
   financial-advisor-vs-wealth-manager comparison page — reuse them verbatim, do not alter the
   numbers.) (Sources: Charles Schwab — Schwab Wealth Advisory; Fidelity — Wealth Management
   Offerings.)
2. Wealth management typically bundles investment management with tax-efficient investing
   strategies, estate-planning coordination (working alongside, not replacing, your attorney),
   and sometimes business-succession or philanthropic planning.
3. Wealth-management fees are often below 1% of assets under management (AUM) at higher account
   balances, similar in structure to standard AUM-based advisor pricing, but because more
   services are bundled in, the total dollar cost of the relationship can be higher than a
   narrower financial-advisor relationship even at a lower percentage rate.
4. Vanguard's own Advisor's Alpha research estimates that good financial advice (behavioral
   coaching, disciplined rebalancing, and tax-aware moves like tax-loss harvesting and asset
   location) can add on the order of 3 percentage points of value a year for many investors,
   though the firm is clear this value is not earned evenly every year and varies by investor.
   This research is about financial-advisor-level portfolio management broadly; it does not
   separately quantify the added value of wealth management's bundled tax/estate coordination on
   top of that. (This is the same figure and framing already used on this site's
   financial-advisor-worth-it guide — reuse it, do not invent a different percentage or claim it
   is wealth-management-specific.) (Source: Vanguard Advisor's Alpha research, as already cited
   on financial-advisor-worth-it.)
5. Below a firm's wealth-management minimum, a household is not locked out of good advice —
   they're simply routed to a different, usually lower- or no-minimum service tier (commonly
   called "financial advisory"), with a narrower scope focused on investment management rather
   than bundled tax/estate coordination.
6. Do not invent a specific dollar figure for what a wealth manager "typically" charges in flat
   dollar terms, or a specific ROI/break-even number — those vary by firm and by client
   complexity; tell the reader to run their own math against their actual AUM and fee quote,
   the same way the site's financial-advisor-worth-it guide does.

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, percentage, or URL beyond what
