1. A custodial Roth IRA can only be funded with a child's own documented earned income (wages or self-employment), capped at the lesser of that income or $7,000 for 2025. (Source: this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-utma and custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-account comparisons.)
2. A custodial Roth IRA's growth and qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free. (Source: this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-utma comparison; IRS Roth IRA rules.)
3. A custodial Roth IRA is opened directly at a brokerage, typically for free or a low minimum, with no attorney required. (Source: this site's own roundup/best-custodial-roth-ira-providers.)
4. A trust is a legal arrangement, typically drafted by an estate planning attorney, that can hold virtually any asset type — cash, securities, real estate, business interests, life insurance — not just financial-account assets. (Source: WebSearch of SmartAsset "How Much Does It Cost to Set Up a Trust" and LegalShield "Trust Fund for Kids: Setup, Costs, and Options", both retrieved 2026-09-19.)
5. A trust has no contribution cap and no earned-income requirement — it can be funded with any amount from any source (a parent, grandparent, or the estate itself). (Source: general trust mechanics; contrast with fact #1's Roth cap.)
6. Attorney-drafted revocable living trust cost: typically $1,500–$4,000 in 2026 for a basic trust package (trust document, pour-over will, power of attorney, healthcare directive); a trust set up specifically to hold assets in a sub-trust for a minor beneficiary, or one with real estate/business interests, commonly runs past $5,000 due to the added drafting complexity. A revocable trust has no required ongoing annual cost, though a later amendment (e.g., changing a beneficiary) typically runs $300–$500 through an attorney. (Source: SmartAsset "How Much Does It Cost to Set Up a Trust", retrieved 2026-09-19.)
7. How a trust's income is taxed depends entirely on its structure — this is the single most important, most-confused fact and must be presented with this exact nuance, not collapsed into one number: (a) A REVOCABLE living trust is normally a "grantor trust" — the person who created it retains control, so its income is reported and taxed on THAT PERSON'S OWN tax return, at their own individual rate, not the trust's. (b) A NON-GRANTOR IRREVOCABLE trust is its own taxpayer and owes tax on any income it retains rather than distributes; its tax brackets are extremely compressed compared to an individual's — a non-grantor trust reaches the top 37% federal bracket on just $15,650 of retained taxable income for 2025, versus $626,350 for an individual filer. A 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax can also apply to a non-grantor trust's retained investment income. (Source: IRS Form 1041 instructions 2025 trust tax rate schedule; Lestna "Trust Income Tax Brackets: Why Trusts Pay 37% on $15,650", retrieved 2026-09-19 — cite the IRS instructions as the primary source and note the compressed-bracket figure is corroborated by secondary tax-planning commentary since the instructions themselves were only partially retrievable.)
8. A custodial account's (UTMA/UGMA) kiddie tax works differently from a trust's compressed brackets: the first $1,350 of a child's unearned income is tax-free, the next $1,350 is taxed at the child's own rate, and anything above $2,700 is taxed at the PARENTS' marginal rate (2025 figures). (Source: this site's own custodial-account-taxes-kiddie-tax guide — do NOT apply this kiddie-tax structure to a trust; a trust is taxed under fact #7's rules instead, since the two are different tax regimes.)
9. A trust lets the person creating it (the grantor) specify exactly when and how the beneficiary receives the money — a single age, staggered ages (e.g., partial distributions at 25, 30, and 35), or conditioned on milestones (e.g., college graduation) — entirely at the grantor's discretion in the trust document. A custodial Roth IRA has no such staggering option; the child gains full legal control of the account at the age of majority (18, or later by state) the same way a UTMA does, though the account's own Roth IRA withdrawal rules still apply to it.
10. A trust requires an ongoing "funding" step for every asset that is meant to be inside it — the deed, account title, or ownership record must be formally changed into the trust's name, or that asset is not covered by the trust no matter what the trust document says. (Source: this site's own living-trust-vs-will comparison mechanics, applied here to the same funding requirement.)
11. Setting up and funding a trust is materially more involved than opening a custodial Roth IRA: a trust typically needs an estate planning attorney to draft it correctly and ongoing attention to fund new assets into it, while a custodial Roth IRA is opened directly at a brokerage in the same way any other account is opened.
12. Do not state a specific FAFSA/financial-aid treatment for trust assets — this depends heavily on the trust's structure (revocable vs. irrevocable, who the trust names as beneficiary) and is unpublished on this list; tell the reader to confirm the treatment of their specific trust with a financial aid professional or the college's aid office. Contrast this explicitly with the custodial Roth IRA, where fact applies: retirement accounts, including a custodial Roth IRA, are NOT reported as an asset on the FAFSA. (Source: this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-utma comparison, for the Roth IRA FAFSA fact only.)
