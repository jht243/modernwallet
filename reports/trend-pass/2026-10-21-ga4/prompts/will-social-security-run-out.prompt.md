## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit/name this page may state
Source for all of it: Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB), "Analysis of the 2026 Social Security Trustees' Report", published June 9, 2026, read 2026-10-21. CRFB summarises the 2026 Social Security Trustees' Report; say "the 2026 Trustees Report, as analysed by CRFB" when attributing.

- The Social Security and Medicare Trustees released their annual reports on June 9, 2026.
- The Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI, retirement and survivors) trust fund is projected to be insolvent (depleted) in 2032. Last year's (2025) report projected 2033, so the date moved one year earlier.
- At OASI insolvency, retirees face an automatic 22% benefit cut, because Social Security cannot legally spend more than its revenues and assets. The cut grows to 38% at the end of the century (2100).
- If the OASI and Disability Insurance (DI) trust funds were theoretically combined, they would run out in 2034, the same date as last year's report, and the cut would be 17% in 2034, growing to 35% by 2100.
- The Trustees recommend that lawmakers address the shortfalls in a timely way so changes can be phased in gradually.
- CRFB estimated for a similar cut that a typical couple retiring in 2033 would face an $18,400 annual reduction to their benefits (CRFB's own earlier estimate, not a Trustees figure).
- The Congressional Budget Office (CBO), earlier in 2026, found the OASI fund would go insolvent in fiscal year 2032, with a 28% average cut to benefits in the years after insolvency, and the theoretically combined funds would run out in 2033. CBO's figures differ from the Trustees' cut size and combined date.
- 2026 cash deficit: the Trustees estimate a combined cash deficit of $270 billion this year. Over the next decade, Social Security will spend $3.8 trillion more than it collects, which is 2.7% of taxable payroll or 0.9% of GDP.
- Annual cash deficits grow from 2.7% of payroll (1.0% of GDP) by 2036 to 3.7% of payroll (1.3% of GDP) in 2050 and 6.6% of payroll (2.2% of GDP) by 2100.
- Costs: total program cost rose from 10.4% of payroll in 2000 to 13.5% in 2010 (when deficits first reappeared) and reached 15.2% in 2025. Costs are projected to reach 16.9% of payroll in 2050 and 20.0% in 2100. Revenues rise from about 13.1% of payroll in 2025 to 13.5% in 2100. Rising costs are driven by the aging of the population and growing benefit generosity (CRFB's characterisation).
- 75-year actuarial deficit: 4.42% of payroll (1.5% of GDP), about $31 trillion on a present-value basis, the largest since 1977. It was 3.82% of payroll in the 2025 report, so it grew 16% (0.60 percentage points). It is 2.3 times the shortfall projected in 2010.
- What worsened the outlook: lower assumed fertility (the Trustees cut the ultimate fertility rate from 1.9 to 1.75 children per woman; 0.35% of payroll), lower assumed immigration of temporary or unlawfully present immigrants (ultimate level 1.2 million a year instead of 1.35 million; 0.21%), and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which reduced revenue from the income taxation of Social Security benefits (0.16% of payroll). Over half of the deterioration was lower fertility, a third lower immigration, a quarter OBBBA (CRFB's breakdown).
- The deterioration is entirely in the retirement trust fund; the disability insurance trust fund's actuarial balance improved slightly, from 0.12% to 0.13% of payroll.
- Fixing it: acting today, the combined funds' long-term solvency could be restored with the equivalent of a 34% (4.25 percentage point) payroll tax increase, a 25% reduction in total benefits, or a 30% reduction in benefits for new beneficiaries. By 2034 the adjustments would be about 15% larger: a 40% (4.9 point) tax increase or a 29% benefit cut for all beneficiaries. Cutting only new beneficiaries' benefits would not be enough even if they were eliminated entirely.
- Reforms such as eliminating the $184,500 payroll tax cap (the 2026 cap) or re-indexing benefits with progressive price indexing would now close around half of the solvency gap.
- The 2027 Social Security taxable maximum is $192,300 and the 2027 COLA is 3.6% (from this site's /guides/social-security-changes-2027/ page; link it rather than restating more).
- "Insolvent" here means the trust fund reserves are exhausted, not that the program ends: benefits would continue, paid from incoming revenue, at the reduced percentage.
- Youngest retirees today turn 68 in 2032 (CRFB's framing: "when today's youngest retirees turn 68").
