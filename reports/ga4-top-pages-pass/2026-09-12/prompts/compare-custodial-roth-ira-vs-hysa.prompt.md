## CLOSED FACT LIST — every number, date, or claim the page may state
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL.

1. A custodial Roth IRA can only be funded with the child's own documented earned income (a summer job,
   babysitting, lawn mowing, or self-employment income), and contributions are capped at the lesser of
   that earned income or $7,000 for 2025. (Reused from this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-
   account page, IRS-sourced.)
2. Money inside a custodial Roth IRA grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals in retirement owe no tax.
   Contributions (not earnings) can generally be withdrawn anytime without tax or penalty; withdrawing
   earnings before age 59½ generally triggers tax plus a 10% penalty, with limited exceptions. (IRS)
3. A custodial Roth IRA is a retirement account and is NOT reported as an asset on the FAFSA. (Reused
   from this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-account page.)
4. A high-yield savings account (HYSA) pays a variable interest rate that moves with the market — it can
   rise or fall — and has no fixed term or maturity date. (Reused from this site's own cd-vs-money-market
   page methodology; HYSA is the same deposit-account category.)
5. A custodial HYSA (a savings account a parent or custodian opens in a child's name) has NO earned-income
   requirement and no age minimum — anyone can deposit gift money or allowance into it at any time,
   unlike a custodial Roth IRA. This is the same funding flexibility a custodial brokerage account has,
   reused from the existing custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-account page's treatment of that funding gap.
6. Deposits in a HYSA at an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union) are protected up to $250,000
   per depositor, per institution. (FDIC; NCUA — same figure already used elsewhere on this site.)
7. A HYSA has no withdrawal age restriction and no early-withdrawal penalty — the money is accessible at
   any time, for any purpose, unlike the Roth IRA's earnings-withdrawal age rule in fact 2.
8. Interest earned in a taxable custodial HYSA is subject to the "kiddie tax": for 2025, a child's
   unearned income above $2,700 is taxed at the parent's marginal rate. (Reused from this site's own
   custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-account page and its dedicated kiddie-tax guide.)
9. A custodial account of either kind (Roth IRA or HYSA opened for a minor) is managed by a custodian
   until the child reaches the age of majority (18 to 21, older in some states), at which point control
   transfers to the child. (Reused from this site's own custodial-roth-ira-vs-utma page treatment.)
10. Do NOT state a specific current HYSA interest rate or APY — rates change constantly and neither this
    site's own pages nor a dated source would stay accurate; tell the reader to compare current rates
    directly rather than citing a number.

## What to cover, section by section
1. Intro — the core mechanical difference: a custodial Roth IRA requires the child's own earned income
   and locks earnings until retirement age in exchange for tax-free growth; a custodial HYSA accepts
   money from anyone, at any time, fully accessible, but pays a taxable, market-driven rate.
2. Section — "The Funding Rule That Decides Whether You Even Have a Choice" — fact 1 vs fact 5: a family
   whose child has no earned income cannot fund a Roth at all, full stop, so the HYSA is often the only
   option regardless of preference.
3. Section — "Tax-Free Growth for Decades vs. a Taxable Rate You Can Touch Anytime" — facts 2, 4, 8: the
   Roth's tax-free compounding over a long horizon vs. the HYSA's kiddie-tax-exposed interest, offset by
   full liquidity.
4. Section — "Access: Locked for Retirement vs. Available Tomorrow" — facts 2 and 7: contributions can
   come out of the Roth anytime, but earnings are effectively locked until 59½; the HYSA has zero lock-in
   at any time, for any goal (a car, college costs, an emergency).
5. Section — "Non-obvious insight: these two accounts are not actually competing for the same dollar" —
   a family can use both: the Roth for a working teen's long-horizon retirement savings (fact 1, 2), and
   a HYSA for near-term goals or for a child too young to have earned income (fact 5) — link to the
   dedicated kiddie-tax guide for the HYSA-interest side and the Roth guide for the Roth side.
6. comparisonTable — dimensions: who can fund it (facts 1, 5), tax treatment (facts 2, 8), access before
   adulthood (facts 2, 7), FDIC/insurance protection (facts 4, 6 for HYSA; note the Roth is not a bank
   deposit and carries market/investment risk instead, not FDIC-insured — this is a real, sourced
   contrast, not an invented one), FAFSA treatment (fact 3 for Roth; state plainly that a custodial HYSA,
   like a custodial brokerage/UTMA, counts as the child's own asset on the FAFSA, assessed at up to 20%,
   reused from the existing custodial-roth-ira-vs-brokerage-account page's identical FAFSA treatment of a
   UTMA/brokerage account — the same funding-source logic applies to a custodial HYSA).
7. verdict — names the condition that decides it (does the child have earned income; is the goal
   retirement-horizon or near-term/flexible), not a blanket winner.
8. FAQs (real autocomplete-derived question shapes):
   - "Can I open a custodial Roth IRA for a child with no income?"
   - "Is a high-yield savings account better than a Roth IRA for a kid?"
   - "Do I pay taxes on interest in a custodial savings account?"
   - "Can a child have both a custodial Roth IRA and a savings account?"
9. Close on a concrete next action: pointing a reader whose child has earned income to the Roth IRA
