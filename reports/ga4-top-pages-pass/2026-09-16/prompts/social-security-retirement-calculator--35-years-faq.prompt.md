## THE CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a number, date, or rule not listed
here or already stated on the page (attached).

1. The Social Security Administration calculates your retirement benefit from your Average
   Indexed Monthly Earnings (AIME), which averages your 35 highest-earning years (after
   indexing each year's earnings for wage growth), per SSA's published benefit-calculation
   methodology.
2. If you have fewer than 35 years of covered earnings, SSA fills each missing year with $0 in
   the averaging calculation — it does not skip those years or average only over the years you
   actually worked. A zero-earning year lowers your average, and therefore your benefit.
3. Because of fact #2, working additional years — including working past the years already
   counted — can raise your benefit if a new year's indexed earnings are higher than the lowest
   year (including any $0 year) currently in your 35-year average, since SSA replaces the lowest
   year with the higher one.
4. Do NOT state a specific dollar benefit amount, a specific number of zero years for any
   hypothetical person, or a specific percentage reduction — the page's existing worked example
   uses a full 35-year-earner ($2,200/month at FRA) and this answer must not contradict or
   restate that figure; it only explains the mechanism for someone with a shorter earnings
   record.
5. This averaging mechanism (AIME/35 years) is separate from the Full Retirement Age reduction
   already covered elsewhere on the page (claiming before FRA) — do not conflate the two; this
   answer covers only the length-of-earnings-record mechanism.
