## CLOSED FACT LIST (anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure, date or URL; if unsure, say to check the IRS page.)
Source A (IRS standard mileage rates page, https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/standard-mileage-rates): 2026 rates in cents per mile. Business: 72.5 (Jan 1-Jun 30), 76 (Jul 1-Dec 31). Medical: 20.5 then 23.5. Moving (military only): 20.5 then 23.5. Charity: 14 then 14. The business, medical and military moving rates rose effective July 1, 2026. The charitable rate did not change. Notices: IR-2025-128 (January-June rates) and IR-2026-29 (July-December rates).
Source B (IRS Topic 502, https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc502): The medical expense deduction covers costs "to the extent these expenses exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income"; you claim it only if you itemize on Schedule A (Form 1040). Medical driving can be counted at the standard mileage rate for medical expenses plus the cost of tolls and parking, or instead at your out-of-pocket expenses for your personal car such as gas and oil. Taxi, bus, train and ambulance fares also qualify.
Source C (general IRS rules, Publications 463 and 526): the charitable rate of 14 cents per mile is set by statute and does not move with the other rates. For charity driving you may instead deduct actual gas and oil costs; either way the charity must be a qualified organization and the deduction requires itemizing. The moving rate applies only to members of the Armed Forces on active duty who move because of a military order and permanent change of station. Keep a log with date, miles and purpose of each trip.
WORKED EXAMPLE (computed; use exactly): 600 medical miles in the second half of 2026 at 23.5 cents = $141.00, plus tolls and parking if any. 200 volunteer miles at 14 cents = $28.00. The same 200 miles at the 76-cent business rate would be $152.00, which is wrong for charity driving. Simplified: deduction only helps if medical costs total above 7.5% of AGI and the filer itemizes.
