## THE CLOSED FACT LIST
Sources read 2026-10-21 (statute and regulation text on law.cornell.edu): 20 U.S.C. 1087e(m) (PSLF); 20 U.S.C. 1087e(d)(1)(D) (income-contingent repayment); 20 U.S.C. 1078-10 (Teacher Loan Forgiveness); 34 CFR 685.213 (total and permanent disability discharge); 26 U.S.C. 108 (tax treatment); the site's own guide on the Standard Repayment Plan changes.
- PSLF: the borrower must have made 120 monthly payments on an eligible Federal Direct Loan after October 1, 2007, under a qualifying repayment plan, and must have been employed in a public service job during the period in which the borrower made each of the 120 payments (20 U.S.C. 1087e(m)(1)). A public service job includes a full-time job in emergency management or government (excluding time as a member of Congress) and other listed fields (1087e(m)(3)(B)). Eligible loans are Federal Direct Stafford, Direct PLUS and Direct Unsubsidized Stafford loans, and Direct Consolidation loans (1087e(m)(3)(A)). The statute does not require the 120 payments to be consecutive in its text (do not claim they are or are not consecutive beyond saying so is not stated in the statute text read).
- Income-contingent repayment: the statute says repayment is "paid over an extended period of time prescribed by the Secretary, not to exceed 25 years," and that the plan is available "before June 30, 2028" (20 U.S.C. 1087e(d)(1)(D)). It is not available for Direct PLUS loans made on behalf of a dependent student. Other income-driven plans (PAYE and others) have their own forgiveness periods that vary; say the length depends on the plan and the borrower, and the reader should check studentaid.gov.
- Plan changes (from the site's guide): on July 1, 2026 the One Big Beautiful Bill Act began replacing the old menu of repayment plans with a Tiered Standard plan and an income-driven Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP); borrowers on ICR or PAYE face a full sunset date of July 1, 2028 under the law. The Tiered Standard plan offers no forgiveness at the end of its term.
- Teacher Loan Forgiveness: the teacher must have been employed as a full-time teacher for 5 consecutive complete school years at a school that meets the low-income criteria; the Secretary may repay not more than $5,000 in the aggregate; the limit is not more than $17,500 for qualifying secondary school math or science teachers and for qualifying special education teachers; the borrower must not be in default on the loan (20 U.S.C. 1078-10(b)(1), (b)(2), (c)(1), (c)(3)).
- Total and permanent disability: a borrower's Direct Loan is discharged if the borrower becomes totally and permanently disabled (34 CFR 685.213(a)(1)). A physician (doctor of medicine or osteopathy legally authorized to practice in a state) can certify, and the regulation also allows a nurse practitioner, physician assistant or certified psychologist; instead of a certification the borrower can submit Social Security Administration documentation showing SSDI or SSI disability benefits; for veterans the Department of Veterans Affairs must have determined the veteran is unemployable due to a service-connected disability. The loan can be reinstated if, within 3 years after the discharge, the borrower receives a new TEACH Grant or a new Direct Loan (with exceptions for certain consolidation loans). The regulation sets no ongoing monitoring period after discharge.
- Tax (from the site's guide /guides/is-student-loan-forgiveness-taxable/): discharges for death or total and permanent disability after December 31, 2025 are excluded from federal gross income under 26 U.S.C. 108(f)(5); a temporary exclusion covered certain student loan discharges after December 31, 2020 and before January 1, 2026; forgiveness not covered by an exclusion is generally income in the year it is canceled.
- Borrower defense: borrowers misled or defrauded by their school may apply for relief through studentaid.gov; we could not confirm this run how the Education Department is currently deciding claims, so tell the reader to check studentaid.gov for status. State no timelines or amounts.
- Private student loans are not covered by these federal programs.
- No broad one-time forgiveness program is part of this fact list; do not discuss any proposed or blocked broad forgiveness plan.
