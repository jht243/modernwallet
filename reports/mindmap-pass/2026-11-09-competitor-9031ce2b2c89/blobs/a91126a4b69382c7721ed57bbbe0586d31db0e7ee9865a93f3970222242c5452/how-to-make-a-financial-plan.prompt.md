## Planned takeaway (inside the "Our Takeaway" section)
Treat a financial plan as a repeating cycle: list your finances, set dated goals, test your current course, act, then review, and start an emergency fund early, as the CFPB suggests. Applies to households starting a plan; complex tax, estate or investment questions may need a professional. Evidence basis: reported (CFP Board, CFPB). Changes if you carry high-interest debt. Next step: write down your income, spending, savings and debts today and pick one dated goal.

FP2. CFP Board guide (via the Code page): the first step covers obtaining qualitative and quantitative information about the client; examples of information a planner gathers include income, expenses, savings, assets, liabilities, insurance coverage and estate plans, plus goals, priorities and risk tolerance. (Present as the kinds of facts to gather, from the CFP Board's process; do not claim an official checklist of plan components.)
FP3. CFPB, "An essential guide to building an emergency fund" (checked 2026-11-09): an emergency fund is a cash reserve set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies, such as car repairs, home repairs, medical bills or a loss of income. Setting one up is one essential way to protect yourself and "one of the first steps you can take to start saving". Even a small amount helps you recover quicker and stay on track toward larger savings goals.
FP4. CFPB emergency fund guide: how much you need depends on your situation; think about the most common unexpected expenses you have had and how much they cost, and set a goal from that. If you are living paycheck to paycheck, putting any money aside can feel difficult but even a small amount can provide some financial security. Strategies include creating a savings habit (set a goal, make consistent contributions, monitor progress), managing your cash flow, and putting away a portion of your tax refund; automatic recurring transfers are often one of the easiest ways to save. (No target number of months is on this list; do not state one.)
FP5. CFPB Your Money, Your Goals toolkit: free tools for setting goals and working through money challenges (cited as a resource only).
FP6. Investor.gov and CFPB sources do not rank paying down high-interest debt against building a reserve; say the sources read do not settle that trade-off and that a nonprofit credit counselor (see CFPB) or a financial professional can look at the specifics.
