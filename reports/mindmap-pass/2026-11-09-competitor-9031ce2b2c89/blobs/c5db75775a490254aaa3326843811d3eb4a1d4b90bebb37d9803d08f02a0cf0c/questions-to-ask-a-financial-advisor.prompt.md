## CLOSED FACT LIST (everything the page may state; anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure, fee, date, rule or URL; if it is not on this list, say it is not covered here)
FQ1. A registered firm's Form CRS (the SEC-prescribed Client Relationship Summary, as printed by one broker-dealer that is also an investment adviser, retrieved 2026-11-09) prints these "Questions to Ask to Start the Conversation": (a) Given my financial situation, should I choose an investment advisory service? Should I choose a brokerage service? Should I choose both types of services? Why or why not? (b) How will you choose investments to recommend to me? (c) What is your relevant experience, including your licenses, education and other qualifications? What do these qualifications mean?
FQ2. Same Form CRS, fees question: "Help me understand how these fees and costs might affect my investments. If I give you $10,000 to invest, how much will go to fees and costs, and how much will be invested for me?" The form notes that fees and costs reduce any amount of money you make on your investments over time, and that you pay fees whether you make or lose money.
FQ3. Same Form CRS: What are your legal obligations to me when providing recommendations as my broker-dealer or when acting as my investment adviser? How else does your firm make money, and what conflicts of interest do you have? Question to ask: "How might your conflicts of interest affect me, and how will you address them?"
FQ4. Same Form CRS: How do your financial professionals make money? The firm explains that its professionals can be paid through commissions, advisory fees or both, and that higher commissions, costs or fees could lead to higher compensation for a financial professional. (Present as an example of what such a disclosure says, from one firm, not as how every firm pays.)
FQ5. Investor.gov Working with an Investment Professional page and Form CRS page: a Form CRS asks whether the firm and its professionals have legal or disciplinary history and for what type of conduct; Investor.gov offers a free search to check registration.
FA1. Investor.gov, Investment Advisers page (checked 2026-11-09): "Investment advisers are required to act in your best interest and not put their interest ahead of yours. At the same time, the way advisers make money creates some conflicts with your interests." You should understand and ask an adviser about these conflicts because they can affect the investment advice they provide.
FA2. Investor.gov Investment Advisers page: advisers typically provide ongoing advice about buying, selling and/or holding investments, and will monitor the performance of your investments and their alignment with your overall investment objectives. The fee you pay is typically based on the value of all of the assets held in your account with the adviser. You may pay other fees and costs related to servicing your account and the investments that you buy, sell or hold.
FA3. Investor.gov Investment Advisers page: advisers also may give advice about market trends or asset allocation or offer financial planning services.
FA4. Investor.gov Investment Advisers page, "Selecting an adviser": before selecting an adviser consider what services and products you need, what services and products the adviser can provide, any limitations on what they can provide, how much you will pay for services and transactions, how the adviser gets paid, what conflicts of interest the adviser may have, and whether the adviser has legal or disciplinary history and, if so, for what type of conduct.
FA5. Investor.gov, Working with an Investment Professional page: the most important question to consider before hiring an investment professional is whether the person is registered with the SEC or with a state securities regulator. It is "really risky to invest with someone who isn't licensed". Investor.gov has a free and simple search tool that shows whether your investment professional is licensed and registered.
FA6. Investor.gov Working with an Investment Professional page: registered broker-dealers and registered investment advisers are required to give retail investors a customer or client relationship summary (Form CRS). It tells you the types of services a firm offers, the fees and costs you will pay, conflicts of interest a broker or adviser may have, the required standard of conduct associated with the services, whether the firm and its financial professionals have reportable legal or disciplinary history, and key questions (conversation starters) to ask. Relationship summaries use similar headings and order of topics so you can compare firms.
FA7. Investor.gov Working with an Investment Professional page, "Tricky Titles": if a broker or adviser has initials after their name, do not assume that makes that individual more qualified than another. The titles are not all the same and do not necessarily mean better service for you; the initials may mean the adviser or broker can only sell certain products. The page points to a FINRA list showing how some credentials are easy to obtain and others hard-earned.
FA8. Investor.gov Form CRS page: both brokers and advisers can give advice about buying investments like stock in a company or an index fund, and about whether to sell or hold afterward. Many firms are registered as both a broker and an adviser (dual registrants) and offer both advisory and brokerage services and accounts; in some cases a combination may be a good choice depending on your goals, preferences and needs.
