## CLOSED FACT LIST (everything the page may state; anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure, fee, date, rule or URL; if it is not on this list, say it is not covered here)
FB1. Investor.gov glossary, "Robo-adviser": the term "generally refers to an automated digital investment advisory program. In most cases, the robo-adviser collects information regarding your financial goals, investment horizon, income and other assets, and risk tolerance by asking you to complete an online questionnaire. Based on that information, it creates and manages an investment portfolio for you." Robo-advisers often seek to offer investment advice for lower costs and fees than traditional advisory programs and in some cases require lower account minimums. The services, approaches to investing and features vary widely.
FB2. SEC Investor Bulletin: Robo-Advisers (Feb. 23, 2017): these programs let individual investors create and manage their investment accounts through a web portal or mobile application, sometimes with little or no interaction with a human being, with the potential benefit of lower costs than traditional advisory programs. You can find information about a robo-adviser's services, approaches and features in its Form ADV Part 1 and Part 2 brochure.
FB3. SEC bulletin: the amount of human interaction varies. Some robo-advisers let you contact an investment professional (a hybrid of automated and personal advice sometimes called "bionic" advice); others offer only technical support staff. Some offer a person only for accounts that meet a minimum size. Unlike a traditional adviser, there may be no initial or later conversation with a person to gather information about your needs. Questions to consider: how much human interaction is important to you, would you like to speak with a person during market events such as downturns, and what is your level of financial literacy.
FB4. SEC bulletin: a robo-adviser's recommendation is limited by the information it requests and receives from you, typically through an online questionnaire; some obtain and consider only limited information, and in many cases the burden to update the information falls on you. Questions to consider: does it ask about high-interest credit card debt or student loans, bank and savings accounts, real estate holdings, retirement accounts or other assets, and how does it take your risk tolerance into account, including when it rebalances after a market decline.
FB5. SEC bulletin: approaches differ: some have several pre-determined portfolios you may or may not be able to customize; some use a limited range of products such as broad-based ETFs; "in some cases robo-advisers may not have been tested under stressed market conditions". Questions: what account types it manages (IRAs, taxable, 401(k), college savings), how it handles volatility (some may be able to freeze sales), how often your account is rebalanced (rebalancing can have tax implications), whether it uses tax loss harvesting (its value depends on your tax situation and it may implicate wash-sale rules; consider a tax adviser).
FB6. SEC bulletin: fees and other costs can greatly impact your return; one of the main benefits of a robo-adviser can be lower fees and costs, so understand what you would be charged. A robo-adviser may offer lower-cost advice, but if it uses investment products with high costs your total overall costs could still be high. In some cases a robo-adviser may offer services not significantly different from those available through a traditional advisory program or a product such as a target date retirement fund. (No fee percentage is on this list; do not state one.)
FB7. Investor.gov: robo-advisers are investment advisers; the SEC bulletin tells readers to check registration. FA5 applies: check whether the firm is registered with the SEC or a state securities regulator before opening an account.
FA1. Investor.gov, Investment Advisers page (checked 2026-11-09): "Investment advisers are required to act in your best interest and not put their interest ahead of yours. At the same time, the way advisers make money creates some conflicts with your interests." You should understand and ask an adviser about these conflicts because they can affect the investment advice they provide.
FA5. Investor.gov, Working with an Investment Professional page: the most important question to consider before hiring an investment professional is whether the person is registered with the SEC or with a state securities regulator. It is "really risky to invest with someone who isn't licensed". Investor.gov has a free and simple search tool that shows whether your investment professional is licensed and registered.
