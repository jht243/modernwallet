## CLOSED FACT LIST (everything the page may state; anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a limit, rate, date or URL; if it is not on this list, say it is not covered here)
F1. IRS Publication 969 (page last reviewed Apr 30, 2026), "What are the benefits of an HSA?": you can claim a tax deduction for contributions you or someone other than your employer make to your HSA even if you do not itemize deductions on Schedule A (Form 1040).
F2. IRS Pub. 969: contributions to your HSA made by your employer (including contributions made through a cafeteria plan) may be excluded from your gross income.
F3. IRS Pub. 969: the interest or other earnings on the assets in the account are tax free. Distributions may be tax free if you pay qualified medical expenses.
F4. IRS Pub. 969: the contributions remain in your account until you use them; an HSA is "portable": it stays with you if you change employers or leave the work force.
F5. IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19 and Pub. 969: for calendar year 2026 the annual contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only high-deductible health plan (HDHP) coverage and $8,750 for family HDHP coverage.
F6. IRS Pub. 969: if you are an eligible individual who is age 55 or older at the end of your tax year, your contribution limit is increased by $1,000.
F7. IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19 and Pub. 969: for 2026 a high deductible health plan has an annual deductible of at least $1,700 for self-only coverage or $3,400 for family coverage, and annual out-of-pocket expenses (deductibles, co-payments and other amounts, but not premiums) no more than $8,500 for self-only coverage or $17,000 for family coverage. (Pub. 969 notes the out-of-pocket maximum does not apply to out-of-network services if the plan uses a network of providers; only in-network deductibles and out-of-pocket expenses count toward the limit.)
F8. Contributions to all your HSAs together cannot exceed the limit (Pub. 969: if you have more than one HSA, your total contributions to all of them cannot be more than the limits). You must reduce what can be contributed by any employer contributions that are excludable from your income.
F9. Arithmetic on F5 and F6 (label as arithmetic): $4,400 + $1,000 = $5,400 for an eligible self-only person age 55 or older in 2026. Do not compute a family total with the age-55 amount (Pub. 969 has spouse-specific rules).
F10. IRS Pub. 969: beginning with the first month you are enrolled in Medicare, your contribution limit is zero. This rule applies to periods of retroactive Medicare coverage: if you delayed applying for Medicare and your enrollment is backdated, contributions made during the retroactive period are considered excess.
F11. IRS Pub. 969: under the last-month rule, if you have family HDHP coverage on the first day of the last month of your tax year you can contribute the full-year limit, but if you fail to remain an eligible individual during the testing period (other than for death or disability) you include in income the contributions that would not have been made except for the last-month rule, plus a 10% additional tax (Form 8889, Part III).
F12. IRS Pub. 969: generally you must pay a 6% excise tax on excess contributions; the excise tax applies to each tax year the excess contribution remains in the account (Form 5329).
F13. IRS Pub. 969: if you take distributions for reasons other than qualified medical expenses, the amount is subject to income tax and may be subject to an additional 20% tax. There is no additional tax on distributions made after the date you are disabled, reach age 65, or die. You are permitted to take a distribution at any time; only amounts used exclusively for qualified medical expenses are tax free.
F14. IRS Pub. 969: you may not use HSA funds to pay for insurance, except long-term care insurance, health care continuation coverage (such as COBRA), health care coverage while receiving unemployment compensation, and Medicare and other health care coverage if you were 65 or older (other than premiums for a Medicare supplemental policy such as Medigap).
F15. IRS Pub. 969: you cannot deduct, as an itemized deduction on Schedule A, qualified medical expenses equal to the tax-free distribution from your HSA.
F16. IRS Pub. 969: you do not have to make withdrawals from your HSA each year; amounts that remain at the end of the year are generally carried over to the next year; an HSA is generally exempt from tax.
F17. IRS Pub. 969: you can roll over amounts from Archer MSAs and other HSAs into an HSA; rollovers are not subject to the annual contribution limit; you do not need to be an eligible individual to roll over from an existing HSA to a new HSA.
F18. IRS Pub. 969: contributions are reported on Form 8889 with your Form 1040; the trustee reports contributions on Form 5498-SA and distributions on Form 1099-SA; employer contributions appear on Form W-2, box 12, code W.
F21. IRS Pub. 969: for the last-month rule the testing period begins with the last month of your tax year and ends on the last day of the 12th month following that month (the page text on the pages read says it 'ends on th[e last day of the 12th month following that month]' for the qualified funding distribution case; use this definition only in that form: begins with the last month of the tax year, ends on the last day of the 12th month after that month).
F22. The existing ModernWallet guide /guides/medicare-savings-programs/ says your state can pay your Medicare premiums through a Medicare Savings Program if you qualify.
F23. Routing: for state income tax treatment and for your own tax saving, a tax professional can apply your state's rules and your marginal rate. Use this as the route wherever the page names those two limits; do not list custodian fees, employer amounts or 2027 limits as open items (delete those bullets).
