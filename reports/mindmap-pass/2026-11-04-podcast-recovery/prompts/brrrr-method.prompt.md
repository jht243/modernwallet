## Planned takeaway (inside section 7 "Our Takeaway")
Conclusion: BRRRR can recycle most or all of an investor's cash only when the post-repair appraisal is high enough that the loan covers the money in the deal: in our modeled example that took about $262,585 on $193,000 invested. It fits an investor who can wait out lender seasoning and buys below the finished value; it does not fit anyone counting on a low appraisal, on a refinance inside six months to recover repair spending (on our reading of the wording; ask the lender), or on a payment the rent cannot cover. ## CLOSED FACT LIST (everything the page may state; anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a rate, limit, LTV, rent figure, date or URL; if it is not on this list, say it is not covered here)
F1. Definition (investor shorthand; no primary source; state it as how investors describe the strategy, not as an official definition): BRRRR stands for buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat. BRRR is the same without the "repeat" step.
F2. Fannie Mae Selling Guide B2-1.3-03, Cash-Out Refinance Transactions (12/10/2025): acceptable uses of a cash-out refinance include taking equity out of the subject property that may be used for any purpose. The transaction must pay off existing mortgage loans by obtaining a new first mortgage secured by the same property, or be a new mortgage on a property with no mortgage lien.
F3. Fannie Mae B2-1.3-03: if an existing first mortgage is being paid off through the transaction, it must be at least 12 months old at the time of refinance, measured from the note date of the existing loan to the note date of the new loan (this does not apply to subordinate liens paid off, or when buying out a co-owner under a legal agreement).
F4. Fannie Mae B2-1.3-03: at least one borrower must have been on title for at least six months prior to the disbursement date of the new loan (see the guide's "Ownership of the Property" for exceptions).
F5. Fannie Mae B2-1.3-03: properties that were listed for sale must have been taken off the market on or before the disbursement date of the new mortgage loan.
F6. Fannie Mae B2-1.3-03: for the maximum allowable loan-to-value (LTV), combined LTV and home equity combined LTV ratios and credit score requirements for manually underwritten cash-out refinances, the guide points to its Eligibility Matrix. (No LTV figure from Fannie Mae is on this list; the page uses none.)
F7. Fannie Mae B2-1.3-03, Delayed Financing Exception: borrowers who purchased the subject property within the past six months (measured from the purchase date to the disbursement date of the new loan) are eligible for a cash-out refinance if all requirements are met: the original purchase was an arms-length transaction; the borrower meets Fannie Mae's borrower eligibility requirements; the purchase is documented by a settlement statement confirming no mortgage financing was used; the title search confirms no existing liens; the sources of funds for the purchase are documented; if the funds came from an unsecured loan or a loan secured by another asset (such as a HELOC on another property), the refinance settlement statement must show all cash-out proceeds paying off or down that loan; gift funds used to purchase may not be reimbursed with the new loan; the new loan can be no more than the documented amount of the borrower's initial investment in purchasing the property plus the financing of closing costs, prepaid fees and points on the new loan, subject to the maximum LTV on the current appraised value; cash-out pricing applies.
F8. Fannie Mae's rules apply to loans sold to or guaranteed by Fannie Mae through the Selling Guide; other lenders and loan types (portfolio, DSCR and others) set their own rules, which are not covered here.
F9. Our scenario inputs (illustrative, author-chosen, saved in evidence/brrrr-method/inputs.json): purchase price $150,000 paid in cash; purchase closing costs $3,000; repairs $40,000; total cash invested $193,000. Assumptions, not lender limits and not Fannie Mae figures: a 75% loan-to-value on the appraised value and a 2% cost on the new loan.
F10. Our scenario results (evidence/brrrr-method/output.json), standard cash-out path: at a $200,000 appraisal the loan is $150,000, net proceeds $147,000 and $46,000 stays in the deal; at $230,000 the loan is $172,500, net proceeds $169,050 and $23,950 stays in; at $260,000 the loan is $195,000, net proceeds $191,100 and $1,900 stays in. Full recovery needs a loan of about $196,939 and an appraisal of about $262,585, which is 1.36 times the $193,000 invested.
F11. Our scenario results, delayed-financing-style cap path (the author's reading: loan capped at the $153,000 initial purchase investment, which is price plus purchase closing costs, plus the 2% financing of the new loan's costs, so about $156,122; Fannie Mae does not state how repair spending is treated): at $200,000 the loan is $150,000 (the 75% limit binds) and $46,000 stays in the deal; at $230,000 and at $260,000 the loan is about $156,122, net proceeds $153,000 and $40,000 stays in the deal.
F12. IRS Publication 527 (2025; page last reviewed Apr 30, 2026): residential rental property placed in service after 1986 is generally depreciated under MACRS; the recovery period for residential rental property (buildings and structural components) is 27.5 years under the general depreciation system (GDS) and 30 years under the alternative system (ADS).
F13. IRS Pub. 527 example: a house bought as a rental for $185,000 with building cost $160,000 and land $25,000 has a depreciation basis of $160,000 (its original cost excluding land); placed in service February 8 under GDS with a 27.5-year recovery period the first-year percentage is 3.182%, a deduction of $5,091 ($160,000 x 3.182%).
F14. IRS Pub. 527 example: a house bought, extensively repaired and ready for rent on July 5, advertised in July and rented beginning September 1, is considered placed in service in July when it was ready and available for rent, and depreciation can begin then.
F15. Not covered: rent, cash flow, the new interest rate and monthly payment, property taxes, insurance, holding costs, vacancy, income taxes, any lender's actual investment-property LTV and any individual deal. The scenario excludes all of them. Say so rather than estimating.
F16. The "2% rule" and the "70% rule" are investor rules of thumb. No primary source on this list defines either, and no figure from them may be used as a standard. Say they are unverified rules of thumb and send the reader to the break-even in section 2.
F17. A flip ends with a sale of the property; a BRRRR ends with a refinance loan and a rental the investor keeps (follows from F1 and F2). Do not state which is better; say which goal each fits.
F18. Timing: the Fannie Mae floor on title is six months (F4) and an existing mortgage being paid off must be 12 months old (F3); repair and lease-up time are not published on any page read and are not estimated.
