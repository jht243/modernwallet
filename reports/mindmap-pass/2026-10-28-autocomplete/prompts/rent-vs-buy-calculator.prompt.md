## The calculator (describe only what it does)
Inputs: home price; down payment %; mortgage rate %; loan term (years); how long you will stay (years); monthly rent for a similar home; property tax % of value a year; home insurance % a year; maintenance % a year; HOA dues a month; closing costs when buying %; selling costs %; home price growth a year %; rent growth a year %; investment return a year %; renter's insurance a month.
Outputs: net worth after the stay if you buy, net worth if you rent and invest, which is ahead and by how much, the break-even year (first year-end at which buying is ahead, or "not within 30 years"), the mortgage payment (principal and interest), the first-month cost of owning and of renting, cash needed to buy, the price-to-rent ratio (price divided by 12 months of rent), and a year-by-year table (years 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 30).
Method the page may state: Buying costs the down payment and closing costs on day one, then every month the mortgage payment, property tax, insurance and maintenance (each a percent of the home's current value), HOA, and mortgage insurance (0.5% of the original loan a year, charged while the loan is above 80% of the price and the down payment is under 20%). Renting costs the rent (which grows each year) plus renter's insurance, and the same day-one cash (down payment plus closing costs) is invested. Each month the cheaper path invests the monthly difference. At every year-end, buying net worth = home value × (1 − selling cost) − loan balance + the buyer's own investments; renting net worth = the renter's investments. The break-even year is the first year-end at which buying net worth is at least renting net worth.
Assumptions the page must state: modeled, not measured, and not a forecast of home prices, rents or returns; the default mortgage rate is Freddie Mac's 30-year average (7.32%, week of October 22, 2026); the other defaults (1.0% property tax, 0.5% insurance, 1.0% maintenance, 3% closing, 6% selling, 3% home price growth, 3% rent growth, 6% investment return, $15 renter's insurance) are planning assumptions the reader should replace with local figures; property tax and insurance vary a lot by state; income-tax effects of owning (mortgage interest and property tax deductions help only filers who itemize), moving costs and special assessments are not modeled.
Provenance (modeled, internal modeling): "In our internal modeling, we compared buying with renting and investing the same cash over 1 to 30 years, then changed one assumption at a time to see which ones move the break-even year." The model was written twice (in Python and in the calculator's TypeScript code) and the two agreed on every scenario below. These are modeled scenarios, not measurements, not tests of any product and not client cases. Tell the reader they can enter the same numbers in the calculator above to check them.
Takeaway (in howItWorks, near the end): buy if you expect to stay past the break-even year and the all-in monthly cost of owning is not far above rent; at the defaults the break-even is year 21, so a seven-year stay favors renting and investing the difference; a lower rate, higher rent or faster home price growth moves the break-even earlier; it reverses if home prices stall or the invested cash earns more than assumed. Informational, not financial advice.
