## Early withdrawal and rollover (IRS "Retirement topics: Tax on early distributions", https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-tax-on-early-distributions ; IRS "Retirement plans FAQs regarding SIMPLE IRA plans", https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-simple-ira-plans)
F1. A 10% additional tax generally applies to early distributions (before age 59 1/2) from qualified plans, which include 401(k) plans, and from traditional IRAs, including the IRAs that hold SIMPLE IRA money, unless an exception applies.
F2. Distributions from a SIMPLE IRA within the first 2 years of participation incur a 25% additional tax instead of 10%.
F3. The 2-year period begins on the first day your employer deposits contributions into your SIMPLE IRA.
F4. The separation-from-service exception (the employee separates from service during or after the year the employee reaches age 55) is available for distributions from qualified plans such as a 401(k) and is NOT available for IRAs, which includes SIMPLE IRAs. For a 401(k) it covers money from the plan of the employer you leave, so it helps only someone who leaves the employer that sponsors that plan in or after the year they turn 55.
F5. The usual exceptions to the early-distribution tax (for example age 59 1/2 or older, disability, a beneficiary of a deceased owner, a series of substantially equal payments, a first-home purchase up to $10,000) also apply to SIMPLE IRA money inside the 2-year period, and when one applies the 25% tax does not apply.
F6. During the 2-year period, SIMPLE IRA money can only be moved to another SIMPLE IRA. If it is paid to any other IRA, the IRS treats it as a withdrawal: you include the amount in gross income and pay the additional 25% tax unless an exception applies.
F7. After the 2-year period, SIMPLE IRA money can be moved tax-free to another IRA (other than a Roth IRA) or to an employer plan such as a 401(k), 403(b) or governmental 457(b) plan. After the 2-year period it can also be rolled into a Roth IRA, but the amount must be included in income.
F8. The employer sets up and sponsors the plan (the IRS describes the employer establishing a SIMPLE IRA plan), so an employee usually cannot choose which plan type the employer offers.
F9. Neither plan avoids the 10% tax on money taken out before 59 1/2 unless an exception applies, so neither suits money you will need before 59 1/2.

## Employer contribution (IRS "Retirement topics: SIMPLE IRA contribution limits", https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-simple-ira-contribution-limits)
F10. The employer is generally required to match each employee's salary reduction contributions dollar for dollar up to 3% of the employee's compensation. This requirement does not apply if the employer makes nonelective contributions instead.
F11. An employer may choose a match lower than 3%, but it must be at least 1% and for no more than 2 out of 5 years. The employer must notify employees of the lower match within a reasonable period before the 60-day election period for the calendar year.
F12. Instead of matching, an employer can make a nonelective contribution of 2% of each eligible employee's compensation, and must make it whether or not the employee contributes. The employer must notify employees within a reasonable period before the 60-day election period.
F13. So the employer must still contribute every year the plan operates: a match (normally up to 3%, as low as 1% in up to 2 of 5 years with notice) or the 2% nonelective contribution. A 401(k) employer match is optional (existing page fact); safe harbor 401(k) designs require a set contribution (existing page fact).
F14. A 401(k) can skip a match in a lean year (existing page fact); a SIMPLE IRA employer cannot skip contributions entirely, but can lower the match to 1% in up to 2 of 5 years with notice.

## Who can sponsor and who must be included (IRS SIMPLE IRA FAQ, https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-simple-ira-plans)
F15. Any employer (including self-employed individuals, tax-exempt organizations and governmental entities) that had no more than 100 employees with $5,000 or more in compensation during the preceding calendar year can establish a SIMPLE IRA plan (the "100-employee limitation"). The count includes all employees employed at any time during the calendar year, including employees who have not met the plan's eligibility requirements.
F16. Grace period: an employer that previously maintained a SIMPLE IRA plan satisfies the 100-employee limitation for the 2 calendar years immediately following the calendar year for which it last satisfied it. There are special rules for acquisitions and similar transactions; the IRS says to see a tax advisor.
F17. All employees who received at least $5,000 in compensation from the employer during any 2 preceding calendar years (whether or not consecutive) and who are reasonably expected to receive at least $5,000 in compensation in the current calendar year are eligible to participate. For this test, compensation includes the employee's elective deferrals under a 401(k), SARSEP or 403(b) plan.
F18. An employee may not opt out of participation. An eligible employee may choose not to make salary reduction contributions for a year; then the employee gets no employer match for that year, but does receive the employer nonelective contribution if the plan provides for it.
F19. A SIMPLE IRA plan cannot have a last-day-of-the-year employment requirement. An eligible employee shares in any SIMPLE IRA contribution, including an eligible employee who quits or dies before the contribution is made.
F20. One-plan requirement: generally an employer cannot contribute to a SIMPLE IRA plan for a calendar year if it maintains another retirement plan and any employee receives an allocation or accrues a benefit under the other plan that year. One exception: the other plan covers only employees under a collective bargaining agreement and the SIMPLE IRA plan excludes them.
F21. An employee can participate in a SIMPLE IRA plan even if also in a plan sponsored by a different employer in the same year; the employee's total salary reduction contributions across all plans is limited to $24,500 in 2026 (IRS contributions page).
F22. From the existing page: employers who grow past 100 employees are disqualified from SIMPLE IRA eligibility and typically move to a 401(k); the switch takes effect January 1; the 2026 SIMPLE IRA employee limit is $17,000 and the 401(k) limit is $24,500.

## Planned takeaway (conditional, must agree with the verdict, table, intro and FAQs)
T1. Money taken out of a SIMPLE IRA in the first 2 years of participation costs a 25% additional tax instead of 10%; after those 2 years the rate matches the 10% that applies to a 401(k) before 59 1/2. The age-55 separation exception exists only for a 401(k) (and only for money from the employer you leave), not for SIMPLE IRAs. Neither plan suits money needed before 59 1/2. Most employees cannot choose the plan, so this matters mainly to owners choosing a plan and employees deciding how much to defer. The choice still turns on headcount, the required employer contribution and administration cost. The employer must contribute to a SIMPLE IRA every year (a match of normally up to 3%, as low as 1% in up to 2 of 5 years, or 2% nonelective), which a 401(k) employer does not have to do. What would change this: a change in the IRS rules.
