## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number, date, rule the page may state

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a dollar figure, percentage, IRS form
number, or rule not on this list; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify with a
tax professional or the cited IRS source.

1. A **qualified annuity** — bought with pre-tax money inside a traditional IRA, 401(k), or
   similar tax-advantaged retirement account — is fully taxable as ordinary income when
   withdrawn, because no after-tax dollars were contributed. [IRS Topic 410 — Pensions and
   Annuities]
2. A **non-qualified annuity** — bought with after-tax money outside a retirement account —
   is only partly taxable: the after-tax contributions ("investment in the contract" / cost
   basis) come back tax-free, and only the earnings portion is taxed. [IRS Topic 410; IRS
   Publication 575]
3. For **non-annuitized withdrawals** (lump sums or partial withdrawals, not turned into a
   stream of payments) from a non-qualified annuity, the IRS uses an **earnings-first rule**:
   the withdrawal is treated as coming from earnings (fully taxable) first, until all the gain
   is withdrawn, and only then as a tax-free return of cost basis. Contracts purchased before
   August 14, 1982 use a different (pro-rata) ordering. [IRS Publication 575]
4. For **annuitized payments** (a periodic stream of payments) from a non-qualified contract,
   the IRS's General Rule spreads the cost basis proportionally across each payment — this is
   the **exclusion ratio**: part of each payment is a tax-free return of principal and part is
   taxable earnings, until the full cost basis has been recovered; after that, the full payment
   is taxable. [IRS Publication 575; IRS Publication 939 for the detailed computation]
5. A **single-sum death-benefit payout** from a variable annuity, paid because the owner or
   annuitant died, is taxable only to the extent it exceeds the unrecovered cost of the
   contract. A surviving joint annuitant who continues receiving payments figures the tax-free
   portion the same way the original annuitant did. [IRS Publication 575]
6. A distribution taken before age 59½ can trigger an **additional 10% tax** on top of ordinary
   income tax; the IRS states this applies to early distributions from a qualified retirement
   plan or a **deferred annuity contract**. Exceptions include disability or terminal illness,
   substantially equal periodic payments, and (for qualified plans) separation from service at
   age 55 or older. [IRS Topic 410; IRS Topic 558]
7. Annuity distributions are reported to the taxpayer on **Form 1099-R** ("Distributions From
   Pensions, Annuities, Retirement or Profit-Sharing Plans, IRAs, Insurance Contracts, etc.").
   [IRS — About Form 1099-R]
8. Whether more of a reader's Social Security benefit becomes taxable is a **separate**
   calculation (combined income vs. thresholds), covered on this site's own Social Security
   taxability guide — do not restate that guide's thresholds or formula here; link to it
   instead for readers stacking annuity income with Social Security.

## FAQ (verbatim real PAA — do not rephrase the questions)

- "How much of my annuity income is taxable?" — answer from facts 1–4 (depends on
  qualified/non-qualified status and payout method; no single percentage).
- "Do you have to report an annuity on a tax return?" — answer from fact 7 (yes, via Form
  1099-R, reported as taxable income to the extent covered by facts 1–4).
- "What type of annuity is not taxable?" — answer from fact 8's logic and facts 1–2: no annuity
  is entirely untaxed, but the after-tax "cost" portion of a non-qualified annuity is never
  taxed again (it is only the earnings, and any qualified annuity, that generate a tax bill);
  a Roth-funded annuity (if applicable) would follow Roth rules — only state this if it can be
  phrased without inventing a Roth-annuity fact not on the list; if uncertain, omit the Roth
  mention rather than guess.
