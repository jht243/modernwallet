Robo-Advisor vs Financial Advisor: Which One Fits You?

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated November 10, 2026

Pick a robo-advisor if you want automated investing for one defined goal and accept little human contact. The SEC says robo-advisers often seek to offer lower costs, but a flat monthly fee can cost more than a percentage fee on a small balance. Pick a human adviser if you need advice beyond investing or want a person to talk to. This comparison rests on Investor.gov, two SEC investor bulletins and a 2019 SEC chairman statement.

Both models can provide ongoing portfolio management, but their operational methods and service scopes differ. An automated digital investment advisory program collects your goals and risk tolerance through an online questionnaire, then builds and manages a portfolio. A human investment adviser provides ongoing investment advice and monitoring, and can deliver planning services alongside securities management.

Which should you choose?

A robo-advisor fits a reader who wants automated investing for a defined goal and accepts little human contact, and the cost depends on your balance. A human adviser fits a reader who needs advice beyond investing or wants a person to call.

If the human is a registered investment adviser, the duty of care and loyalty is the same as a robo-advisor's. If the person is a broker or dual registrant, a different standard may apply, shown in Form CRS.

This outcome changes if an automated platform provides a hybrid human adviser at your balance level, or if the human is broker-only. Read both firms' relationship summaries and Form ADV Part 2 disclosures to compare all-in costs and human access before opening an account.

Robo-Advisor or Financial Advisor: Which Fits Your Situation

Your choice between a robo-advisor and a traditional human advisor depends on the complexity of your financial profile and your preferences for personal support. If your goal is automated investing for a defined goal, an automated platform builds and manages a portfolio.

According to the SEC Investor Bulletin on Robo-Advisers published by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Office of Investor Education and Advocacy (OIEA), a robo-advisor is an automated digital investment advisory program. These platforms collect your financial goals, investment time horizon, household income, other assets, and risk tolerance through an online questionnaire. The software then uses that collected information to build and manage an investment portfolio for you. Robo-advisors often seek to offer lower costs and fees, and some require lower account minimums.

Human investment advisers operate differently during the initial evaluation process. As detailed by Investor.gov on Investment Advisers, an investment adviser is a firm or person that provides investment advice about securities for compensation. These professionals typically provide ongoing advice about buying, selling, or holding investments, monitor performance, and may give planning services. Unlike an automated program that relies on an online questionnaire, a traditional adviser can hold a conversation to gather your needs.

What Robo-Advisors and Financial Advisors Cost You

Comparing costs between automated platforms and personal advisers requires examining both fee structures and auxiliary account expenses. Most traditional investment advisers charge an asset-based fee calculated as a percentage of the total assets held in your account. As noted in the Investor.gov Relationship Summary (Form CRS) guidance, advisers are generally focused on your portfolio over time and typically paid an ongoing asset-based fee on total account value.

Robo-advisors use diverse pricing frameworks, ranging from asset-based percentage fees to recurring subscriptions. The SEC Investor Bulletin on Subscription-Based Advisory Fees notes that subscription pricing entails a set recurring fee, typically billed monthly. For smaller robo-adviser accounts, that fee might be $3, $5, or $10 per month for investment management. The SEC bulletin warns that on small balances a flat monthly fee can cost more than a traditional asset-based fee. A $3 monthly fee on a $500 account is $36 a year, more than 7 percent.

Advisory management charges do not encompass all investment expenses. Both human advisers and robo-advisers invest client money into underlying funds that carry internal operational expenses. The SEC bulletin on robo-advisers notes that many digital programs build portfolios using exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which deduct ongoing management expenses from fund performance. Similarly, clients may pay other fees such as brokerage, custody, and fund expenses. How Much Human Contact You Get with Each Option

The level of personal contact varies widely across advisory options and should dictate which path you select. When working with a traditional human financial advisor, direct personal dialogue serves as the foundation of the relationship. You talk directly with a person who gathers your needs and provides ongoing advice.

Robo-advisory platforms deliver varying levels of human communication. The SEC bulletin on robo-advisers explains that some digital programs offer access to an investment professional, often described as a hybrid or bionic model. Other automated platforms restrict human contact entirely to technical support staff. Furthermore, some automated firms condition access to human professionals on maintaining a minimum account size, leaving smaller account holders with purely digital interfaces.

Unlike traditional advisory relationships, digital programs typically lack an initial conversation with a person to assess your personal needs. The SEC bulletin points out that robo-adviser recommendations are limited by the information requested, and the burden to update your profile data falls squarely on you. If your information changes, the robo-advisor cannot incorporate those developments until you update your profile.

Duty of Care Depends on How Your Advisor Is Registered

The legal standard of conduct governing your investment professional depends entirely on their regulatory registration. In a July 2019 statement, then SEC Chairman Jay Clayton explained that the investment adviser fiduciary duty is comprised of a duty of care and a duty of loyalty. This fiduciary framework applies to registered investment advisers, requiring them to act in the best interest of their clients and avoid placing their own financial interests ahead of the client's interests.

Robo-advisors registered with the SEC operate as registered investment advisers, which means they owe clients an adviser fiduciary duty of care and loyalty. Similarly, human professionals who operate as registered investment advisers are held to this same fiduciary standard. However, many individual financial professionals do not operate solely as investment advisers. Some professionals are registered representatives of broker-dealers, while others are dual registrants licensed as both brokers and investment advisers. For broker-dealers, Regulation Best Interest establishes the governing standard of conduct, requiring brokers to act in the best interest of retail customers when making investment recommendations.

Determining which standard applies to your advisory relationship requires reviewing regulatory disclosure documents. According to the SEC Investor Bulletin on Form ADV, retail investors should review Form ADV Part 2 and the Form CRS relationship summary. Form ADV Part 2A serves as an adviser's primary narrative brochure, detailing advisory business practices, fee schedules, conflict points, and disciplinary history across 18 standardized items. Form CRS explicitly states whether a firm is an investment adviser, a broker-dealer, or a dual registrant. You can verify firm registrations and review public filings on the SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) database.

Who Each Option Is Not For and What Would Change the Answer

A robo-advisor may not fit you if you want a person to call or your finances include accounts its questionnaire does not ask about. The SEC bulletin says some robo-advisers offer hybrid access to an investment professional and some use tax loss harvesting, so ask each one. A human adviser may not fit a small balance with one simple goal. See is a financial advisor worth it for the break-even math.

Specific operational changes can flip this general advisory recommendation. If an automated robo-advisory platform offers a hybrid service tier with an investment professional at your balance, the robo-advisor may satisfy your need for personal communication. Conversely, if a human professional you interview is registered exclusively as a broker-dealer representative rather than an investment adviser, the regulatory baseline shifts. Checking Form CRS confirms whether your prospective advisor operates as an investment adviser or a broker. Frequently asked questions

Are robo-advisors better than financial advisors?

Robo-advisors are better for investors who want automated investment management for a defined goal and who do not require personal financial planning. The right option depends on your account size and the breadth of services you require.

Is a robo-advisor a good idea?

A robo-advisor is a good idea if you want an automated portfolio built with exchange-traded funds. The SEC says robo-advisers often seek to offer lower costs, though dollar fees can add up on a small balance. It may not fit if you want a dedicated person to call, but some robo-advisors offer hybrid professional access or tax loss harvesting, so check each service.

Robo-advisor vs financial planner: what is the difference?

A robo-advisor is an automated digital investment advisory program that builds a portfolio from your questionnaire answers. Financial planner is a job title, and Investor.gov lists a financial planner among the people you can hire to help with investment decisions, so ask what services and registration the person has.
