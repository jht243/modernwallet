Robo-Advisor or Financial Advisor: Which Fits Your Situation Your choice between a robo-advisor and a traditional human advisor depends on the complexity of your financial profile and your preferences for personal support. If your goal is automated investing for a defined goal, an automated platform builds and manages a portfolio. According to the SEC Investor Bulletin on Robo-Advisers published by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) Office of Investor Education and Advocacy (OIEA), a robo-advisor is an automated digital investment advisory program. These platforms collect your financial goals, investment time horizon, household income, other assets, and risk tolerance through an online questionnaire. The software then uses that collected information to build and manage an investment portfolio for you. Robo-advisors often seek to offer lower costs and fees, and some require lower account minimums. Human investment advisers operate differently during the initial evaluation process. As detailed by Investor.gov on Investment Advisers , an investment adviser is a firm or person that provides investment advice about securities for compensation. These professionals typically provide ongoing advice about buying, selling, or holding investments, monitor performance, and may give planning services. Unlike an automated program that relies on an online questionnaire, a traditional adviser can hold a conversation to gather your needs. What Robo-Advisors and Financial Advisors Cost You Comparing costs between automated platforms and personal advisers requires examining both fee structures and auxiliary account expenses. Most traditional investment advisers charge an asset-based fee calculated as a percentage of the total assets held in your account. As noted in the Investor.gov Relationship Summary (Form CRS) guidance, advisers are generally focused on your portfolio over time and typically paid an ongoing asset-based fee on total account value. Robo-advisors use diverse pricing frameworks, ranging from asset-based percentage fees to recurring subscriptions. The SEC Investor Bulletin on Subscription-Based Advisory Fees notes that subscription pricing entails a set recurring fee, typically billed monthly. For smaller robo-adviser accounts, that fee might be $3, $5, or $10 per month for investment management. The SEC bulletin warns that on small balances a flat monthly fee can cost more than a traditional asset-based fee. A $3 monthly fee on a $500 account is $36 a year, more than 7 percent. Advisory management charges do not encompass all investment expenses. Both human advisers and robo-advisers invest client money into underlying funds that carry internal operational expenses. The SEC bulletin on robo-advisers notes that many digital programs build portfolios using exchange-traded funds (ETFs), which deduct ongoing management expenses from fund performance. Similarly, clients may pay other fees such as brokerage, custody, and fund expenses. How Much Human Contact You Get with Each Option The level of personal contact varies widely across advisory options and should dictate which path you select. When working with a traditional human financial advisor, direct personal dialogue serves as the foundation of the relationship. You talk directly with a person who gathers your needs and provides ongoing advice. Robo-advisory platforms deliver varying levels of human communication. The SEC bulletin on robo-advisers explains that some digital programs offer access to an investment professional, often described as a hybrid or bionic model. Other automated platforms restrict human contact entirely to technical support staff. Furthermore, some automated firms condition access to human professionals on maintaining a minimum account size, leaving smaller account holders with purely digital interfaces. Unlike traditional advisory relationships, digital programs typically lack an initial conversation with a person to assess your personal needs. The SEC bulletin points out that robo-adviser recommendations are limited by the information requested, and the burden to update your profile data falls squarely on you. If your information changes, the robo-advisor cannot incorporate those developments until you update your profile. Duty of Care Depends on How Your Advisor Is Registered The legal standard of conduct governing your investment professional depends entirely on their regulatory registration. In a July 2019 statement , then SEC Chairman Jay Clayton explained that the investment adviser fiduciary duty is comprised of a duty of care and a duty of loyalty. This fiduciary framework applies to registered investment advisers, requiring them to act in the best interest of their clients and avoid placing their own financial interests ahead of the client's interests. Robo-advisors registered with the SEC operate as registered investment advisers, which means they owe clients an adviser fiduciary duty of care and loyalty. Similarly, human professionals who operate as registered investment advisers are held to this same fiduciary standard. However, many individual financial professionals do not operate solely as investment advisers. Some professionals are registered representatives of broker-dealers, while others are dual registrants licensed as both brokers and investment advisers. For broker-dealers, Regulation Best Interest establishes the governing standard of conduct, requiring brokers to act in the best interest of retail customers when making investment recommendations. Determining which standard applies to your advisory relationship requires reviewing regulatory disclosure documents. According to the SEC Investor Bulletin on Form ADV , retail investors should review Form ADV Part 2 and the Form CRS relationship summary. Form ADV Part 2A serves as an adviser's primary narrative brochure, detailing advisory business practices, fee schedules, conflict points, and disciplinary history across 18 standardized items. Form CRS explicitly states whether a firm is an investment adviser, a broker-dealer, or a dual registrant. You can verify firm registrations and review public filings on the SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) database.
