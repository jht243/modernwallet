In the guides we publish here, we start from the official public filings because they reveal fee structures, conflicts, and past regulatory actions before you sign an agreement. At ModernWallet, we suggest opening the Part 2A brochure before other sections because the regulatory agency designates it as the primary disclosure document for advisory practices. Learning how to read Form ADV gives you a direct look at how an advisory firm operates and how its professionals get paid. The document serves as the uniform registration filing for both federal and state authorities. Reviewing the brochure, the individual supplement, and the relationship summary prepares you to spot material disciplinary events and ask direct questions during your first meeting. Tools for this journey Investing → Retirement → Net worth → How to Find and Read Form ADV in the Order That Helps To start your search, open the Investor.gov Check Out Your Investment Professional tool, which directs you straight to the SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure database . This public portal lets you search by individual name or firm name. The regulatory agency does not mandate an official reading sequence for clients. Our suggested order is an editorial judgment designed to help you absorb the most practical information first: Search for the advisory firm on the SEC public portal to confirm its registration status. Open the Form ADV Part 2A narrative brochure to review services, fee schedules, conflicts, and disciplinary history. Open the Form ADV Part 2B brochure supplement to review the specific background of the individual who manages your portfolio. Read the Form ADV Part 3 relationship summary to compare service tiers, conduct standards, and basic fee models across firms. Check the Form ADV Part 1 filing for formal corporate ownership, affiliate connections, and recorded disciplinary items. Taking this sequence lets you focus on fees and conflicts before reviewing corporate census data. Anything unclear during your reading should become an immediate question for the professional. What Each Part of Form ADV Contains According to the SEC investor bulletin on Form ADV , the uniform registration filing divides into several distinct segments. Each part addresses different elements of an advisory firm's business practices and personnel. Part About What it contains Delivered to clients? Part 1 The advisory firm Check-the-box details on business operations, ownership structure, total clients, employee counts, business practices, corporate affiliations, and disciplinary events of the adviser or its employees No; public on IAPD portal Part 2A The advisory firm Plain-English narrative brochure covering 18 standardized items, including advisory services, asset amounts, fee schedules, conflicts, risk descriptions, and disciplinary details Yes; delivered to clients initially and offered annually Part 2B Individual professionals Brochure supplement covering six items on individual background, including education, five-year business history, disciplinary history, and outside business activities Yes; delivered for supervised individuals formulating advice or exercising discretion Part 3 The advisory firm Form CRS relationship summary covering services, fees, conflicts, required standard of conduct, disciplinary history, and key conversation starters Yes; delivered to retail investors As summarized in the Investor.gov glossary entry on Form ADV , Part 1 provides structured business data, while Part 2 functions as the narrative disclosure document explaining practical terms. What to Look For in the Part 2A Brochure The Part 2A brochure contains 18 items presented in a standardized order so that you can evaluate multiple firms side by side. The SEC investor bulletin identifies several critical areas that every investor should inspect closely. **Advisory business**: The firm describes the types of advisory services it provides and discloses the total amount of client assets it manages. Reviewing this section helps you understand if the firm handles portfolio management, financial planning, or specialized strategies, which connects to the details in our guide to what is a registered investment advisor . **Fees and compensation**: The brochure outlines fee schedules, explains whether rates are negotiable, and specifies whether payments are billed directly to you or deducted from your account. It also lists additional expenses you might incur, such as brokerage commissions, custody charges, and underlying fund fees. You can use these details to assess total costs before signing an advisory contract. **Performance-based fees and side-by-side management**: This item covers performance-based fees and side-by-side management. **Methods of analysis, investment strategies, and risk of loss**: The firm explains its research methods, core strategies, and the risks associated with its recommendations. **Disciplinary information**: The SEC bulletin explains that firms must disclose material legal or disciplinary events. Certain regulatory or legal actions within the last 10 years are presumed to be material. **Code of ethics, participation in client transactions, and personal trading**: This disclosure covers code of ethics, participation in client transactions, and personal trading, connecting to financial advisor conflicts of interest . **Brokerage practices**: The brochure covers the firm's brokerage practices. Every firm answers these 18 items in the exact same sequence. That fixed layout allows you to open two brochures from competing firms and compare identical sections without searching through different page structures. Reviewing Part 2B, Part 3, and Part 1 for Complete Background While Part 2A covers the entire firm, the Part 2B brochure supplement focuses on the individual professional handling your money. The SEC bulletin explains that an adviser must deliver this supplement for each supervised individual who formulates investment advice and maintains direct client contact, or who exercises discretionary trading authority over client assets. The Part 2B supplement covers six specific items. It outlines the individual's formal education, business background over the past five years, disciplinary information, and outside business activities. Part 3, also known as the relationship summary or Form CRS, provides a succinct comparison format. Per Investor.gov guidance on Form CRS , the relationship summary covers services offered, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, required standards of conduct, legal or disciplinary history, and key conversation starters. It serves as an accessible starting point before you dig into the comprehensive narrative of Part 2A. Part 1 remains available on the SEC IAPD portal for investors who want institutional data. It uses a check-the-box layout to record exact ownership structures, business practices, employee counts, corporate affiliations, and disciplinary events involving the firm or its staff. If a firm operates under several parent entities or affiliates, Part 1 lists those corporate connections. Check the Person and the Firm According to Investor.gov advice on investment advisers , investors should always check both the advisory firm and the individual representative before establishing a working relationship. Investment advisers generally must register with either the SEC or relevant state securities authorities. Advisers provide ongoing portfolio advice over time, monitor performance, and are required to act in your best interest without placing their interests ahead of yours. Their fees are typically based on the total value of assets held in your account, though some provide planning services under different fee arrangements. Brokers operate under a different business model. As outlined in the SEC guidance on Form CRS, broker-dealers generally execute discrete transactions and earn sales commissions or markups on each trade. While investment advisers file Form ADV, broker-dealers and individual brokers register through FINRA and file disclosures accessible on FINRA BrokerCheck . Many financial firms maintain dual registration, operating as both an investment adviser and a broker-dealer. In those environments, you must clarify when a professional is acting as an investment adviser and when they are acting as a broker. You can prepare for these discussions by reviewing our suggested questions to ask a financial advisor during your initial interview. Limits of Form ADV and When the Answer Changes Reading Form ADV will not help you evaluate professionals who are not registered investment advisers. Unregistered individuals and professionals who act purely as brokers do not submit Form ADV filings. For pure brokerage accounts, you must rely on FINRA BrokerCheck and Form CRS rather than narrative brochures. In our editorial judgment, a clean Form ADV filing does not show advice quality. Certain conditions will change how you read Form ADV or alter the conclusions you draw from the text: The firm files an annual summary of material changes with a revised brochure, altering fee percentages, service structures, or conflict disclosures. A newly recorded legal or disciplinary event appears in Part 1 or Part 2B, requiring you to reconsider whether to keep the relationship under the guidelines in our breakdown of when to switch financial advisors . You negotiate a customized contract with negotiated billing terms that supersede standard schedule numbers shown in Part 2A. The regulatory agency updates filing requirements or disclosure thresholds for registered entities. The SEC requires investment advisers to deliver an annual summary of material changes to existing clients along with a revised brochure or an offer to deliver one. If you notice unexpected updates in that annual summary, schedule a meeting to review those changes with your advisor. How to Read Form ADV and Take Practical Action When you open the Part 2A brochure, confirm that the fee calculation method matches the payment structure the professional described to you in person. Finally, review the Part 2B educational and employment background. Reading Form ADV before you sign an agreement gives you the facts needed to hire the right professional or walk away before committing your assets. Frequently asked questions Is Form ADV public? Yes, Form ADV is a public document. Form ADV filings for registered investment advisory firms and their representatives are public on the SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure database. How do I search Form ADV? You can search Form ADV by visiting the Investor.gov Check Out Your Investment Professional search tool, which directs you to the SEC Investment Adviser Public Disclosure portal. Enter the firm or individual advisor name to view their current filings. What is Form ADV Part 2A? Form ADV Part 2A is the narrative brochure that registered investment advisers must prepare. It contains 18 standardized items written in plain English, detailing services offered, fee schedules, conflicts of interest, investment strategies, and disciplinary information. What is Form ADV Part 3? Form ADV Part 3, also known as Form CRS, is the relationship summary document designed for retail investors. It provides concise information about the firm's services, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, standard of conduct, and disciplinary history. Is Form ADV annual? Advisers must deliver an annual summary of material changes to their clients along with a revised brochure or an offer to deliver the revised brochure. This annual disclosure ensures clients stay informed about updates to business practices, fee schedules, or disciplinary records.
