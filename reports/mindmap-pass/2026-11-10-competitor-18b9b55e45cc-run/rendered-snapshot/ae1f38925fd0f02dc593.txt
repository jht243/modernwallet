In the guides we publish here, we show the tradeoffs between managing money yourself and paying an ongoing professional fee. You can manage your own portfolio by making every trade yourself, or you can hire an adviser for ongoing oversight. At ModernWallet, we show the math, name what moves it, and explain the tradeoff. Choosing between investing on your own and hiring a professional depends on the services you require, all-in costs, and potential conflicts of interest. Per guidance from Investor.gov on investing on your own , the first step in self-directed investing is creating a clear financial plan. That plan establishes how much capital you invest, how long you plan to invest, your specific goals, and your risk tolerance. When you invest on your own, you accept sole responsibility for every investment decision. Investor.gov notes that self-directed investors execute trades through online investing platforms, direct investing programs, or dividend reinvestment plans. In contrast, hiring an investment professional delegates ongoing portfolio oversight to an external manager. Investor.gov on investment advisers notes that an investment adviser is a firm or person paid to give advice about securities. Advisers generally provide ongoing recommendations about buying, selling, or holding securities. Services That Explain What Investment Management Covers An investment manager provides ongoing portfolio oversight, performance reviews, and asset selection tailored to client goals. Under guidance published by Investor.gov on working with an investment professional , investment advisers are required to act in your best interest and cannot place their own interests ahead of yours. Advisers may also offer optional financial planning services alongside ongoing portfolio monitoring. In discretionary accounts, an adviser makes trades and delegated decisions. The scope of work provided by an adviser differs substantially from transaction-based brokerage services. As detailed on Investor.gov regarding the relationship summary (Form CRS) , brokers are generally transactional professionals paid commissions or markups per transaction. Investment advisers focus on managing your portfolio over time. Dual-registered firms operate under both models, so you should understand when they act as a broker and when as an adviser. What Assets Under Management Means for Account Fees According to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) investor bulletin on Form ADV , an adviser must disclose the amount of client assets managed in Form ADV Part 2A. Investor.gov states that an adviser's fee is typically based on the value of all of the assets held in your account with the adviser. The Form CRS disclosure guidance explains that an asset-based fee can cost more than transaction-based fees. However, an asset-based fee aligns with continuous portfolio monitoring and delegated trading authority. Form ADV Part 2A requires firms to disclose whether fees are negotiable, how billing occurs, and whether custodial or fund expenses apply. Decision Framework for Managing Investments or Hiring an Adviser Deciding between self-directed investing and hiring an investment adviser depends on required services, total costs, and potential conflicts. Sourced regulatory guidance highlights six core factors: required services, account limits, total costs, payment structure, potential conflicts, and legal or disciplinary history. Our judgment is that self-directed investing works best when you want complete control and have a clear financial plan. Steps to Check an Investment Adviser Before Hiring Before hiring an investment manager, examine their regulatory registration and public disciplinary record. The SEC Investor Bulletin confirms that Form ADV is the uniform registration document filed by investment advisers with the SEC or state securities authorities. Form ADV contains three separate sections that provide verifiable background details on advisory firms and their individual representatives. Part 1 uses a check-the-box format covering ownership, business practices, and disciplinary events involving the firm or its employees. Part 2A is a plain-English narrative brochure answering 18 mandatory items, including advisory services, fee schedules, methods of analysis, and material disciplinary disclosures occurring within the past ten years. Part 2B supplements detail the educational background, five-year business history, and disciplinary events of supervised individuals who formulate investment advice or direct client portfolios. Retail investors can review Part 3, known as Form CRS, to examine core services, fees, conflicts of interest, and required conduct standards. The SEC makes these brochures and relationship summaries accessible on the Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) database . You can also run background searches through the Investor.gov professional search tool . To learn more about navigating these filings, review our guide on how to read Form ADV and our overview of what is a registered investment advisor . Situations Where Hiring an Adviser Does Not Fit Hiring an investment manager is not necessary for every investor. Hiring an adviser does not fit if you want self-direction and accept responsibility for every decision. Investor.gov notes that ways to invest on your own include online investing, direct investing, and dividend reinvestment plans. An asset-based fee can represent a larger share of a small account balance. Your approach changes if you only need infrequent trade execution rather than ongoing portfolio supervision. A registered broker paid per transaction offers a different model that avoids ongoing asset-based management fees. Investor.gov notes that how financial professionals make money creates specific conflicts of interest, so you must always verify fee structures before entering into an advisory agreement. Our Takeaway on What Is Investment Management Our takeaway is to invest on your own if you can create a financial plan, manage risk tolerance, and accept complete responsibility for your investment decisions. Conversely, hire an investment adviser if you need ongoing portfolio monitoring, desire discretionary trade execution, and accept an asset-based fee calculated on your assets under management. This choice applies to investors who hold investable assets and must balance continuous oversight against ongoing advisory fees. This decision changes if your investable balance is small relative to total advisory expenses, or if your needs are met through single transactions executed by a broker. To determine your next step regarding what is investment management, write down your timeline and risk goals, review our guide on what a financial advisor does , and compare a prospective adviser's Form CRS against investing on your own. Frequently asked questions What does assets under management mean? The SEC bulletin lists the amount of client assets an adviser manages as a required Part 2A advisory-business disclosure. Investor.gov notes that ongoing advisory fees are typically based on the total value of all assets held in your account. What are assets under management fees? Assets under management fees are advisory charges typically based on the value of all assets held in your account with the adviser. The SEC Form ADV Part 2A requires firms to describe their fee schedules, explain whether fees are negotiable, and state how payments are deducted. Investor.gov notes that asset-based fees may cost more than transactional commissions but support continuous portfolio monitoring. How does assets under management work? An investment adviser monitors your investments over time and assesses a fee tied to the total account balance. Under Form ADV Part 2A guidelines, the adviser must specify its methods of analysis, investment strategies, and how other costs like custody and fund fees apply. Is investment management the same as wealth management? Investment management focuses specifically on buying, selling, monitoring, and adjusting securities within a portfolio.
