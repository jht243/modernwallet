Rule of 78 Calculator

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated November 23, 2026

The Rule of 78 is a method that allocates more of a precomputed loan's finance charge to earlier months using the sum of the digits. This schedule credits interest heavily toward the start of the loan, so paying off early refunds less unearned interest than the actuarial method. On a $15,000 loan at 7% APR over 48 months paid off after 12 payments, the lender keeps $28.65 more in interest under the Rule of 78. Federal law under 15 U.S.C. 1615 requires a refund at least as favorable as the actuarial method only for precomputed consumer credit transactions longer than 61 months that were made after September 30, 1993.

In our internal modeling, we compared the Rule of 78 with the actuarial method on the same loans to see what an early payoff costs. For a $15,000 loan at 7% over 48 months, paying the balance in full after 12 payments yields an unearned interest refund of $1,269.31 under the Rule of 78 compared with $1,297.96 under the actuarial method. That difference leaves $28.65 more with the lender. Enter your own loan in the calculator below, or read the month-by-month table further down, to check these figures.

How the Rule of 78 Splits Your Interest

A precomputed loan calculates the total finance charge at origination and adds it to the principal before spreading the total across equal monthly installments. The standard monthly installment is calculated using the principal, monthly rate, and number of payments. You can inspect equal monthly installments and standard amortization schedules with our loan payment calculator. The total finance charge equals the monthly payment multiplied by the number of months minus the principal amount borrowed. When you prepay a precomputed loan, the lender calculates how much of that total finance charge has been earned and how much must be refunded.

The Rule of 78 weights each payment's interest using the sum of the digits of the loan term. For a loan of n months, the denominator S equals n(n + 1) / 2. On a 12-month loan, S equals 12 + 11 + ... + 1, which totals 78. That calculation gives the rule its name. On a 48-month loan, S equals 1,176. In month j, the lender earns a fraction of the finance charge equal to (n - j + 1) / S. After k payments, the earned interest fraction is k(2n - k + 1) / (n(n + 1)). The refund of unearned interest is the total finance charge minus this earned interest amount.

Consider our baseline example of a $15,000 loan at 7% APR over 48 months with a total finance charge of $2,241.30. In month 1, the Rule of 78 assigns 48 / 1,176 of the total interest, which equals $91.48. Month 48 is credited just $1.91. Under the actuarial method, month 1 interest is $87.50, based on the initial balance multiplied by the monthly rate, while month 48 interest is $2.08. After 12 payments, the Rule of 78 has earned $971.99, or 43.37% of the finance charge, while the actuarial method has earned $943.34, or 42.09%.

The actuarial method applies each payment first to accumulated interest on the actual remaining balance and then subtracts the remainder from the unpaid principal. The calculator assumes a fixed-rate precomputed loan, level monthly payments, payoff immediately after a scheduled payment, and no additional fees. It does not account for specific contract terms, state-level regulations, or rebate-on-default rules. For terms exceeding 61 months, the calculator displays a notice stating that the result is a formula illustration because federal law restricts this practice on consumer loans.

Is the Rule of 78 Legal?

Federal law under 15 U.S.C. 1615(b) requires that any refund of interest on a precomputed consumer credit transaction with a term exceeding 61 months consummated after September 30, 1993, must be computed using a method at least as favorable as the actuarial method. This requirement means the Rule of 78 cannot be used to reduce an early payoff refund below the actuarial level on those long-term consumer loans. It is not a blanket ban across all credit transactions.

Under 15 U.S.C. 1615(a)(1), if a consumer prepays the financed amount in full under any consumer credit transaction, the creditor must promptly refund any unearned portion of the interest charge. This rule applies regardless of whether prepayment occurs through refinancing, consolidation, restructuring, or creditor acceleration. Under 1615(a)(2), no refund is required if the total refund amount is less than $1.

Federal statute 15 U.S.C. 1615(b) does not address terms of 61 months or less, leaving those shorter loans subject to state laws and individual contract provisions. We take no position on state laws governing credit terms. Under 15 U.S.C. 1615(c), a creditor must provide a payoff statement within 5 days of a consumer request detailing the amount needed to prepay in full, including any required refund. A consumer is entitled to one free payoff statement per year.

Who This Is Not For

This calculation does not apply to simple-interest loans. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), simple interest is far more common for auto loans than precomputed interest. On a simple-interest loan, interest accrues daily or monthly on the actual outstanding balance. Borrowers looking to calculate extra payment savings on a simple-interest auto loan should use our auto loan early payoff calculator instead.

This calculator also does not cover business credit or loans originated outside the United States. Subsection (b) of 15 U.S.C. 1615 covers precomputed consumer credit transactions, and business loans follow rules this page does not cover.

Borrowers who need a formal legal interpretation of an existing financing agreement cannot rely solely on a mathematical model. For a term of 61 months or less, the contract and state law decide. A written payoff statement from the lender shows the refund it will actually give, and 15 U.S.C. 1615(c) entitles you to one free statement each year.

What Would Change Our Answer

A loan contract based on simple interest or the actuarial method will invalidate this comparison. If an agreement does not use precomputed interest, interest is calculated solely on the outstanding principal balance rather than front-loaded via sum-of-the-digits arithmetic. In that case, interest is charged on the actual outstanding balance, so the Rule of 78 split does not apply.

A precomputed consumer loan with a term exceeding 61 months also alters the outcome under federal statute. Loans exceeding 61 months consummated after September 30, 1993, must provide a refund at least as favorable as the actuarial method under 15 U.S.C. 1615(b). Any lender gain shown on such a loan is only a formula illustration.

Specific state laws or contract terms can also dictate different rebate requirements. Finally, an official payoff statement from the lender showing a different calculated figure supersedes any estimate from this model.

Our Takeaway

Based on our internal modeling, the Rule of 78 accelerates interest charges into the early months of a precomputed loan, reducing the refund of unearned interest when you pay the balance off early. That cost difference ranges from a few dollars on small, short-term loans ($1.64 on a 24-month loan at 6%) to dozens of dollars on larger balances ($28.65 on our 48-month baseline and $88.13 on a 60-month loan at 8%), eventually tapering to $0.00 at the end of the term. Federal law requires an actuarial-or-better refund only for precomputed consumer loans longer than 61 months made after September 30, 1993. To see how your own financing is affected, run your loan terms through the rule of 78 calculator and compare the result against your lender's written payoff statement.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Rule of 78?

The Rule of 78 is a method used on precomputed loans to allocate interest charges across payment periods. It assigns larger shares of interest to early payments based on the sum of the digits of the loan term. This means paying off early refunds less unearned interest than the actuarial method.

What is the Rule of 78 formula?

The formula credits each month with a share of the finance charge equal to the number of months from that month to the end of the loan, divided by the sum of all the month numbers in the term. For an n-month loan, the sum is S = n(n + 1) / 2. Each month j is allocated a fraction of the total finance charge equal to (n - j + 1) / S.

Can you show a Rule of 78 example?

On a $15,000 loan at 7% over 48 months, the total finance charge is $2,241.30. In month 1, the Rule of 78 credits $91.48 of interest, compared with $87.50 under the actuarial method. After 12 payments, paying off the loan yields a $1,269.31 refund under the Rule of 78, leaving the lender with $28.65 more.

Is the Rule of 78 legal?

Federal law under 15 U.S.C. 1615(b) requires an actuarial-or-better refund on precomputed consumer credit transactions longer than 61 months consummated after September 30, 1993. The statute does not address terms of 61 months or less. Individual state laws and credit contracts determine whether it can be used for shorter loans.

Does the Rule of 78 apply to car loans?

It applies only if the auto financing agreement is written as a precomputed interest loan. The CFPB notes that precomputed interest is uncommon on auto loans, while simple interest is far more standard. Simple-interest auto loans calculate interest on the unpaid daily balance rather than using the Rule of 78.

How do I find out what my lender will refund if I pay off early?

You can request an official payoff statement directly from your loan servicer. Under 15 U.S.C. 1615(c), creditors must provide a statement showing the payoff balance and any unearned interest refund within 5 days of your request. Consumers are entitled to receive one such statement each year without charge.

How is the Rule of 78 different from simple interest?

Simple-interest loans charge interest strictly based on the outstanding principal balance as payments are made. Precomputed loans calculate the total finance charge upfront and allocate it across payments using a set formula. With the Rule of 78, more of each early payment goes toward interest, reducing your savings if you prepay.

Why does the Rule of 78 refund less when I pay off early?

The Rule of 78 front-loads interest by crediting larger portions of the total finance charge to earlier months. By the time you pay off the balance, the lender has already recognized more earned interest than under standard amortization. This leaves a smaller remaining unearned interest balance to refund.
