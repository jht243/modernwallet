## The calculator (describe only what it does)
Inputs: business miles from January 1 to June 30, 2026; business miles from July 1 to December 31, 2026; the employer's rate in cents per mile (left blank, the IRS rate for each half is used); optional business tolls and parking.
Outputs: reimbursement owed; the mileage portion; the same miles at the IRS rate; the amount paid above the IRS rate (which counts as taxable wages); the blended cents-per-mile rate. A note appears when the employer's rate is above the IRS rate (the excess is reported as wages) or below it (the employee receives less than the IRS-rate amount).
Method the page may state: mileage portion = miles in each half times the rate for that half; tolls and parking are added on top; the excess over the IRS rate is measured half by half because the IRS rate changed on July 1, 2026.
Assumptions the page must state: count business miles only, and the regular commute from home to the usual workplace is not business mileage; the employer's written policy decides what it pays; federal law does not require an employer to use the IRS rate, and some states set their own reimbursement rules (link the state guide); keep a mileage log.

## CLOSED FACT LIST — every number the page may state. Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never state a state's rate or law detail, a typical employer rate, or a rate for any year other than 2026 and 2025.
IRS facts:
- 2026 business standard mileage rate: 72.5 cents per mile from January 1 to June 30, 2026 (IRS news release IR-2025-128), and 76 cents per mile from July 1 to December 31, 2026 (IR-2026-29).
- 2025 business rate: 70 cents per mile.
- Under an accountable plan, a mileage allowance at or below the federal rate is not included in the employee's wages; the part of an allowance above the federal rate is included in wages (IRS Publication 463).
- Business parking fees and tolls are separate from the per-mile rate; parking at your regular workplace is a commuting expense (IRS Publication 463).
- Most employees cannot deduct unreimbursed mileage on their federal return (IRS Publication 463 limits the deduction to a few groups such as Armed Forces reservists and qualified performing artists).
Worked example (computed by the calculator):
- 1,200 business miles January to June and 1,000 miles July to December, 2,200 miles in total.
- At the IRS rates: 1,200 × 72.5 cents = $870 and 1,000 × 76 cents = $760, for a reimbursement of $1,630.
- That is a blended rate of about 74.09 cents a mile.
- If the employer pays 80 cents a mile for the same miles: $1,760, of which $130 is above the IRS rate and counts as wages.
- If the employer pays 60 cents a mile: $1,320, which is $310 less than the IRS-rate amount.
No other figures.
