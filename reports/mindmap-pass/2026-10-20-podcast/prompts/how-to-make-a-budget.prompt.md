## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit/name this page may state
Source for the method: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), "Budgeting: How to create a budget and stick with it" (archived blog post, June 5, 2019), read 2026-10-20.

- The CFPB frames budget-building as four steps: (1) "Where does my money come from?" record all income sources, including self-employment, multiple jobs, child support and government benefits (the CFPB offers an Income Tracker for this); (2) "Where does my money go?" log spending to see typical monthly outflows sorted into categories (the CFPB offers a Spending Tracker); if that feels overwhelming, review expenses one week at a time; (3) "What are all my bills and when are they due?" use a bill calendar to track due dates and spot tight weeks (the CFPB offers a Bill Calendar); late or missed payments can affect credit scores; (4) "Create your working budget": combine income, spending and bill timing into a realistic budget (the CFPB offers a Budget Worksheet).
- The CFPB advises updating the budget after a change in employment or spending habits. It gives no fixed review schedule, and the article contains no percentages or dollar amounts.
- The 50/30/20 rule (a method this site's own calculator uses): 50% of take-home pay to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings and debt repayment beyond minimums. Treat it as a starting split, not a rule that fits everyone; high-cost areas or heavy debt can make the needs share larger.
- A zero-based budget assigns every dollar of income to a job (spending, saving or debt) until income minus assignments equals zero.
- ILLUSTRATIVE arithmetic only (label it "for example", a hypothetical, not data): take-home pay of $4,000 a month under 50/30/20 is $2,000 needs, $1,200 wants, $800 savings and extra debt payments (computed: 4,000 x 0.50, 0.30, 0.20). A zero-based version of the same $4,000 might assign $2,150 to housing and bills, $600 to groceries and transport, $350 to minimum debt payments, $400 to wants, $500 to savings = $4,000 (all hypothetical; say the reader's figures will differ).
- Method points that need no numbers and may be stated as general guidance: use a month of real bank and card statements rather than memory; put fixed costs (rent, insurance, minimum payments) first and flexible costs (food, fun) after; save a set amount on payday before spending rather than saving what is left; build a small cash cushion before aggressive debt payoff (link the emergency fund guide for how much); expect to adjust categories for two or three months.
