In these formulas, P represents initial principal, r is the annual interest rate, n is compounding periods per year, and t is total investment years. In our internal modeling, we ran both formulas on a $10,000 initial balance at a 5% nominal rate over 10 years, confirming that monthly compounding produces a future value (FV) of $16,470.09. We verified our mathematical power outputs against a month-by-month calculation loop and checked the built-in function against the documented test case published by Microsoft Support , which confirms =FV(0.06/12,10,-200,-500,1) equals $2,581.40. Spreadsheets require cash outflows to be entered as negative numbers. If you omit the negative signs before regular contributions or initial principal, the formula produces a negative future balance. When regular deposits occur on a different timeline than compounding, such as monthly deposits alongside daily compounding, you must compute an effective periodic rate rather than using a simple rate division. The Compound Interest Formula Step by Step The standard compound interest formula evaluates exponential asset growth using the equation A = P*(1 + r/n)^(n*t). Each component represents a concrete variable in your savings plan: A is the final future balance after all interest accrues. P is the starting principal balance. r is the nominal annual interest rate expressed as a decimal. n is the number of times interest compounds per calendar year. t is the overall duration of the investment in years. Consider a starting balance of $10,000 invested at 5% nominal annual interest for 10 years with monthly compounding. In cell format, write =10000*(1+0.05/12)^(12*10). The term 0.05/12 divides the annual percentage rate (APR) into a monthly rate of approximately 0.004167, while 12*10 calculates the 120 total compounding periods. Evaluating this expression yields $16,470.09. Changing the compounding frequency alters the exponent and periodic divisor. Using the same $10,000 balance at 5% over 10 years, annual compounding (n=1) entered as =10000*(1+0.05/1)^(1*10) produces $16,288.95. Quarterly compounding (n=4) entered as =10000*(1+0.05/4)^(4*10) yields $16,436.19. Daily compounding (n=365) entered as =10000*(1+0.05/365)^(365*10) grows to $16,486.65. You can also evaluate a lump sum using the built-in future value syntax =FV(0.05/12,12*10,0,-10000), which returns the identical $16,470.09. Adding Monthly Contributions With FV When you add recurring savings to an existing investment, use the future value function documented on the Microsoft Support and Google Docs Editors Help pages. Both platforms implement the syntax FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type]). The arguments represent specific cash flow parameters: rate is the interest rate applied per single period. nper is the total count of compounding payment periods. pmt is the cash deposit added each period. pv is the present value or initial principal balance. type indicates payment timing, where 0 represents the end of the period and 1 represents the beginning. For a $10,000 starting balance with $200 deposited at the end of each month at 5% annual interest for 10 years, enter =FV(0.05/12,12*10,-200,-10000). This returns $47,526.55. Over 10 years, your cumulative cash contributions total $34,000, consisting of the $10,000 principal plus $24,000 in monthly additions. Total compound interest earned equals $13,526.55. If you initiate deposits at the beginning of each period instead, enter =FV(0.05/12,12*10,-200,-10000,1), which yields $47,655.95. If you begin with zero initial principal and contribute only regular monthly deposits, omit the principal entry: =FV(0.05/12,12*10,-200) yields $31,056.46. When deposits and compounding occur strictly once per year, enter =FV(5%,10,-2400,-10000), returning $46,475.89. One condition applies: the basic =FV(rate/n, n*years, -deposit, -principal) shortcut assumes the contribution schedule matches the compounding frequency. If you contribute $200 monthly while the financial institution compounds interest daily, standard rate division slightly understates growth. To resolve this mismatch, calculate the effective monthly rate using (1+0.05/365)^(365/12)-1, which equals about 0.0041750727. Entering =FV(0.0041750727,12*10,-200,-10000) produces about $47,559.87. That creates a difference of about $33 over 10 years compared to the simple monthly division output of $47,526.55. Common Spreadsheet Mistakes Formulas fail when users mismatch timing inputs or violate spreadsheet sign rules. Avoid these three common calculation errors: Entering annual interest rates against monthly periods. Passing an unadjusted annual rate such as 5% or 0.05 alongside 120 monthly periods in =FV(0.05, 120, -200, -10000) causes the software to apply a 5% return each individual month. This mistake inflates the projected balance to $4,880,767.80 instead of the true $47,526.55 balance. Always divide the annual rate by your annual periods. Omitting negative signs on outgoing deposits and principal. Microsoft Support documents the sign convention for these functions. Money deposited represents cash leaving your hand, which must be entered as a negative number. Writing =FV(0.05/12, 120, 200, 10000) yields -$47,526.55. Insert negative signs before payment and present value arguments to display positive savings balances. Ignoring compounding and contribution schedule mismatches. The standard FV formula assumes periodic contributions occur on the identical schedule as interest compounding. If you deposit funds monthly but earn interest compounded daily, using naive monthly division understates growth. Converting your nominal daily rate into an effective monthly rate eliminates this variance. Our Takeaway To model investment compounding accurately, use =FV(rate/n, n*years, -deposit, -principal) whenever your deposit frequency matches your compounding schedule, or =P*(1+r/n)^(n*t) for fixed lump-sum balances. This spreadsheet modeling approach is not suitable for individuals seeking instant figures without formula entry, nor does it accommodate variable-rate products. Calculate compound interest in Excel using =P*(1+r/n)^(n*t) for a lump sum or =FV(rate/n, n*years, -pmt, -pv) for recurring payments. For example, a $10,000 lump sum at 5% interest for 10 years compounded monthly is entered as =10000*(1+0.05/12)^(12*10), which outputs $16,470.09. Enter outgoing investments as negative numbers to ensure the function outputs a positive balance. Is compound interest calculated monthly or yearly? Compound interest can compound on any schedule specified by the financial institution, commonly daily, monthly, or yearly. On a $10,000 balance at 5% over 10 years, annual compounding produces $16,288.95 while monthly compounding produces $16,470.09. Does the same compound interest formula work in Google Sheets? Yes, Google Sheets uses the same syntax and argument order as Excel for calculating compound interest. Both applications accept the standard mathematical power equation =P*(1+r/n)^(n*t) as well as the built-in financial function =FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type]). How do you calculate quarterly or half-yearly compounding? Calculate quarterly compounding by setting the periodic divisor n to 4, and half-yearly compounding by setting n to 2. For a $10,000 investment at 5% for 10 years compounded quarterly, the formula =10000*(1+0.05/4)^(4*10) produces $16,436.19. To model semi-annual compounding under the same parameters, replace the divisor and multiplier with 2 in the formula =P*(1+r/2)^(2*t). How do you include monthly deposits in the compound interest formula? Include monthly deposits by using the future value function =FV(rate/12, 12*years, -monthly_deposit, -initial_principal). For a $10,000 starting balance with $200 added at the end of each month at 5% interest over 10 years, enter =FV(0.05/12, 12*10, -200, -10000). This returns $47,526.55, reflecting $34,000 in total out-of-pocket deposits and $13,526.55 in earned compound interest. What interest rate should you enter in spreadsheet formulas? Enter the periodic interest rate, which is the nominal annual rate divided by the number of compounding periods per year. If your annual rate is 5% and interest compounds monthly, enter 0.05/12 or 5%/12 as the rate argument. Entering the full annual rate of 0.05 alongside 120 monthly periods erroneously calculates 5% growth per month, severely overstating results.
