## What Happens If You Stop Contributing

Stopping contributions at age 45 leaves $1,147,049 at age 65 out of a projected $1,540,606, because the money already invested keeps compounding for another twenty years. Freezing new deposits does not halt investment growth on your existing balance. The earlier you pause deposits, the more compound growth you forfeit over time.

In our internal modeling, we evaluated an investor starting at age 30 with $20,000 saved, adding $800 monthly at a 7 percent annual return until retirement at 65. Using the calculator accumulation formula, that baseline schedule produces a nominal nest egg of $1,540,606 from $336,000 in total employee and employer deposits. If that saver never contributes another dollar past the starting $20,000, the account still compounds to $213,532 at age 65, which equals $75,886 in today's dollars.

Contributing for twelve years until age 42 is enough to cross the seven-figure threshold, yielding $1,027,619 at age 65. Stopping deposits at age 45 forfeits 25.5 percent of the potential nest egg because those skipped contributions miss twenty years of investment compounding. By contrast, at a 0 percent return, stopping at 45 leaves $164,000 compared to $356,000 at age 65, meaning the loss equals the skipped deposits without compounding losses.

Our calculator defaults to a 3 percent annual inflation rate, translating the full $1,540,606 projection into $547,506 in today's purchasing power. A lower inflation rate of 2 percent raises that real balance to $770,345, while higher inflation of 4 percent cuts purchasing power to $390,413. You can compare how various price trends erode future capital over long horizons by testing inputs in our [inflation calculator](/inflation-calculator/).

Losing an employer match cuts your ending balance substantially even if your personal contribution rate remains unchanged. In our hypothetical breakdown of the $800 monthly deposit, we assumed $500 comes from employee salary deferrals and $300 comes from company matching funds. Without that assumed $300 match, the final nest egg drops to $1,042,953 at age 65, leaving $370,648 in today's dollars and creating a $497,653 shortfall.

Our takeaway is that continuing to save matters most during your thirties and forties, while stopping contributions after age 50 still preserves 84.3 percent of your baseline wealth. This modeling assumes a constant 7 percent return compounded annually, so outcomes will differ under volatile market cycles, variable inflation rates, or different employer match structures.

This projection does not serve savers who have already retired or individuals who need a structured portfolio decumulation strategy.
