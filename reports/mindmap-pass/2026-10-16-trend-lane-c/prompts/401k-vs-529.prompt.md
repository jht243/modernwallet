answer: A 401(k) and a 529 plan do different jobs: the 401(k) is for your own retirement and the 529 is for a student's education costs, so they are not interchangeable and money in one cannot simply be moved to the other. Which gets the next dollar depends on which goal is closer, how the tax treatment fits and what access you will need, and a 529 has its own limits on what a withdrawal can pay for.
answer placement: section 1 "What Each Account Is For"

## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

401(k):
- For 2026 the annual limit on employee elective deferrals to a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457 plan or the federal Thrift Savings Plan is $24,500, up from $23,500 for 2025 (IRS news release on 2026 limits; IRS "401(k) and profit-sharing plan contribution limits").
- For 2026, participants aged 50 and over in most of these plans can make a catch-up contribution of $8,000 if the plan permits it, so they can generally contribute up to $32,500; employees aged 60, 61, 62 and 63 have a higher catch-up limit of $11,250 for 2026 (IRS).
- Your plan's terms may impose a lower limit than the IRS limit on elective deferrals (IRS).
- Generally, early distributions are those received from a qualified retirement plan such as a 401(k) before age 59½. A 10% additional tax applies to the portion of an early distribution that is includible in gross income, unless an exception applies. Exceptions for distributions from a qualified plan other than an IRA include a series of substantially equal periodic payments over life expectancy, total and permanent disability, terminal illness, payment to a beneficiary or estate after death, and distributions after separating from service at or after age 55 (IRS Topic 558).

- 401(k) tax treatment: a 401(k) is a plan that allows employees to contribute a portion of their wages to individual accounts. Elective salary deferrals are excluded from the employee's taxable income (except designated Roth deferrals). Employers can contribute to employees' accounts. Distributions, including earnings, are includible in taxable income at retirement (except qualified distributions of designated Roth accounts) (IRS "401(k) plans" page). Elective deferrals are generally made on a pretax basis, are not subject to income tax withholding at the time of deferral and are not reported as wages in box 1 of Form W-2, but they are still wages subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes; some plans also let you make deferrals after tax as designated Roth contributions (IRS Topic 424). The Internal Revenue Code limits what an employee may elect to defer, and the plan terms may limit it further (IRS Topic 424).
- Computed from the IRS figures above (state it as the sum): for 2026 an employee aged 60, 61, 62 or 63 can generally contribute $24,500 plus the $11,250 higher catch-up, which is $35,750, if the plan permits catch-up contributions.

529 plan (qualified tuition program):
- A 529 plan, legally a qualified tuition program, is established and maintained by a state or state agency or instrumentality (and eligible educational institutions can run prepaid-tuition versions). It lets a contributor prepay a beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses or contribute to an account for paying those expenses (IRS Topic 313). There are two types: prepaid tuition plans and education savings plans, and all 50 states and the District of Columbia sponsor at least one type (SEC Investor.gov). The SEC does not regulate or oversee 529 plans (SEC Investor.gov).
- Qualified higher education expenses generally include expenses required for enrollment or attendance at a college, university, vocational school or other postsecondary school eligible for federal student aid programs (IRS Topic 313).
- For elementary or secondary school (kindergarten through grade 12) enrollment, qualified expenses include tuition, curriculum and curricular materials, books and other instructional materials, outside tutoring and classes, certain test fees, dual enrollment fees and educational therapies for students with disabilities, limited to $20,000 per year from all of the beneficiary's 529 plans ($10,000 before Dec. 31, 2025) (IRS Topic 313).
- Qualified expenses also include fees, books, supplies and equipment for a registered apprenticeship program, qualified education loan repayments in limited amounts, and certain postsecondary credentialing expenses (IRS Topic 313). Amounts can be withdrawn to pay principal or interest on the designated beneficiary's or their sibling's student loan, limited to $10,000 lifetime per individual (IRS Topic 313).
- Contributions to a 529 plan are not deductible on your federal return (IRS Topic 313). (Some states offer a state tax benefit; this list has no state details, so say it varies by state and the reader should check their state plan.)
- Earnings accumulate tax free while in the account. The beneficiary does not generally have to include earnings in income, and distributions are not taxable when used for qualified higher education expenses (IRS Topic 313).
- If a distribution is greater than the beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses, a portion of the earnings is taxable (IRS Topic 313).
- 529 contributions on behalf of a beneficiary cannot be more than the amount needed to provide for the beneficiary's qualified education expenses. Contact the program's trustee or administrator for the program's contribution limit (IRS Topic 313). There are no income restrictions on individual contributors (IRS Publication 970).
- Roth IRA rollover: for distributions made after Dec. 31, 2023, the beneficiary of a 529 plan may roll money into their own Roth IRA. For the rollover to be tax free it must be a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, is subject to the Roth IRA annual contribution limit and a $35,000 lifetime limit, must come from a 529 account open at least 15 years, and may not exceed the amount contributed to the 529 (and its earnings) before the 5-year period ending on the date of the distribution (IRS Topic 313).
- A 529 distribution is reported on Form 1099-Q, which splits the gross distribution into earnings and basis (return of investment) (IRS Topic 313).
- A 401(k) is not on the list of things a 529 can be funded from by a transfer: nothing in the sources says money can move directly from a 401(k) to a 529. Do not state or imply that a 401(k) can be rolled into a 529. Say only that the sources describe no such transfer, that pulling money from a 401(k) before 59½ can trigger the 10% additional tax plus income tax on the taxable portion (Topic 558), and that the reader should confirm their plan's rules.
