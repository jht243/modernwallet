Learning how to make a budget in this order means you pick a method like 50/30/20 or zero-based after you know what comes in and what goes out. The four steps come from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) , which offers a tool for each one. You can start the first step today with your latest pay stub and a notepad. Tools for this journey Monthly Budget Calculator → 50/30/20 Budget Calculator → Zero-Based Budget Calculator → The Four Steps to Build a Budget from Scratch A budget gets built in four steps, and the first three steps each answer a question the CFPB asks: Where does my money come from? Write down every source of income, including pay from each job, self-employment income, child support and government benefits. The CFPB offers an Income Tracker for this step. Where does my money go? Log your spending for a month and sort it into categories, so you can see what a typical month costs you. The CFPB offers a Spending Tracker for this step. What are all my bills and when are they due? Put every bill on a calendar by its due date. The CFPB offers a Bill Calendar to track due dates and spot tight weeks. Create your working budget. Combine your income, your spending and your bill timing into one realistic plan. Start step 1 today. Pull your latest pay stub and any benefit or support statements, then write down each amount and the date it arrives. Step 2 needs a full month of records. Do them in order anyway, since step 4 only works when it is built from the numbers you collected in steps 1 to 3. Where the Numbers Come from When You Make a Budget The spending numbers in your budget should come from a month of real bank and card statements rather than from memory. Download the last full month from every checking account and credit card you use. Copy each charge into a category such as housing, groceries, transport, debt payments or fun. If a full month of statements feels overwhelming, the CFPB suggests reviewing your expenses one week at a time. Four weekly sessions cover the same month of spending in smaller pieces. Pick one evening a week and finish one week of charges in that sitting. For income, write down take-home pay, the amount that lands in your account after deductions. The 50/30/20 split covered below is a share of take-home pay, so using a larger pre-deduction figure would make every share too big. If your pay changes from month to month, step 1 needs a different approach, and our guide on how to budget with irregular income walks through it. If a recurring charge is one you want to stop, use the subscription cancellation planner to identify the biller and keep a dated record. Choosing Between 50/30/20 and Zero-Based Budgets A 50/30/20 budget splits take-home pay into three fixed shares, while a zero-based budget assigns every dollar of income a job until nothing is left unassigned. Both methods need the numbers from the four steps above. Pick one after step 2, once you know what a real month costs. Under 50/30/20, 50% of take-home pay goes to needs, 30% to wants and 20% to savings and debt payments beyond the minimums. For example, a hypothetical take-home pay of $4,000 a month splits into $2,000 for needs, $1,200 for wants and $800 for savings and extra debt payments ($4,000 x 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20). You can run your own figure through the 50/30/20 Budget Calculator . A zero-based budget gives the same hypothetical $4,000 a list of jobs instead of three shares. One version might look like this: $2,150 for housing and bills $600 for groceries and transport $350 for minimum debt payments $400 for wants $500 for savings Those five amounts add up to $4,000, so income minus assignments equals zero. Your own figures will differ, and the Zero-Based Budget Calculator does the subtraction for you. The two examples show how needs can pass 50% of take-home pay. If you count groceries, transport and minimum payments as needs, the zero-based household spends $3,100 on needs ($2,150 + $600 + $350). That is 77.5% of $4,000, or $1,100 more than the $2,000 needs share under 50/30/20. High housing costs or heavy debt can push your needs past 50% the same way. Treat 50/30/20 as a starting split that will not fit every household. Total your needs from step 2 and divide by your take-home pay. If the result is 50% or less, start with 50/30/20, because three shares are fewer numbers to track. If the result is above 50%, skip 50/30/20 and use a zero-based budget. Our answer would flip only if your needs later drop under half your pay, at which point the three-share split becomes the easier one to run. If your debts already exceed what your income can cover, a nonprofit credit counselor or a fee-only financial planner can review your specific situation before you pick either method. Fixed Costs, Flexible Costs and Pay-Yourself-First Timing Fixed costs go into your working budget first, and flexible costs fill in the space that remains. Fixed costs are bills like rent, insurance and minimum debt payments. Flexible costs are spending like food and fun, where the amount moves with your choices. Write the fixed costs down at their full amounts before you assign a single dollar to flexible categories. Whatever remains after fixed costs is the real limit for groceries, eating out and entertainment combined. If that remainder looks too small, try the Household Budget Calculator . Save a set amount on payday, before you spend, instead of saving whatever is left at the end of the month. Set up the transfer for the day your pay arrives, so the savings line in your budget happens first. Build a small cash cushion before you start paying debt down aggressively. Our guide on how much emergency fund you need covers how large that cushion should be. Once the cushion exists, debt snowball vs avalanche compares two ways to order your extra debt payments. A Bill Calendar for Tight Weeks A bill calendar lists every bill by due date, so you can spot the weeks when more money goes out than comes in. This is step 3 of the CFPB method, and it fixes a gap a monthly total hides. A budget can balance for the month and still come up short in the week before payday. Mark each payday on the same calendar as your bills. If two large bills land in the days before your next paycheck, set aside part of the earlier paycheck when it arrives. That way the money for those bills is sitting in your account on the due date. The CFPB notes that late or missed payments can affect your credit score. A calendar with every due date written down shows which weeks are tight. Add a reminder ahead of each due date so you can move money if the balance looks low. Why Budgets Fail in the First Three Months New budgets usually need two or three months of adjustment before the categories match how you spend. Expect that from the start, and treat a missed category as information for next month's numbers. Three problems can show up in that stretch, and each one has a fix: Categories set too tight. If groceries run over every week, raise that line and lower a looser category by the same amount, so the total still matches your income. Forgotten irregular costs. When a forgotten cost shows up, add it as its own category and set aside a share of it each month. No review. Pick a regular day to compare what you spent against each category. The CFPB advises updating your budget after a change in employment or in your spending habits. It gives no fixed review schedule, so treat a job change or a change in how you spend as your signal to revisit the four steps. If higher prices keep breaking your categories, our guide on how to budget when prices keep rising covers adjusting for that. If every month ends with nothing left no matter how you adjust, compare your income with your fixed costs. Our guide on how to stop living paycheck to paycheck works through that situation step by step. Frequently asked questions How do I make a budget if I have never had one? Follow the four CFPB steps in order: list your income, track a month of spending, put your bills on a calendar by due date, then build a working budget from those numbers. Start with your latest pay stub today, and use a month of bank and card statements for the spending step. What is the easiest budgeting method for beginners? The 50/30/20 split is the simpler method if your needs take half your take-home pay or less, because it has only three shares to track. If your needs take more than half, a zero-based budget fits better. Try the 50/30/20 Budget Calculator with your own numbers. How much of my income should go to savings in a budget? The 50/30/20 method puts 20% of take-home pay toward savings and debt payments beyond the minimums. Treat that as a starting split, since high housing costs or heavy debt can leave less room. A fee-only financial planner can tailor the savings share to your situation. How often should I update my budget? Update your budget whenever your employment or spending habits change, which is the trigger the CFPB names. The CFPB gives no fixed review schedule. In the first two or three months, expect to adjust categories as your real spending shows up. Should I budget by hand, in a spreadsheet or in an app? Use the format you will open every week. Any format works once it holds your income, a month of spending and your bill due dates. Sources We prioritize primary sources for rules, formulas, rates, limits, and definitions.
