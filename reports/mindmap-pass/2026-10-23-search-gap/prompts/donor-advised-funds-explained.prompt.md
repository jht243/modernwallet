Source: IRS Publication 526 (https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526)
- A donor-advised fund is a fund or account in which a donor can, because of being a donor, advise the fund how to distribute or invest amounts held in the fund (Internal Revenue Code section 170(f)(18)).
- You can't deduct a contribution to a donor-advised fund if the sponsoring organization is a war veterans' organization, a fraternal society, or a nonprofit cemetery company, or if you don't have a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the sponsoring organization that it has exclusive legal control over the assets contributed.
- Charitable contribution limit for cash given to a public charity: generally 60% of adjusted gross income (AGI).
- A 30% of AGI limit applies to noncash contributions of capital gain property to a 50%-limit organization if you deduct fair market value without reducing for appreciation.
- Contributions over the limit can be carried over to later years (Publication 526 shows a worked carryover of $1,000 into the next year).

Source: Fidelity Charitable Giving Account page (https://www.fidelitycharitable.org/giving-account.html), as published at time of writing
- Its donor-advised fund is called the Giving Account. The contribution is irrevocable.
- No minimum initial contribution and no minimum balance.
- You can donate cash, stocks, bonds, and non-publicly traded assets such as private business interests.
- Each grant must be at least $50, and you must recommend at least one grant every two years; otherwise Fidelity Charitable makes a grant from the account.
- Administrative fee: the greater of $100 or 0.6% of the balance, with the percentage lower as the balance grows. Investment fees range from 0.015% to 0.93% depending on how the money is invested.
- You can name a successor to carry on giving from the account.

Source: Vanguard Charitable (https://www.vanguardcharitable.org/start-giving/open-an-account)
- $25,000 minimum initial contribution and $5,000 for each additional gift.

Source: DAFgiving360 (https://www.dafgiving360.org/features/fees-and-minimums) formerly known as Schwab Charitable
- No minimum for its core account.

Source: site pages already published (verified in earlier runs; cite the IRS pages above, not these)
- The 5% minimum distribution rule applies to private foundations, not to donor-advised funds, so money can sit in a DAF without a federal payout requirement. A private foundation pays a 1.39% excise tax on net investment income (https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations).
- The sponsor's board has final say over every grant; donors have advisory privileges, not legal control. Most sponsors will grant out an account that sits inactive for a few years. Donors can request anonymity on each grant.
- A DAF can be opened the same day through some sponsors.
- The 2026 standard deduction for married filing jointly is $32,200. Most filers take the standard deduction, so a charitable gift only helps if itemized deductions exceed it. Bunching means combining several years of planned giving into one tax year, and a DAF lets you take the full deduction the year you contribute while granting to charities over later years.
- Most inherited stocks, real estate and other property get a step-up in basis to fair market value on the date of death (IRS Publication 551, https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551), so if you sell right away you may owe little or no capital gains tax.
- Donating appreciated shares directly to a DAF or charity avoids capital gains tax on the gain and still earns a charitable deduction, versus selling first and donating the cash.
- The term used by the IRS is donor-advised fund. "Donor-assisted fund" is a common misspelling of the same term, not a separate IRS product.
