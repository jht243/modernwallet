Annual Cash Back by Spending Pattern Chase Freedom Flex vs Unlimited breaks even when activated 5% category purchases equal 12.5% of comparable annual spending. For this calculation, comparable annual spending means purchases outside Chase Travel, dining and drugstores. Both cards list the same 5% travel and 3% dining and drugstore rates, so those purchases contribute the same cash back on either card. If you have $24,000 in comparable purchases plus dining purchases, use $24,000 as the spending figure in the break-even rule. Our internal cost modeling assumes that you activate the Freedom Flex category every quarter and that purchases you count in it match that quarter's category. That is the best case for Freedom Flex. We leave out the new cardmember bonus, introductory APR and balance transfer fees. We also assume the modeled 5% purchases do not overlap with the shared travel, dining or drugstore categories. The calculation compares cash back from the published earn rates, with both annual fees at $0. How the 12.5% Rule Works Let S be comparable annual spending and B be eligible 5% category spending in each quarter, capped at $1,500 per quarter. Under the model's equal-quarter assumption, annual Freedom Flex cash back minus Freedom Unlimited cash back is 0.16 × B − 0.005 × S. Freedom Flex wins only when that result is greater than zero. Set the result to zero and the tie occurs when four quarters of eligible spending, or 4 × B, equal 12.5% of S. The formula accounts for both rates on category purchases. Freedom Flex earns 5% on eligible, activated purchases, while Freedom Unlimited earns 1.5% on those same purchases. On the remaining comparable purchases, Freedom Flex earns 1% and Freedom Unlimited earns 1.5%. More spending in a qualifying quarterly category helps Freedom Flex, but more spending at the ordinary rate helps Freedom Unlimited. The $1,500 quarterly cap limits how far Freedom Flex can pull ahead. Four quarters at the cap put $6,000 a year into the modeled 5% category. That reaches 12.5% of comparable spending at $48,000, producing a tie. Above $48,000, the full $6,000 is less than 12.5% of S, so Freedom Flex cannot win under these assumptions. The $48,000 boundary applies to comparable spending as defined above, rather than to a total that includes Chase Travel, dining or drugstores.
