A 401(k) is an employer plan for contributing wages to a retirement account, while an annuity is an insurance contract that can provide income payments. At The Modern Wallet, we base this comparison on the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) rules for 401(k) plans and Investor.gov guidance on annuities. The IRS says a 401(k) may include an employer match if its plan permits one. Investor.gov says an annuity can provide income, but its costs and withdrawal terms depend on the contract. Annuity and 401(k) Compared Side by Side Annuity 401(k) Plan What it is An insurance contract bought with one payment or a series of payments. A feature of a qualified employer plan that lets employees contribute wages to individual accounts. Who provides it An insurance company agrees to make the contract's income payments. An employer provides the plan. Employees contribute wages, and employers can contribute. Tax treatment Growth can be tax-deferred until income payments begin. A withdrawal may trigger taxes. Traditional salary deferrals are excluded from taxable income, and distributions are generally taxable. Designated Roth contributions and qualified distributions follow different rules. 2026 contribution limit The cited Investor.gov annuity guidance states no contribution limit. The employee elective-deferral limit is $24,500 or 100% of compensation, whichever is less. Employer match The annuity guidance describes payments made to buy a contract and does not describe an employer match. An employer can match contributions if the plan permits it. A match may be discretionary. Early-distribution tax A taxable distribution from a deferred annuity contract before age 59½ generally faces a 10% additional tax, subject to exceptions. A taxable early distribution from a 401(k) generally faces a 10% additional tax before age 59½, subject to exceptions. Withdrawal costs and fees Surrender charges can apply during a contract's surrender period. Fees may be explicit or reflected in credited interest and limits on gains. The cited IRS plan guidance gives no 401(k) plan-fee figure for comparison. Risk Risk varies by contract type. Registered index-linked and variable annuities can lose money. Payments depend on the insurer's claims-paying ability. The cited IRS pages describe individual accounts but give no investment-risk measure for a particular plan. Income payments The contract can provide payments for a set period or for life, depending on its terms. The cited IRS plan guidance does not describe a guaranteed income payment from the 401(k) itself. 📣 Building in finance? Put your product in front of readers comparing options on this page. Advertise With Us → Our Takeaway on Annuity vs 401(k) If an employer's 401(k) offers a match, start by examining that match. The IRS says a match depends on the plan document and may be discretionary, so it is a conditional reason to contribute rather than a rule for every plan. An annuity addresses a separate need: income payments under an insurance contract. Investor.gov says annuities suit long investment horizons and can carry surrender charges and fees. This takeaway is based on the IRS and Investor.gov guidance. It rests on no product testing. It does not fit someone seeking a monthly annuity payout figure, because the cited sources provide no figure that applies across contracts. The answer also changes if you have no employer plan, your plan offers no match, or you are near retirement and need income payments. Read the IRS contribution rules to understand how a match can work, then use Investor.gov's annuity questions to assess any contract you are offered. What an Annuity Costs and What Locks Up Your Money An annuity's surrender terms and fees can make access to its value costly. Investor.gov says a surrender charge may apply when you withdraw money within a specified number of years. The charge generally declines over time, so the year in which you withdraw matters when you read a contract. Some contracts also have adjustments to the amount paid when you withdraw. Investor.gov identifies market value adjustments as one example and says they are often negative. If you may need money during the surrender period, ask for the contract's surrender schedule and an explanation of any adjustment before deciding whether its income terms fit your goal. Annuity costs do not all appear as a separate annual bill. Investor.gov says many fixed annuities, fixed indexed annuities and registered index-linked annuities (RILAs) have no explicit ongoing fee. Their costs can instead appear in the interest credited or limits on gains. Compare those contract terms alongside any listed fee rather than treating a zero explicit fee as a complete cost figure. Other annuities can charge a base contract fee, fees for underlying funds or fees for optional benefits. Investor.gov gives an example of a 1.25% base contract fee on a $300,000 contract, which amounts to $3,750 a year. That is an illustration from its guidance. It is not a quoted fee for every annuity. Ask which charges apply to the specific contract and which benefits each charge buys. The type of annuity also changes the risk you accept. A fixed annuity guarantees a minimum interest rate, while a fixed indexed annuity bases interest partly on a benchmark and credits no less than zero interest under the description in Investor.gov's guidance. A RILA can lose money and places limits on gains and losses. A variable annuity directs money to mutual funds and has no stated limit on gains or losses in that guidance. Even a contract that promises payments depends on the insurance company's financial strength and claims-paying ability. Investor.gov says an insurer in financial difficulty may be unable to pay. Ask how the promised income works and review the insurer's claims-paying ability. After receiving an annuity contract, state law generally provides a free-look period, usually 10 to 30 days, during which you can change your mind. Taxes and Early Withdrawals The tax comparison depends on which 401(k) contribution you make and how you take money from an annuity. Under the IRS 401(k) plan rules , traditional elective salary deferrals are excluded from taxable income when contributed. Distributions, including earnings, are generally included in taxable income at retirement. Designated Roth deferrals are included in income when contributed, while qualified distributions from designated Roth accounts are excluded. For 2026, the IRS contribution rules set the employee elective-deferral limit at $24,500 or 100% of compensation, whichever is less. Catch-up contributions may be allowed if you are at least 50 and the plan permits them. For more detail on the employee limit, see our 2026 401(k) contribution limits . The cited annuity guidance states no comparable contribution limit for an annuity contract. Investor.gov says a deferred annuity can grow tax-deferred during its accumulation phase, before income payments begin. An immediate annuity is bought with one payment and typically starts paying income within a year. Those descriptions tell you when a contract is designed to pay, but they do not establish the tax amount on a particular withdrawal. Investor.gov advises consulting a tax adviser because annuity tax rules are complicated and can change. IRS Topic 558 supplies the early-distribution rule for both sides of this comparison. It generally treats distributions received before age 59½ from a qualified retirement plan, such as a 401(k), or a deferred annuity contract as early distributions. The additional tax is 10% of the portion included in gross income. A distribution that is not taxable, such as a rollover to another qualified plan, is not subject to that additional tax. Exceptions can change the result. Topic 558 lists exceptions for certain distributions from qualified plans other than individual retirement accounts (IRAs), including total and permanent disability and distributions after separation from service at or after age 55. Its rules for that age-55 exception should not be assumed to apply to every annuity withdrawal. The IRS addresses IRA early distributions separately in Topic 557, so do not use the qualified-plan exception list as an IRA checklist. Buying an Annuity Inside a 401(k) or IRA Investor.gov urges caution when an annuity is proposed inside a traditional 401(k) or traditional IRA. An annuity is a contract, while a 401(k) is an employer plan. The distinction matters because the question is then whether the contract adds a benefit you want within an account that is already tax-deferred. Investor.gov says an annuity bought inside a tax-deferred retirement plan provides no additional tax deferral. It also says the annuity is taxed like any other investment in the plan. If tax deferral is the reason given for placing the contract there, ask what else the annuity would provide and what its contract terms would cost. The potential benefit may instead be the income payments or another benefit specified in the contract. The income promise needs a close read. Investor.gov says annuity payments may continue for your life, the life of a spouse or partner, or a set period. Those are different payment arrangements, and the contract determines which one applies. Ask when payments start, how long they last and what happens under the contract's death-benefit terms. A payout figure cannot be inferred from the $24,500 401(k) deferral limit or from the description of an annuity type. Also distinguish a contract bought for immediate income from one intended to grow before payments begin. An immediate annuity typically starts income within a year after a single payment. A deferred annuity has an accumulation phase before its payout phase and may accept a single payment or flexible payments. Investor.gov says annuities are appropriate only for investors with a long-term investment horizon, and surrender charges can make an early change costly. A person weighing an annuity inside a traditional plan should ask how it fits their overall financial situation, as Investor.gov recommends. That question includes the contract's income terms, surrender schedule, explicit fees, limits on gains and the insurer's claims-paying ability. Ask about the proposed contract and the insurer's claims-paying ability rather than relying on the general label 'annuity.' Who This Is Not For and What Would Change Our Answer The match-first takeaway applies only when you can use an employer 401(k) that offers a match. The IRS says an employer may make matching contributions if its plan document permits them, and a match can be discretionary. A plan without a match removes that specific reason to prioritize a 401(k) contribution. Read the plan's match terms before using the match as a deciding factor. If you have no employer 401(k), there is no plan match to compare with an annuity. An IRA may be a more relevant account comparison, though the annuity's contract terms remain a separate question. Our 401(k) vs Roth IRA page covers the account distinction. It does not turn an annuity into the same kind of account as either one. This takeaway also does not serve someone who needs a dependable monthly payout estimate from a general comparison page. An annuity payment depends on the insurer's terms, your age, the contract type and the payment options. No single monthly figure in the cited sources can stand in for a contract quote. Ask insurers for written quotes using the same proposed payment and payout choices so the quoted income terms can be compared. If you may need access to the money during a surrender-charge period, give the withdrawal schedule more weight than a distant income promise. Investor.gov says surrender charges may apply for a specified number of years, and withdrawing a contract's value can also have tax consequences. Ask for the surrender schedule and any market value adjustment terms before considering that contract. The answer can shift again when you are near retirement and need income payments. Investor.gov describes immediate annuities that typically begin paying within a year and deferred annuities that pay later. Neither description supplies a monthly payout or makes a particular contract suitable for everyone. Compare the timing and duration of a written income quote with the timing of the income you need. Changes to the IRS limit or to the published tax rules would also require a fresh comparison. The $24,500 employee deferral limit stated here is for 2026. For an annuity vs 401k decision, read the IRS contribution rules for the applicable year, then obtain a written annuity quote and surrender schedule before comparing the two. Frequently asked questions Is an annuity better than a 401(k)? Neither is better in general because they do different jobs. A 401(k) is an employer plan that may offer a match, while an annuity is an insurance contract that can provide income payments. A match depends on the plan, and annuity costs depend on the contract. Can you have both an annuity and a 401(k)? Yes. An annuity is an insurance contract and a 401(k) is an employer plan. Investor.gov urges caution if an annuity is proposed inside a traditional 401(k) because it provides no additional tax deferral there. What is the 401(k) contribution limit in 2026? The 2026 employee elective-deferral limit is $24,500 or 100% of compensation, whichever is less. Catch-up contributions may be allowed if you are at least 50 and the plan permits them. See the 2026 401(k) contribution limits guide for more detail. What are surrender charges on an annuity? Surrender charges are amounts you may owe for withdrawing from an annuity within a specified number of years. Investor.gov says they generally decline over time. The proposed contract's schedule determines when a charge applies. Are annuity withdrawals taxed? An annuity withdrawal may be taxable. Under IRS Topic 558 , a distribution from a deferred annuity contract before age 59½ generally faces a 10% additional tax on its taxable portion, subject to exceptions. Do annuities have fees? Yes, annuity costs can include contract fees, underlying fund fees, optional-benefit fees or surrender charges. Investor.gov says some contracts have implicit costs reflected in credited interest or limits on gains rather than an explicit ongoing fee. How much does an annuity pay per month? There is no monthly amount that applies to every annuity. Payments depend on the insurer's rates, your age, the contract type and its options. Ask insurers for written quotes and use Investor.gov's questions to ask before buying to compare their terms.
