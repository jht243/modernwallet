answer: Verdict with the break-even. A 529 is worth it when the money will go to qualified education spending, because the earnings are then free of federal income tax; it is a weaker fit when the beneficiary may not use the money, because earnings withdrawn for non-qualified spending owe income tax plus a 10 percent additional tax, and contributions are not federally deductible. - 529 plans are also called qualified tuition programs (QTPs). States may establish and maintain programs that let you prepay or contribute to an account for paying a student's qualified education expenses at an eligible educational institution. [IRS Pub. 970 ch. 7]
- "You can't deduct either payments or contributions to a QTP." Contributions are not deductible federally. [IRS Pub. 970 ch. 7; IRS 529 Q&A: "Contributions to a 529 plan, however, are not deductible."]
- No tax is due on a distribution from a QTP unless the amount distributed is greater than the beneficiary's adjusted qualified education expenses (AQEE). [IRS Pub. 970]
- Earnings are not subject to federal tax and generally not subject to state tax when used for qualified education expenses. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- There are no income restrictions on the contributor or the beneficiary; you can set up a plan and name anyone (a relative, a friend, even yourself) as beneficiary; there is no limit to the number of plans you set up. Each account has one designated beneficiary. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- Whoever purchases the plan is the custodian and controls the funds until withdrawn. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- Congress created 529 plans in 1996; they are named for section 529 of the Internal Revenue Code. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- Two basic types: prepaid tuition plans and savings plans; each state has its own plan and states may offer both. You are not restricted to your own state's plan; compare features; your state's plan may offer incentives. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- 529 plans are sponsored by states, state agencies or educational institutions; all 50 states and DC sponsor at least one type; a group of private colleges and universities sponsor a prepaid tuition plan. The SEC does not regulate or oversee 529 plans. [SEC Investor.gov glossary]
- Contributions cannot exceed the amount necessary to provide for the qualified education expenses of the beneficiary. If contributions plus other gifts to one beneficiary exceed $19,000 during the year there may be gift tax consequences; Form 709 instructions describe a special rule for 529 plans. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- The benefit comes with the tax-free withdrawal of earnings built up over time; a plan set up while the student is already enrolled may not accrue enough earnings to be of immediate benefit. [IRS 529 Q&A]
- Qualified expenses: tuition and fees; books, supplies and equipment; room and board for students enrolled at least half-time, capped at the greater of the school's cost-of-attendance room and board allowance or the actual amount charged for school-owned housing; computer equipment, software and internet access if used primarily by the beneficiary while enrolled; fees, books, supplies and equipment for registered apprenticeship programs; and no more than $10,000 of principal or interest on qualified student loans of the beneficiary or a sibling (lifetime); certain postsecondary credentialing expenses. [IRS Pub. 970; IRS Topic 313]
- K-12: tuition and certain other expenses at an eligible elementary or secondary school qualify, limited to a total of $20,000 per year from all of the beneficiary's QTPs ($10,000 limit before Dec. 31, 2025). [IRS Topic 313]
- Taxable portion of a distribution: multiply the distributed earnings by AQEE divided by the total distribution; the remainder of the earnings is income to the beneficiary. [IRS Pub. 970]
- IRS Pub. 970 worked example: parents contributed $18,000 over the years; balance $27,000; the student had $8,300 of qualified expenses, of which $3,100 was a tax-free scholarship, so AQEE is $5,200; the QTP distribution was $5,300 and Form 1099-Q showed $950 of it was earnings. Tax-free earnings = $950 x $5,200 / $5,300 = $932; taxable earnings = $950 - $932 = $18. If the parents also claimed a $2,500 American opportunity credit on $4,000 of expenses, AQEE falls to $1,200, tax-free earnings are $215 and taxable earnings are $735. [IRS Pub. 970 examples 1 and 2]
- A taxable distribution generally also owes a 10% additional tax on the amount included in income. Exceptions: paid on or after the death of the beneficiary; the beneficiary is disabled; included in income because the beneficiary received a tax-free scholarship, veterans' or employer educational assistance or other tax-free educational assistance (only to the extent the distribution is not more than that assistance); attendance at a U.S. military academy (to the extent of the costs of advanced education); included in income only because the expenses were used for the American opportunity or lifetime learning credit. [IRS Pub. 970]
- The part of a distribution that represents what you paid in is a return of investment and is not included in income; Form 1099-Q splits each distribution into earnings (box 2) and basis (box 3). [IRS Pub. 970]
- Plain arithmetic (not from a source; label as an illustration): if a $10,000 non-qualified withdrawal is 40% earnings, $4,000 is earnings and $6,000 is return of contributions. The $4,000 is added to income and taxed at the owner's ordinary rate, and the 10% additional tax on it is $400. The $6,000 is not taxed. The rate on the $4,000 depends on the owner's bracket; state no bracket.
- Changing the designated beneficiary to a member of the beneficiary's family has no income tax consequences. Family includes spouse, son, daughter, stepchild, foster child, adopted child or their descendants; brother, sister, half and step siblings; father, mother or ancestors; stepparents; nieces and nephews; aunts and uncles; in-laws; first cousins; and spouses of any of these. [IRS Pub. 970]
- Rollovers: a distribution is not taxable if rolled over within 60 days to another QTP for the same beneficiary or a family member, or to an ABLE account for the beneficiary or a family member (subject to the ABLE annual limit). For a same-beneficiary QTP-to-QTP rollover, generally only one is allowed within 12 months. [IRS Pub. 970]
- Roth IRA rollover: beneficiary only; distributions after Dec. 31, 2023; direct trustee-to-trustee transfer; subject to the Roth IRA annual contribution limit and a $35,000 lifetime limit; the QTP account must have been open at least 15 years; may not exceed the amount contributed to the QTP (and attributable earnings) before the 5-year period ending on the date of the distribution. [IRS Topic 313; IRS Pub. 970]
- Student loans: up to $10,000 lifetime of QTP distributions can pay principal or interest on the beneficiary's or a sibling's qualified student loans; interest paid this way does not qualify for the student loan interest deduction. [IRS Topic 313; IRS Pub. 970]
- The education credits (American opportunity, lifetime learning) can be claimed in the same year as a tax-free QTP distribution only if the same expenses are not used for both. [IRS Pub. 970]
