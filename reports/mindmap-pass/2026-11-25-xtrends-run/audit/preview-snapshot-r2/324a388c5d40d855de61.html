How Retail Investors Get IPO Shares

Retail access to initial public offering shares before exchange trading begins is limited for most retail investors and underwriting distribution rules. The SEC investor bulletin on IPOs explains that underwriters distribute the majority of initial public offering shares to institutional accounts and high-net-worth clients first. Because of this distribution model, most individual investors can only purchase shares once the stock begins trading on the secondary market, which frequently opens at a price above the original initial public offering price.

Underwriting distributions are regulated under FINRA Rule 5131, which governs new issue allocations and distributions. Anthropic's Form S-1 registration statement notes that the lead representatives may allocate shares to underwriters for distribution to their online brokerage account holders. The filing also states that the underwriters do not expect sales to discretionary accounts to exceed five percent of the total shares offered. To learn more about how public offerings function, review our guide on what is an IPO.

If you want to participate in an offering before public trading starts, you must ask your individual brokerage firm whether it offers initial public offering access and what eligibility criteria it enforces. The filing guarantees no shares to U.S. retail account holders.

Pre-IPO Offers and Scams

Unsolicited opportunities to purchase private shares before an initial public offering carry real risk. According to the Investor.gov pre-IPO alert issued on June 7, 2024, pre-IPO investing carries substantial risk, including the potential loss of your entire investment principal. Private pre-IPO offerings are not registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and offerings marketed broadly to the general public may be illegal.

The Investor.gov alert names these risks of pre-IPO investing:

  • No public listing: The company may delay its plans indefinitely or cancel its public offering altogether.
  • No resale market: A secondary trading market may never develop, leaving buyers unable to resell or transfer their holdings.

The alert highlights multiple warning signs associated with private stock solicitations. Red flags include unregistered individuals or firms offering private equity deals, aggressive sales tactics such as unsolicited cold calls, marketing pushes on social media channels, and high-pressure claims centered around popular emerging technologies. Before transferring money to any entity promising access to private shares, verify the registration status of the seller using the free registration check on Investor.gov.

Lock-Ups and What Happens After Listing

In its Form S-1 registration statement, Anthropic described a 180-day lock-up for company insiders and certain stockholders, along with a 365-day lock-up for specified holders. Underwriting representatives may release lock-up securities in whole or in part before those periods end.

The final prospectus controls the actual terms. Read the "Shares Eligible for Future Sale" section in the official Form 424B4 filing index to verify the governing restrictions.

The CNBC article from November 24, 2026, which reported that shares closed below the $83 initial public offering (IPO) price, does not mention lock-ups. According to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) lock-up guide, a lock-up agreement temporarily prevents company insiders and early investors from selling their equity. When lock-up restrictions expire or underwriters authorize an early release, blocks of previously restricted shares can reach the market and increase the circulating supply.
