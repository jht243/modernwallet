## How Retail Investors Get IPO Shares
Retail access to initial public offering shares before exchange trading begins is limited for most retail investors and underwriting distribution rules. The [SEC investor bulletin on IPOs](https://www.sec.gov/files/ipo-investorbulletin.pdf) explains that underwriters distribute the majority of initial public offering shares to institutional accounts and high-net-worth clients first. Because of this distribution model, most individual investors can only purchase shares once the stock begins trading on the secondary market, which frequently opens at a price above the original initial public offering price.

Underwriting distributions are regulated under [FINRA Rule 5131](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/5131), which governs new issue allocations and distributions. Anthropic's Form S-1 registration statement notes that the lead representatives may allocate shares to underwriters for distribution to their online brokerage account holders. The filing also states that the underwriters do not expect sales to discretionary accounts to exceed five percent of the total shares offered. To learn more about how public offerings function, review our guide on [what is an IPO](/guides/what-is-an-ipo/).

If you want to participate in an offering before public trading starts, you must ask your individual brokerage firm whether it offers initial public offering access and what eligibility criteria it enforces. The filing guarantees no shares to U.S. retail account holders.

## Pre-IPO Offers and Scams
Unsolicited opportunities to purchase private shares before an initial public offering carry real risk. According to the [Investor.gov pre-IPO alert](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alert-pre-ipo-investment-scams-updated) issued on June 7, 2024, pre-IPO investing carries substantial risk, including the potential loss of your entire investment principal. Private pre-IPO offerings are not registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), and offerings marketed broadly to the general public may be illegal.

The Investor.gov alert names these risks of pre-IPO investing:

- No public listing: The company may delay its plans indefinitely or cancel its public offering altogether.
- No resale market: A secondary trading market may never develop, leaving buyers unable to resell or transfer their holdings.

The alert highlights multiple warning signs associated with private stock solicitations. Red flags include unregistered individuals or firms offering private equity deals, aggressive sales tactics such as unsolicited cold calls, marketing pushes on social media channels, and high-pressure claims centered around popular emerging technologies. Before transferring money to any entity promising access to private shares, verify the registration status of the seller using the free registration check on Investor.gov.

## Lock-Up and What Happens After Listing
The Form S-1 registration statement establishes a 180-day lock-up and market standoff period for company insiders and certain stockholders, along with an extended 365-day lock-up period for specified holders. The filing notes that the underwriting representatives retain discretionary authority to release lock-up securities in whole or in part prior to expiration.

The [SEC lock-up guide](https://www.sec.gov/fast-answers/answerslockuphtm.html) notes that lock-up agreements restrict company insiders from selling their shares for a designated window of time following a public listing. Once these contractual restrictions expire or underwriters grant releases, substantial blocks of previously restricted shares can enter public circulation and reach the market.
