## Will You Lose Medicaid on January 1?
You will not automatically lose Medicaid on January 1, 2027, for missing the community engagement benchmark, but your immediate protection depends directly on whether your state chooses to pause disenrollments. Under section 71119 of Public Law 119-21, often called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation, the federal baseline requires states to implement community engagement rules no later than January 1, 2027. However, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/medicaid-community-engagement-requirement-certain-individuals-interim-final-rule-comment-period-cms)) released State Medicaid Director Letter SMD# 26-006 on November 13, 2026, granting states an optional hold-harmless transition window.

In a state that formally elects this hold-harmless period, the Medicaid agency can suspend all eligibility denials and disenrollments based on community engagement noncompliance through June 30, 2027. This suspension applies to initial applications submitted and redetermination renewals initiated through that mid-year date. Even in participating states, administrators must still collect information and assess whether members meet the engagement rules. Enrollees must continue submitting paperwork, but coverage cannot be terminated strictly for failing the work requirement during this window.

In states that do not adopt the hold-harmless policy, standard enforcement procedures begin immediately at the start of 2027. If an individual in an enforcement state fails to demonstrate required hours or an exemption, the state agency cannot terminate benefits without warning. Federal regulations under the CMS Interim Final Rule (IFR) require the agency to send a formal noncompliance notice. Beneficiaries then receive a window of 30 calendar days to submit proof of qualifying activities or claim an exemption before disenrollment takes effect.

## Who the Rule Covers and Who Is Exempt
The federal community engagement mandate applies strictly to non-pregnant adults ages 19 through 64 who are enrolled in the Medicaid expansion adult group or certain section 1115 minimum essential coverage demonstrations across 43 states and the District of Columbia. United States territories are not subject to the mandate, and individuals entitled to or enrolled in Medicare are excluded from these requirements. To satisfy the standard, covered adults must log at least 80 hours per month of qualifying activities or earn wages equal to at least 80 times the federal minimum wage, which equals $580 per month in 2026.

Qualifying activities can be combined to reach the 80-hour monthly threshold. Approved activities include work, community service, a work program, or half-time education, or a combination. When checking compliance, state agencies review past records: applicants must demonstrate compliance for at least one month prior to application, while existing enrollees are checked across one or more months between redetermination cycles as determined by individual state rules.

Federal policy provides explicit statutory exemptions that protect vulnerable groups from disenrollment. According to official CMS documentation, you are exempt from the 80-hour monthly rule if you meet any of the following criteria:

* You are pregnant or postpartum.
* You are a parent or primary caretaker responsible for a child age 13 or younger, or you care for an individual with a recognized disability.
* You are a former foster youth.
* You are an American Indian or Alaska Native individual.
* You are a totally disabled veteran.
* You are deemed medically frail.
* You are in substance use disorder treatment.
* You are currently complying with existing work requirements under the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) program or the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP).
* You are currently incarcerated in a correctional facility.

If you qualify for any exemption, you must notify your state agency to ensure your administrative record is coded appropriately.

## What a Hold-Harmless State Can and Cannot Do
State Medicaid Director Letter SMD# 26-006 establishes clear boundaries regarding state authority during the transition. A state that elects the hold-harmless option receives federal protection against CMS enforcement actions if it chooses to halt adverse eligibility decisions. Under this mechanism, the state Medicaid program can suspend both application denials and mid-year disenrollments for individuals who do not show compliance with community engagement standards. States have the flexibility to implement this relief for the entire period through June 30, 2027, or choose a shorter calendar duration that suits their technical rollout schedules.

Electing the hold-harmless policy does not allow a state to pause normal Medicaid verification standards. States are expressly required to build reporting mechanisms, gather activity documentation, and evaluate whether applicants qualify for exemptions. States intending to use the hold-harmless option starting January 1, 2027, were asked by CMS to submit notifications by December 11, 2026, through their formal community engagement mitigation plans.

The hold-harmless period applies only to community engagement standards under section 1902(xx)(1) of the Social Security Act and 42 CFR 435.559. A state cannot suspend denials or terminations based on standard eligibility rules. If an enrollee turns 65 or reports household income exceeding statutory Medicaid expansion thresholds, the state must disenroll them according to regular redetermination timelines.

As verified on November 23, 2026, by the [Oregon Health Authority](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ERD/Pages/OHA-to-delay-enforcement-of-new-federal-Medicaid-work-rules-to-keep-people-in-Oregon-insured-11.23.2026.aspx) (OHA), Oregon is the only state officially confirmed to have elected the hold-harmless transition. OHA confirmed that no Oregon Health Plan member will face coverage denials or disenrollment for community engagement noncompliance through June 30, 2027. Oregon state officials noted that more than 600,000 members received informational notices in September 2026, and internal projections estimated that up to one-third of covered adults risked losing access purely due to reporting hurdles. Implementation also occurs alongside ongoing legal developments: on October 29, 2026, the federal district court in Massachusetts v. Oz partially vacated specific CMS regulatory limits regarding medical condition exemptions, which CMS formally acknowledged in footnote three of SMD# 26-006.
