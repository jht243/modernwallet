answer: A 401(k) loan lets you borrow from your own vested balance if your plan allows it, up to the lesser of $50,000 (less any higher balance you carried in the past year) or the greater of $10,000 or half your vested balance. It generally has to be repaid in level payments, at least quarterly, within 5 years unless it buys your main home. A loan that is not repaid on those terms is treated as a taxable distribution.

## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- Under federal tax law, a loan from a qualified employer plan is treated as a distribution unless it meets exceptions. Rule: a loan is treated as having been received as a distribution (26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(1)(A)).
- Amount limit (26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(A)): the loan, added to all other outstanding loans from the plan, must not exceed the lesser of (i) $50,000, reduced by the excess (if any) of the highest outstanding loan balance from the plan during the 1-year period ending the day before the loan is made over the outstanding balance on the date the loan is made, or (ii) the greater of one-half of the present value of the employee's nonforfeitable accrued benefit under the plan or $10,000.
- Repayment term (§ 72(p)(2)(B)(i)): by its terms the loan must be repaid within 5 years. The exception (§ 72(p)(2)(B)(ii)): the 5-year limit does not apply to a loan used to acquire a dwelling unit that is to be used within a reasonable time (determined when the loan is made) as the participant's principal residence.
- Level payments (§ 72(p)(2)(C)): substantially level amortization of the loan, with payments not less frequently than quarterly, is required over the term of the loan.
- Interest deduction (§ 72(p)(3)(A)): no deduction is allowed for interest on a plan loan during any period the borrower is a key employee or the loan is secured by certain elective deferrals. Say only that interest deductibility is restricted in these cases; give no further rule.
- 10% additional tax: a 10% additional tax applies to certain early distributions from retirement plans such as a 401(k) (IRS Topic 558: "the law imposes a 10% additional tax on certain early distributions from certain retirement plans"). Early distributions are generally those received before age 59½; exceptions exist (IRS Topic 558). A loan that is treated as a distribution can therefore bring income tax and, for a younger borrower, this additional tax; state it as "can", not as certain.
- Plan loan offset rollover (26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(3)(C)(i)): for a "qualified plan loan offset amount", a rollover can be made up to the due date (including extensions) for filing the tax return for the tax year in which the amount is treated as distributed from the plan. The ordinary rollover window is 60 days after the distribution is received (§ 402(c)(3)(A)). Do not define which offsets qualify beyond saying it applies to a qualified plan loan offset amount, and tell the reader to ask the plan administrator or a tax professional.
- Whether a plan offers loans, its fees, interest rate, the number of loans allowed and its default rules are set by the plan. The statute lets plans offer loans; it does not require them.

Computed from the statute (state the arithmetic): a participant with a $60,000 vested balance and no prior loans: half is $30,000, which is greater than $10,000, and lower than $50,000, so the cap is $30,000. A participant with a $15,000 vested balance: half is $7,500, the greater of that and $10,000 is $10,000, so the cap is $10,000. A participant with $200,000 vested and no prior loans: half is $100,000, the lesser of that and $50,000 is $50,000. Present these as illustrations of the formula, not advice. Treat "vested" as a plain-language stand-in for "nonforfeitable accrued benefit".
