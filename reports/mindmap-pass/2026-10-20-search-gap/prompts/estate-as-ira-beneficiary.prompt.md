CLOSED FACT LIST (Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify at the IRS page.):
- IRS Publication 590-B: an estate is not an individual, so it cannot be a designated beneficiary; the publication's example of a beneficiary that is not an individual is "the owner's estate".
- IRS Pub. 590-B: the 5-year rule applies when the owner died before the required beginning date and the beneficiary is not an individual (such as an estate or trust). The entire balance must be withdrawn by December 31 of the year containing the fifth anniversary of the owner's death. No life expectancy table is used. Any amount left after that date is subject to the excise tax for insufficient distributions.
- The law sets no required annual withdrawals in years 1 through 4 under the 5-year rule; withdrawals can follow any schedule as long as the account is empty by the deadline (Vanguard and IRA-industry guidance, consistent with Pub. 590-B).
- IRS Pub. 590-B: if the owner died on or after the required beginning date and there is no designated beneficiary, the payout period is the owner's life expectancy for the owner's age in the year of death, reduced by one for each later year.
- IRS Pub. 590-B: required beginning date = April 1 of the year following the year the owner reaches age 73.
- IRS Pub. 590-B: designated beneficiaries are generally identified as of September 30 of the calendar year after the owner's death.
- The 10-year rule applies to designated beneficiaries (individuals); an estate is not one (Vanguard RMD rules for inherited IRAs; Pub. 590-B).
- IRS Pub. 590-B: the beneficiary can deduct the estate tax paid on any part of a distribution that is income with respect to a decedent, for the tax year the income is reported; for amounts that are not that kind of income there is no deduction. Pub. 559 explains how to claim it ("Estate Tax Deduction").
- IRS Pub. 590-B: the value of payments receivable by a beneficiary of a decedent's traditional IRA may be included in the decedent's gross estate; Schedule I of Form 706 instructions cover the details.
- IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Table 5, 2026 tax on estates and trusts: taxable income not over $3,300 is taxed at 10%; over $3,300 to $11,700 is $330 plus 24% of the excess over $3,300; over $11,700 to $16,000 is $2,346 plus 35% of the excess over $11,700; over $16,000 is $3,851 plus 37% of the excess over $16,000.
- 2026 federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000 per individual (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21); the federal rate on the excess is a flat 40% (this site's estate-tax guide).
- A beneficiary designation on an IRA or 401(k) overrides the will (this site's estate planning guide).
- An estate reports its income on Form 1041, U.S. Income Tax Return for Estates and Trusts (IRS).
- Whether distributions are taxed to the estate or passed out to the heirs, and how a state taxes them, depends on the facts; the page must say to ask a CPA or estate attorney. Roth IRA balances inherited by an estate follow the same payout clocks, but qualified Roth distributions are not taxed; do not state more than this.
