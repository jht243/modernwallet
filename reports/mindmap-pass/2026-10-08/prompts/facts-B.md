## B1) Form 4547
- Official title: Form 4547, "Trump Account Election(s)". Instructions revision 12/2025. [irs.gov/instructions/i4547]
- Two purposes: (1) elect to open an initial Trump Account (a traditional IRA for the child's exclusive benefit); (2) elect the one-time $1,000 pilot program contribution. [i4547; irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-4547]
- Who may open an account: a legal guardian, parent, adult sibling or grandparent, in that order of priority. Who may also request the $1,000: a person who expects the child to be their qualifying child for the election year. [i4547]
- Pilot ($1,000) eligibility: child born 2025 through 2028, U.S. citizen, valid SSN, the filer's qualifying child. [i4547]
- Account eligibility: child under 18 at year-end, valid SSN, no prior Trump Account election. [i4547]
- Four parts: Part I the authorized individual's name, address, SSN (this person becomes the responsible party); Part II the child's information, with an authorization checkbox on line 6; Part III the pilot election checkbox on line 7; Part IV consent to disclosure, signature, paid preparer. [i4547]
- Filing: any time, including with an e-filed Form 1040 (the IRS calls e-filing with the return the fastest method). Paper filers mail it separately to the address at IRS.gov/PaperReturns. Do not attach it to a Form 1040-X and do not amend a return to add it. The instructions also point to trumpaccounts.gov for online elections. [i4547]
- The online route at irs.gov/trumpaccounts requires an ID.me sign-in plus the child's SSN, date of birth and address. [irs.gov/trumpaccounts]
- Deadline: none stated beyond "any time" while the child is eligible; TurboTax advises making the election before the year the child turns 18. [TurboTax, updated Oct 2 2026]
- TurboTax: "Yes, TurboTax users can file IRS Form 4547 with their federal income tax return" — requires e-filing the return. [TurboTax]
- Temporary regulations T.D. 10056 (published September 30, 2026) — Treasury made an election on or about October 1, 2026 for every eligible child without a prior election ("auto accounts"), which is expected to "generally eliminate the need for any election by a person other than the Secretary"; Form 4547 remains available for rare exceptions. [Federal Register 2026-20026]
- BUT: the regulations say "the Secretary cannot make a pilot program election". An auto account gets the $1,000 only if an eligible individual has made a pilot election. [Federal Register 2026-20026]
- A Form 4547 election alone does not establish an account; the account must be activated (claimed). [Federal Register 2026-20026]
- Treasury (Oct 1, 2026) says the account must be claimed in the app to receive the $1,000. [sb0642]
- UNKNOWN — say so plainly: whether claiming in the app also counts as the pilot election (making Part III unnecessary). Tell parents of children born 2025–2028 to confirm the $1,000 election is on file, via the app or the 1-866-USA-4547 call center, and that filing Form 4547 Part III is the documented way to make it.

## B2) FAFSA
- The U.S. Education Department had not issued guidance naming Trump Accounts as of early October 2026; FAFSA materials do not mention them. [search of fsapartners.ed.gov]
- Expert view 1 (Mark Kantrowitz, quoted by CNBC July 9, 2026): reported as a STUDENT asset, assessed at up to 20%; e.g. a $10,000 balance could reduce aid by about $2,000. A parent-owned 529 is assessed at up to 5.64%.
- Expert view 2 (Kalman Chany): the account could be treated like an IRA; retirement accounts are not reportable FAFSA assets.
- This is UNSETTLED. The page must present both views and not pick a definitive answer.
- After December 31 of the year the child turns 17, traditional-IRA rules apply. Withdrawals are taxable income and could count as student income on a later FAFSA. Whether the balance is then excluded is not confirmed.

## B3) Gift tax
- Rev. Proc. 2026-25 (IRS, June 29, 2026) creates a safe harbor: if all conditions are met, each contribution is a completed gift that is not a future interest and qualifies for the annual exclusion, so no Form 709 is required. [irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-26-25.pdf]
- Five conditions, all required: (1) the donor is an individual; (2) the donor's only taxable gifts that year are cash contributions to Trump Accounts, each made before the year the beneficiary turns 18; (3) total gifts to each beneficiary, including the contribution, are at or below the annual exclusion; (4) no gift or GST tax liability results; (5) no gift tax return is otherwise required or filed that year.
- If any condition fails, the donor files Form 709 and reports all Trump Account contributions as future-interest gifts. IRS example: a $5,000 contribution plus a $14,500 cash gift to the same child totals $19,500, over $19,000, so the safe harbor fails.
- 2026 annual gift tax exclusion: $19,000 per recipient. Lifetime exemption: $15,000,000.
- Before the Rev. Proc., ACTEC (estate lawyers' group) had argued contributions would be future-interest gifts.
- Gift-tax treatment of employer contributions is not covered by the Rev. Proc. (do not state a rule).

## B4) Stock donations
- Source: temporary regulations T.D. 10056 (§1.530A-7T) plus an identical proposed rule, published September 30, 2026; comments due about November 30, 2026. [Federal Register 2026-20026; KPMG]
- Eligible donors are limited to: a state or local government, the United States or DC, an Indian tribal government, or a 501(c)(3) charity (including through a donor-advised fund). Individuals and companies can give stock only through a 501(c)(3).
- Donations go through Treasury as a "general funding contribution" split equally across a qualified class (e.g. all beneficiaries under 18, those in a state or designated area with at least 5,000 eligible children, or those born in specified years). Families cannot pick the stock or refuse it.
- Qualifying stock: issued by a U.S. (domestic) corporation, listed on a registered national exchange, no pre-existing transfer restrictions.
- Shares are HELD, not sold right away: until the earlier of 5 years after receipt or the end of the growth period (December 31 of the year the child turns 17). If sold early, the trustee must buy back the same shares as soon as practicable. Afterward, the trustee sells within a reasonable time and reinvests in an eligible index fund.
- Treasury's rationale: the eligible-investment rule limits what the account buys, not what is contributed.
- Criticism: Morningstar's Christine Benz said the accounts got "very weird very quickly"; others cite lack of diversification and that families cannot sell to cut losses during the hold; no way to decline the gift. Michael Dell called the criticism "nonsense".
- Base rule unchanged for family money: eligible investments are mutual funds or ETFs tracking the S&P 500 or another mostly-U.S. stock index, no leverage, fees at or below 0.1%.
