## THE CLOSED FACT LIST
Sources read 2026-11-12: the Federal Register final rule "Improving Transparency Into Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure" (Federal Register document 2026-22306, 91 FR 69092, published Nov. 2, 2026, Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA), Department of Labor (DOL), RIN 1210-AB37, new 29 CFR 2550.408b-22), full text read via govinfo. Every fact below comes from that document. Attribute facts to the final rule or to the Department. The preamble facts and the regulatory text are both in the document.

Rule facts:
1. Who must disclose: "providers of pharmacy benefit management services and affiliated providers of brokerage and consulting services" (SUMMARY). A covered service provider is one that enters a contract or arrangement with the covered plan and reasonably expects $1,000 or more in direct or indirect compensation (para (c)(1)). The rule defines PBM services by function, "regardless of whether the person ... identifies itself as a 'pharmacy benefit manager'" (para (d)(1)).
2. Who receives the disclosures: "fiduciaries of large, self-insured group health plans" (SUMMARY). The disclosure goes "to a responsible plan fiduciary, in writing" (paras (e) and (g)). A responsible plan fiduciary is a fiduciary with authority to cause the covered plan to enter, extend or renew the service contract (para (m)(4)). The preamble says plan fiduciaries include plan sponsors responsible for selecting and monitoring service providers. The operative text contains no duty to send disclosures to plan participants (workers). The word "participants" appears only in the per-participant fee example, the copay claw-back definition, the formulary-incentive explanation and the plan-size definitions.
3. Which plans (CR-A2, state exactly): para (b) defines a "covered plan" as a group health plan "offered by a specified large employer or that is a specified large plan", other than (1) "a group health plan in which all of the benefits are provided exclusively through a contract or policy of insurance issued by a health insurance issuer" and (2) a group health plan "established by a plan sponsor that is, or that is affiliated with, a drug manufacturer, distributor, wholesaler, pharmacy benefit manager, rebate aggregator (or other purchasing entity designed to aggregate rebates)". The summary describes the rule as covering large, self-insured group health plans. Do not write that the rule applies to "self-insured plans only" without this paragraph (b) qualifier: the regulatory text defines coverage by size and by those two exclusions, so a plan whose benefits are all provided through an insurance policy is out, and so is a plan sponsored by a drug-supply-chain affiliate.
3a. Specified large employer (para (m)(6)): for a plan maintained by a single employer, an employer that employed an average of at least 100 employees on business days during the preceding calendar year and employs at least 1 employee on the first day of the calendar year, counted under the employee-counting and aggregation rules of Internal Revenue Code section 4980H.
3b. Specified large plan (para (m)(7)): a group health plan established or maintained by a plan sponsor described in 29 U.S.C. 1002(16)(B)(ii) or (iii) (a category the rule treats separately from single-employer plans, which are tested under para (m)(6); the rule does not restate the statute's wording on the page, so describe it only as sponsors described in those statute subsections) that had an average of at least 100 participants on business days during the preceding plan year. Do not say that any plan with 100 or more participants is a specified large plan. Say a single-employer plan is covered through the 100-employee test and a specified large plan is a different category defined by who sponsors the plan.
4. Smaller self-insured plans: not covered by this rule's detailed disclosures. They "are covered plans for purposes of the application of the statutory exemption in ERISA section 408(b)(2)(B)", so their service providers "must provide the generalized compensation disclosures specified in the statute" (preamble 1.2.1). The Department "is reserving" action on fully insured plans.
5. Timing: the initial disclosure is due "not later than the date that is reasonably in advance of the date on which the service contract or arrangement is entered, and extended or renewed" and for extensions and renewals "90 calendar days in advance is deemed to be a reasonable period of time" (para (e)). After that, disclosures come "on a semiannual basis (or at the request of the covered plan, quarterly) no later than 30 calendar days after the end of each six-month period or quarter" (para (g)). A provider must correct errors within 30 calendar days of knowing (para (l)).
6. What must be disclosed (paras (e) and (g)): a description of services; direct compensation in aggregate and by service; indirect compensation including manufacturer payments (rebates, fees, alternative discounts, other remuneration) in aggregate and for each formulary drug, with the amount passed to the plan and the amount retained; spread compensation by drug (National Drug Code) and by pharmacy channel (retail, mail order, specialty), plus arrangements that steer patients to affiliated pharmacies; pharmacy recoupment, including "copay claw-back compensation" (defined as the difference between a copayment or coinsurance amount paid to the pharmacy by a plan participant and the reimbursement to the pharmacy); price-protection agreements; termination compensation; formulary placement incentives and explanations of why reasonably available therapeutic equivalent alternatives were omitted; net price per fill by drug and channel (or a methodology with an objective way to verify); fiduciary status; and an audit right for the plan.
7. Format and use: amounts are expressed in dollars, with good-faith estimates allowed. A machine-readable file must be provided on request (para (k)(3)). The provider may not restrict the plan's use of the disclosures except for reasonable confidentiality agreements for third parties the fiduciary rediscloses to; plans may share with regulators and legal counsel (para (k)(4)).
8. Dates: "This regulation is effective January 4, 2027. This regulation is applicable to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027." (DATES; para (p)). The preamble explains the applicability date gives time to revise current and future contracts. By arithmetic on those dates: a calendar-year plan's first plan year beginning on or after July 1, 2027 starts January 1, 2028, so the rule does not apply to a calendar-year plan's 2027 plan year; a plan whose year begins July 1, 2027 would be the earliest covered. The rule was published in the Federal Register on Nov. 2, 2026 and signed Oct. 28, 2026 by Daniel Aronowitz, Assistant Secretary, EBSA.
9. The rule sets no cost-sharing amount. The regulatory text (paras (a) to (p)) imposes disclosure and audit obligations and contains no copay, coinsurance, deductible, premium or formulary requirement. The preamble says rebates "can offset premiums and other healthcare costs" and that without disclosure "responsible plan fiduciaries are unable to assess whether the underlying fees for PBM services are reasonable".
10. How copays can still be affected, indirectly (the Department's own preamble): "In many self-insured group health plans, coinsurance is often based on list price, resulting in higher out-of-pocket costs for participants"; participants "often pay the full cost of the drug up to the amount of their annual deductible and a portion of prescription drug costs after the deductible is satisfied, typically in the form of coinsurance" (section B.1); the cost controls PBMs market to employers "can result in higher out-of-pockets costs for employees due to less favorable copayments or coinsurance" (regulatory impact analysis); and manufacturers raise list prices to protect net prices, so "patients may pay cost-sharing based on the drug's list price, even though the net price after rebates is substantially lower". Copay claw-back and rebate details are disclosed to the plan fiduciary, not to workers, and not as a refund to workers.

What the Department and the researchers it cites say about costs (CR-A1: state BOTH directions, attributed, in the cost section and the takeaway):
11. The Department's expectation, lower costs possible: the Department says disclosure of spread pricing at the drug and pharmacy-channel level, formulary placement incentives and the reasons therapeutic alternatives were omitted lets responsible plan fiduciaries "attain more appropriate formulary placement, more equitable patient cost-sharing, and broader access to prescription drugs ... which could result in reduced prescription drug spending for large, self-insured group health plans and lower out-of-pocket costs for participants". It also says disclosures "may result in self-insured group health plans retaining greater rebate shares, increasing the use of generics and biosimilars, and promoting less expensive pharmacy networks", which "can result in cost savings for self-insured group health plans, which may share these cost savings with plan participants through reduced premium payments, as well as lower out-of-pocket costs that participants and beneficiaries face when filling their prescriptions." The wording is "could", "may" and "can": the Department states an expectation, not a guarantee.
12. CBO and the bigger-impact statement (Department's regulatory impact discussion, section 8.1): the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimated in October 2024 that requiring PBMs to share prescription drug price information with health issuers would reduce prescription drug prices by 0.1 percent to 1.0 percent, and noted that increased transparency would help some PBM clients, particularly smaller plans, negotiate better contract terms, while predicting the overall impact would be limited because many existing contracts already include information sharing. The Department then says: "the self-insured plans covered in this final rule is not subject to state disclosure laws, thus the final rule could have a bigger impact than CBO's estimates." Quote this sentence in substance and attribute it to the Department.
13. CBO and the bill S. 1339 (a different bill, the Pharmacy Benefit Manager Reform Act): CBO estimated that bill "could reduce net retail prescription drug costs by more than 0.5 percent in the first full year of implementation, which could lower average premiums for employment-based health insurance by less than 0.1 percent in the first year", with the effect on premiums diminishing to "less than 0.01 percent by 2034", and said "this does not imply that premiums would decline; rather, premiums are still expected to increase, but at a slower rate". This concerns a different bill, not this final rule.
14. Scanlon (2024), cited by the Department: a study of outpatient prescription drug claims for chronic conditions in employer-sponsored health plans from 2014 to 2022 examined state-level PBM disclosure laws. It found that inter-firm disclosures, "those most like the ones contemplated in this rulemaking", increased prescription drug costs for plans by 3.5 percent but reduced out-of-pocket costs for participants (the sum of the copayment and coinsurance) by 1 percent. The effect varied by drug market: in competitive drug markets disclosures increased plan costs while the impact on participants was insignificant; in monopoly drug markets there was no significant impact on plans while patient costs significantly declined (a 9.4 percent decrease in out-of-pocket costs for those drugs). The author is Ginger Scanlon, "Prescription for Savings? Disclosure in the Drug Market" (Dec. 20, 2024).
15. Mixed research, cited by the Department: a 2025 study of Medicare Part D data from 2016 to 2022 (Nicola Maria Fiore, "Can Transparency Reduce Drug Costs", revised Apr. 17, 2026) found that state laws requiring PBMs to disclose rebate information "did not reduce prescription drug costs, including premiums, deductibles, or out-of-pocket spending". The Department concludes that "while disclosure mandates may improve transparency, their direct effects on consumer drug spending remain uncertain." These studies concern state laws and bills, not this final rule.
15a. Net reading for the page (derived only from facts 11 to 15): the Department expects lower plan costs and possibly lower participant out-of-pocket costs and says the effect could exceed CBO's estimates; the cited studies disagree on whether disclosure lowers costs, and Scanlon found plans' costs rose 3.5 percent while participant out-of-pocket costs fell 1 percent; whether any savings reach a worker's copay depends on what the plan sponsor does with the information. Do not turn any of this into a promise that copays will fall, or that they will not.
16. Related statute: ERISA section 726 (added by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026) requires PBM reports on drug spending to large self-insured plans every six months, applicable to plan years beginning on or after August 3, 2028, and requires all group health plans to receive a summary document "that plans can provide to participants and beneficiaries upon request by the participant or beneficiary" (preamble 1.2). This is separate from, and later than, this rule.
17. A PBM is the intermediary a health plan hires to manage its prescription drug benefit. The rule's own function-based definition (fact 1) covers firms that provide pharmacy benefit management services. Do not state how PBMs make money in terms beyond facts 1, 6, 10 and 11 (direct compensation, manufacturer rebates and fees, spread compensation, pharmacy recoupment, formulary placement incentives).
