What the DOL PBM Fee Disclosure Rule Means for Employer Drug Plans and Copays

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated November 12, 2026

The Department of Labor (DOL) rule on pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) fee disclosure does not change the prescription drug plans or copays that workers choose during 2026 open enrollment. The Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA) issued the final regulation, Improving Transparency Into Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure, which is the DOL PBM fee disclosure rule. It becomes effective January 4, 2027. It applies to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027.

The rule requires PBMs to disclose their direct and indirect compensation to the fiduciaries of large, self-insured group health plans. Workers do not receive these disclosures. Employers do not have to change copays, deductibles, premiums or drug formularies. The rule covers fee transparency between PBMs and the employer plan fiduciaries who hire them, and it sets no price limit for consumers.

Workers comparing employer coverage for the upcoming calendar year will not see adjustments driven by this regulation. The rule gives no calendar date for the first disclosures. The earliest covered plan years begin July 1, 2027, and calendar-year plans first become covered on January 1, 2028. Because the rule requires disclosure reasonably in advance of a contract's start or renewal, fiduciaries could see the first disclosures before those dates. Any future shift in copays depends on how employers use the fee data.

What the Rule Changes for Workers During Open Enrollment

The final regulation does not alter the prescription drug plans, copayment tiers, or deductibles presented to workers during 2026 open enrollment. The rule takes effect on January 4, 2027, and its disclosure obligations apply only to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027. Furthermore, the regulation applies strictly to covered large self-insured plans, and its operative text creates no obligation for service providers or employers to send fee disclosures to plan participants. The rule sets no limits on copays, establishes no caps on coinsurance, and contains no requirements regarding plan premiums or covered drug lists.

What the regulation changes is the level of compensation detail delivered to responsible plan fiduciaries who oversee covered employer health plans. Service providers of pharmacy benefit management services expecting $1,000 or more in direct or indirect compensation must provide written disclosures detailing their fees, manufacturer rebates, spread pricing, and pharmacy recoupment. Plan fiduciaries must receive this information in advance of signing, extending, or renewing a service contract, followed by semiannual or quarterly reporting.

For an employee selecting a workplace benefit package today, the terms displayed in open enrollment materials remain the operative terms. The regulation does not automatically lower out-of-pocket costs at the pharmacy counter, and it does not mandate refunds of pharmacy revenue to individual workers. Any eventual change in cost sharing would occur later and indirectly, if an employer uses the reported fee data to negotiate different contract terms with its PBM.

Which Employer Plans the Rule Covers

The regulation defines covered plans by size and excludes two categories of group health coverage. Under paragraph (b) of the rule, a covered plan is a group health plan offered by a specified large employer or that is a specified large plan. Two exclusions apply. Any group health plan whose benefits are all provided through an insurance policy issued by a health insurance issuer is out. So is any plan established by a sponsor that is, or is affiliated with, a drug manufacturer, distributor, wholesaler, PBM or rebate aggregator. Medicare Part D plans are not group health plans under paragraph (b).

For a plan maintained by a single employer, paragraph (m)(6) defines a specified large employer. That is an employer with an average of at least 100 employees on business days during the preceding calendar year. It must also have at least one employee on the first day of the calendar year. The count follows the employee-counting and aggregation rules of Internal Revenue Code section 4980H. Paragraph (m)(7) defines a specified large plan separately. It is a plan established or maintained by a sponsor described in 29 U.S.C. 1002(16)(B)(ii) or (iii) that averaged at least 100 participants on business days during the preceding plan year.

Smaller self-insured plans below these thresholds do not receive the detailed fee schedules mandated by this regulation. Under preamble section 1.2.1, smaller self-insured plans remain subject to the statutory exemption in section 408(b)(2)(B) of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA). Their service providers must supply generalized statutory compensation disclosures. The Department stated that it is reserving action regarding fully insured health plans.

What PBMs Must Disclose to Plan Fiduciaries

The disclosure duty falls on providers of pharmacy benefit management services and on affiliated brokerage and consulting providers. It applies when the provider reasonably expects $1,000 or more in direct or indirect compensation under a contract. The rule applies based on the functions performed, regardless of whether a company identifies itself as a pharmacy benefit manager. The disclosure must be delivered in writing to a responsible plan fiduciary with the authority to enter, extend, or renew the contract.

Under paragraphs (e) and (g) of the regulation, service providers must supply comprehensive data on services and compensation streams:

  • A detailed description of all pharmacy benefit management, consulting, and brokerage services provided to the plan.
  • Direct compensation received from the plan, reported both in the aggregate and broken down by individual service.
  • Indirect compensation, including manufacturer rebates, fees, alternative discounts and other remuneration. It is reported in the aggregate and for each formulary drug, with the amounts passed to the plan and the amounts retained by the provider.
  • Spread compensation broken down by drug National Drug Code (NDC) and pharmacy channel (retail, mail order, and specialty), including arrangements steering patients toward affiliated pharmacies.
  • Pharmacy recoupment figures, including copay claw-back compensation, defined as the difference between a copayment or coinsurance paid by a participant to a pharmacy and the actual reimbursement paid to that pharmacy.
  • Price-protection agreements, formulary placement incentives, and explanations of why reasonably available therapeutic equivalent alternatives were omitted from the formulary.
  • Net price per fill by drug and pharmacy channel, or an objective methodology that allows the plan fiduciary to verify the calculation.
  • The fiduciary status of the service provider and a formal audit right permitting the plan to review provider records.

All dollar figures must be stated as actual dollar amounts, though good-faith estimates are permitted when exact figures cannot be determined. Covered service providers must provide disclosures in a machine-readable file upon request. Providers may not restrict the plan fiduciary's use of the disclosed information, except through reasonable confidentiality agreements for third parties. They cannot prevent sharing with legal counsel or regulatory agencies.

When the Disclosures Start

The Department published the final regulation in the Federal Register on November 2, 2026, following its signing on October 28, 2026, by Daniel Aronowitz, Assistant Secretary of EBSA. The rule establishes an effective date of January 4, 2027, but sets its applicability date to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027. The Department adopted this delayed applicability date to allow employers and service providers adequate time to renegotiate existing contracts and structure future arrangements.

By simple arithmetic on the Federal Register dates, the regulation does not apply to a standard calendar-year employer plan for its 2027 plan year. A calendar-year plan's first plan year beginning on or after July 1, 2027, starts on January 1, 2028. The earliest plans subject to the regulation will be non-calendar-year plans whose coverage years begin on or shortly after July 1, 2027.

When the rule applies, initial disclosures must be provided reasonably in advance of entering, extending, or renewing a service contract. For contract extensions and renewals, the rule deems 90 calendar days in advance to be a reasonable timeframe. Ongoing disclosures must follow on a semiannual basis, or quarterly upon the covered plan's request, delivered no later than 30 calendar days following the end of each reporting period. A good-faith error does not by itself make the contract unreasonable. The provider must give the plan fiduciary the correct information as soon as practicable and within 30 calendar days of learning of it. These requirements are separate from ERISA section 726, a provision added by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026. That provision mandates its own semiannual reporting on drug spending for plan years beginning on or after August 3, 2028.

How Disclosure Could Reach Copays Indirectly

Because the regulation imposes disclosure duties rather than drug pricing mandates, any effect on worker out-of-pocket spending depends entirely on employer action. The Department says in the preamble that coinsurance in many self-insured plans is based on a drug's list price. The net cost after manufacturer rebates is lower. A manufacturer may raise a list price to protect its net price. An employee whose plan charges coinsurance then pays a percentage of that elevated list price, even when the net cost to the plan is much lower.

The Department also observed that participants frequently pay the full cost of prescription drugs until satisfying their annual deductibles. PBMs market cost-control mechanisms to employers that can result in less favorable copayments or higher coinsurance for employees.

Seeing these figures does not trigger a direct refund to the worker who paid the copayment. The rule requires that claw-backs and retained manufacturer fees be disclosed to the employer's plan fiduciary. The rule does not return that money to participants. An employer receiving this information could renegotiate its contract. It could also demand that rebates pass through to reduce cost sharing, or base coinsurance on net price instead of list price. Workers whose plans carry a deductible can read about the HSA tax advantages of a health savings account (HSA), one way to pay those costs.

What the Research Says About Costs

The Department of Labor and independent economic researchers present differing perspectives on whether transparency mandates reduce prescription spending, showing that marketwide outcomes remain uncertain. The Department expects that disclosing spread pricing, rebate retention, and formulary placement incentives will enable fiduciaries to attain more equitable patient cost sharing and broader medication access. In the regulatory impact analysis, the Department states that disclosures may help self-insured plans retain higher rebate shares, expand generic adoption, and lower out-of-pocket costs for participants filling prescriptions.

In section 8.1 of the regulatory impact discussion, the Department references an October 2024 report from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO). The CBO estimated that requiring PBMs to share drug price information with health issuers would reduce drug prices by 0.1 percent to 1.0 percent. It noted that transparency helps smaller plans negotiate. It also expected limited overall savings because many contracts already include data sharing. The Department responded: "the self-insured plans covered in this final rule is not subject to state disclosure laws, thus the final rule could have a bigger impact than CBO's estimates."

A separate CBO analysis of S. 1339, the Pharmacy Benefit Manager Reform Act, examined a different legislative proposal. CBO estimated that measure could reduce net retail drug spending by more than 0.5 percent in year one. Premiums for employment-based coverage could fall by less than 0.1 percent initially. That premium effect falls below 0.01 percent by 2034. CBO emphasized this slowed the rate of premium growth rather than causing premiums to decline.

Empirical research on state-level transparency mandates reveals mixed results. A study by Ginger Scanlon titled Prescription for Savings? Disclosure in the Drug Market examined employer-sponsored claims from 2014 to 2022. Scanlon found that inter-firm disclosures most comparable to this rule increased prescription drug costs for plans by 3.5 percent while reducing participant out-of-pocket costs by 1 percent. In monopoly drug markets, patient costs declined by 9.4 percent with no significant impact on plan costs, whereas in competitive markets, plan costs rose without significant gains for workers. Nicola Maria Fiore reached the opposite result in Can Transparency Reduce Drug Costs. Her study of Medicare Part D data found that state PBM rebate disclosure laws did not reduce drug spending, premiums, deductibles or out-of-pocket costs. The Department concluded that while disclosure mandates improve transparency, their direct effects on consumer drug spending remain uncertain.

Who This Is Not For

This regulation does not apply to all health plans, and workers in several categories of coverage will see no change in how their PBM operates. Employees covered by fully insured group health plans, where an employer purchases pre-packaged coverage from an insurance carrier, are excluded from the rule's detailed disclosures. Workers in a smaller self-insured plan are outside this rule's detailed disclosures. In that plan the employer averaged fewer than 100 employees in the preceding calendar year (counted together with related employers under IRC section 4980H), or the plan averaged fewer than 100 participants. Their plan fiduciaries receive only the generalized ERISA section 408(b)(2)(B) disclosures.

Medicare Part D plans are not group health plans under paragraph (b), so Medicare enrollees are outside this rule. People seeking information on Medicare drug benefit trends can consult the guide on Medicare Part D 2027 premiums.

Finally, this overview is an informational explainer for workers and plan participants, not legal, financial, or compliance advice for employers. Plan sponsors, fiduciaries and benefits consultants who need guidance on contract compliance or fiduciary liability under ERISA should consult the full text of the rule in the Federal Register.

What Would Change This Answer

Three things could change the conclusion that the rule does not alter worker copays.

  1. The calendar. The earliest covered plan years begin July 1, 2027, and calendar-year plans first become covered on January 1, 2028. The rule gives no calendar date for the first disclosures. Because it requires disclosure reasonably in advance of a contract's start or renewal, fiduciaries could see them earlier.
  2. An employer redesign. If a plan fiduciary finds substantial spread pricing or retained rebates and renegotiates the PBM contract to pass savings through at the pharmacy counter, employee copays or coinsurance could shift.
  3. A change to the rule. If federal agencies modify the regulatory text, publish guidance expanding coverage to fully insured plans, or alter implementation dates, the obligations described here would change.

Takeaway

For 2026 open enrollment, workers should treat the DOL PBM fee disclosure rule as regulatory background rather than a factor affecting their immediate out-of-pocket health costs. When choosing coverage for 2027, compare each employer plan option based on its published copayment schedule, annual deductible, coinsurance rates, and formulary coverage. The regulation sets no copays, applies to specified large plans that are not fully insured, and delivers its fee disclosures to plan fiduciaries instead of individual workers. The rule gives no calendar date for the first disclosures.

The economic evidence cited by the Department shows that long-term cost impacts remain uncertain. The Department expects lower plan spending and possible out-of-pocket savings. It also says the rule could have a bigger impact than CBO's 0.1 to 1.0 percent price reduction estimate. Independent researchers found conflicting results. One study showed plan costs rising 3.5 percent while worker costs fell 1 percent. Workers choosing between an HSA and a flexible spending account (FSA) can read HSA vs FSA. Ask your employer's human resources or benefits department three things about the DOL PBM fee disclosure rule: whether your health plan is self-insured, what date its plan year begins and how many employees the employer averaged last year.

Frequently asked questions

What is a PBM?

A pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) is a third-party intermediary hired by a health plan to manage its prescription drug benefit. The Department of Labor's rule defines PBM services functionally, covering providers of pharmacy benefit management services regardless of whether the person identifies itself as a PBM.

Does the DOL PBM fee disclosure rule lower my copays?

The rule does not directly lower worker copays. It imposes disclosure requirements on PBMs to report compensation to employer plan fiduciaries, but it contains no legal mandate setting or reducing copayments, coinsurance, or deductibles. Any change in worker costs depends on whether an employer renegotiates its contract after reviewing the fee disclosures.

When does the DOL PBM fee disclosure rule take effect?

The regulation takes effect on January 4, 2027, and applies to plan years beginning on or after July 1, 2027. For standard calendar-year employer plans, the first covered plan year begins on January 1, 2028, by simple arithmetic on the Federal Register dates.

Does the rule apply to my employer's drug plan?

The rule covers two kinds of group health plan. One is offered by a specified large employer, meaning an average of at least 100 employees, counted under IRC section 4980H aggregation rules. The other is a specified large plan, meaning an average of at least 100 participants. It excludes plans whose benefits are all provided through an insurance policy and plans of sponsors affiliated with drug manufacturers, distributors, wholesalers, PBMs or rebate aggregators. The Department describes the result as large, self-insured plans.

Will workers see the PBM disclosures?

No. The regulation requires PBMs to provide written disclosures to responsible employer plan fiduciaries who have the authority to contract for plan services. The operative text contains no requirement to distribute fee schedules, rebate amounts, or spread pricing data directly to plan participants.

What is spread pricing?

The rule defines spread compensation as the difference, for each drug, between what the plan pays the PBM and what the PBM pays the pharmacy. PBMs must report it to plan fiduciaries by drug National Drug Code and pharmacy channel (retail, mail order and specialty).

What is a copay claw-back?

The regulation defines copay claw-back compensation as the difference between a copayment or coinsurance amount paid to the pharmacy by a plan participant and the reimbursement to the pharmacy. Under the rule, PBMs must disclose these recoupment amounts to plan fiduciaries.

What is ERISA section 726?

ERISA section 726 is a separate statutory provision added by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026. It requires PBMs to give large self-insured plans semiannual drug spending reports, starting with plan years on or after August 3, 2028. It also requires a summary document that plans can give participants on request.
