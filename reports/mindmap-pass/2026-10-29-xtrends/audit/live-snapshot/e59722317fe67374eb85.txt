How it works The calculator tracks two paths, buying and renting, month by month. Buying costs the down payment and closing costs on day one. Each month after that, the buyer pays the mortgage payment plus property tax, insurance and maintenance, each figured as a percent of the home's current value. HOA dues are added, and so is mortgage insurance at 0.5% of the original loan a year. Mortgage insurance applies only while the down payment is under 20% and the loan is above 80% of the price. Renting costs the rent, which grows each year, plus renter's insurance. The renter keeps the down payment and closing costs and invests them on day one. Each month, whichever path costs less invests the difference. At every year-end, buying net worth is the home value after selling costs, minus the loan balance, plus the buyer's own investments. Renting net worth is the renter's investments. The break-even year is the first year-end at which buying net worth is at least renting net worth. In our internal modeling, we compared buying with renting and investing the same cash over 1 to 30 years. We then changed one assumption at a time to see which ones move the break-even year. We wrote the model twice, once in Python and once in the calculator's TypeScript code, and the two versions agreed on every scenario below.
