Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) vs WIC

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated November 4, 2026

The SNAP vs WIC difference is household grocery purchasing power versus food and nutrition benefits for eligible individual participants. A family that qualifies for both programs can receive both at the same time.

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) loads monthly grocery benefits for a whole household onto an Electronic Benefit Transfer (EBT) card.

The Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) serves pregnant, postpartum and breastfeeding participants, infants, and children under 5. It provides specific healthy foods, nutrition education, breastfeeding support and referrals.

For a household that fits both programs, the practical answer is to file two separate applications. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) Food and Nutrition Administration (FNA) publishes the federal rules for both programs.

Applications go to different offices: a state SNAP agency for SNAP and a local WIC agency for WIC.

SNAP and WIC Eligibility and Food Benefits

SNAP covers groceries for an eligible household, while WIC covers specified foods and nutrition services for each family member who fits a WIC participant category. The table below draws on four USDA pages: SNAP eligibility, what SNAP can buy, WIC eligibility and WIC benefits.

Three differences in the table decide which application a family needs:

  • Who qualifies. SNAP looks at the household, which applies in its own state and must meet income limits, resource limits and other program rules. WIC first asks whether someone is pregnant, up to 6 months postpartum, breastfeeding up to the infant's first birthday, an infant, or a child up to the fifth birthday.
  • What the card buys. A SNAP EBT card buys any food for the household at authorized stores, with listed exclusions. The WIC benefit card buys foods on the WIC list at WIC-approved retailers.
  • What comes with the food. SNAP provides a monthly grocery allotment. WIC adds one-on-one nutrition counseling, breastfeeding support and referrals to health and community services, all at no cost to the family.

A household with no pregnant, postpartum or breastfeeding member, no infant and no child under 5 has no one WIC can enroll. For that household, SNAP is the program to check.

A household with at least one of those members can check both.

Eligible Families Can Receive Both

A family enrolled in SNAP can also enroll in WIC, and SNAP receipt may already satisfy the WIC income requirement. If you or children in your care receive Medicaid, SNAP or Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), you may already be income eligible for WIC.

The USDA WIC eligibility page says those applicants can skip the WIC income chart.

Income is only one of the WIC requirements USDA lists. SNAP receipt does not settle the other three:

  1. Participant category. The person enrolling must be pregnant, postpartum, breastfeeding, an infant or a child under 5.
  2. Location. You apply through a WIC agency in your area, and some areas offer more than one way to apply, including online.
  3. Nutrition assessment. Every applicant receives a free, simple health check from WIC staff before enrollment, and WIC uses that information to personalize benefits and services.

The California WIC Program states that only staff at a local WIC office can officially confirm that a family qualifies. A current SNAP case can cover the income step of WIC screening, and local WIC staff decide the rest at the appointment.

The reverse path works differently. A WIC family that wants SNAP applies with its state SNAP agency.

SNAP vs WIC Household Rules

SNAP and WIC define a household differently, so the same family can report a different household size to each program. For SNAP, USDA groups everyone who lives together and buys and prepares meals together as one household.

Spouses and most children under age 22 who live together count as one SNAP household even if they buy and prepare meals separately.

Other SNAP rules affect separate-household status and eligibility for people receiving institutional meals. A person 60 or older who cannot buy and prepare meals separately because of a permanent disability may form a separate SNAP household with a spouse.

The separate-household exception requires the other people in the home to have income no higher than 165% of the poverty level. A person who gets most meals from an institution is normally not eligible, with exceptions for elderly and disabled people.

For WIC, the household is everyone you live with and share income and expenses with. USDA's WIC page says this includes children, people you are not related to, students away at college, and military service members away on active duty.

If someone in the household is pregnant, household size goes up by one for each expected birth.

Counting a person in the WIC household size does not make that person a WIC participant. In California, WIC completes a nutrition assessment for each qualifying family member and loads food benefits for those qualifying members onto the California WIC Card.

Household size sets the income limit in both programs. USDA sets the SNAP gross monthly income limit at 130% of poverty for applications from Oct.

1, 2026, through Sept. 30, 2027.

In the 48 contiguous states, the District of Columbia, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands, that limit is $2,345 for two people, $2,960 for three and $3,575 for four, with higher limits in Alaska and Hawaii.

Most SNAP households must also pass a net income test after deductions such as 20% of earned income, a standard deduction and dependent care costs needed for work, training or education. WIC counts household income before deductions or taxes, including wages and tips, Social Security, child support, unemployment benefits and retirement payments.

WIC leaves out loans, AmeriCorps income and certain military pay such as Basic Allowance for Housing and combat pay. The USDA WIC page shows income limits after you select a household size and state.

SNAP vs WIC Food Benefits

SNAP pays for food for the whole household, while WIC pays for a set list of healthy foods for each participant along with nutrition services. Both benefits arrive on cards that work like debit cards at approved stores: an EBT card for SNAP and a WIC benefit card for WIC.

SNAP can buy any food for the household, such as:

  • fruits and vegetables
  • meat, poultry and fish
  • dairy products
  • breads and cereals
  • snack foods and non-alcoholic beverages
  • seeds and plants that produce food for the household to eat

SNAP cannot buy:

- beer, wine, liquor, cigarettes or tobacco

  • vitamins, medicines and supplements, including any item with a Supplement Facts label
  • foods that are hot at the point of sale
  • live animals, with exceptions such as shellfish and fish removed from water
  • nonfood items such as pet food, cleaning supplies, paper products, hygiene items and cosmetics

WIC foods include fresh fruits and vegetables, baby food, milk, eggs, beans and cheese, according to USDA. The California WIC Program also lists whole grains, bread, cereal, juice, peanut butter, soy milk and tofu.

The WIC services come alongside the food:

  • Nutrition education: one-on-one counseling with personalized shopping advice, kid-friendly meal planning, tips for picky eaters, and help with food allergies and special dietary needs.
  • Breastfeeding support: connection to breastfeeding support staff for tips and resources.
  • Health and community resources: referrals to services ranging from medical and mental health care to a food bank.

Diapers fall outside both food benefits. SNAP excludes paper products, household supplies and hygiene items, and WIC benefits buy foods on the WIC list.

How to Apply Through the Right Agency

SNAP applications go to the state SNAP agency where you live, and WIC applications go to a local WIC agency, so a family applying for both contacts two offices. USDA's FNA does not process SNAP applications or have access to case information.

The SNAP application runs through these steps:

  1. Find your state in the SNAP State Directory of Resources. Each state has its own form, and some states accept applications online.
  2. Contact the state agency through its website, a local SNAP office or the state's toll-free SNAP hotline. If you cannot apply yourself, you can name an authorized representative in writing to apply and interview for you.
  3. Complete an eligibility interview, usually by phone or in person, and give proof of the information on your application.
  4. Watch for a decision notice, which in most cases arrives within 30 days. Approved benefits count back to the date you applied.

Some households can receive SNAP within 7 days of applying. USDA gives examples such as a household with less than $100 in liquid resources and $150 in monthly gross income.

If you disagree with a SNAP decision, you can request a fair hearing within 90 days, by phone, in writing or in person at the local SNAP office.

USDA's SNAP eligibility page notes that the Working Families Tax Cuts law changes certain SNAP rules, including work requirements and non-citizen eligibility, and that USDA is updating the page. For details, contact your state agency through the SNAP State Directory of Resources.

The WIC application follows a different path:

  1. Use the WIC Eligibility Tool or the WIC locator on the same USDA WIC eligibility page to reach a local WIC agency. Some WIC agencies are run by Tribes.
  2. Ask the local WIC office which documents to bring. The California WIC Program may ask for identification, proof of address and income, proof of pregnancy, and medical forms from a health care provider.
  3. Bring the infant or child if staff ask, and complete the nutrition assessment for each qualifying member.
  4. In California, WIC food benefits load onto the California WIC Card at that first appointment and can be used right away.

The Next Step for Your Household

A household with a pregnant, postpartum or breastfeeding member, an infant, or a child under 5 should check both programs, because enrolling in one does not replace the other. Current SNAP receipt may satisfy the WIC income test.

Local WIC staff still confirm the participant category, the local agency match and the nutrition assessment.

A household with no WIC category member has no WIC application to file. For that household, the food-assistance step from this comparison is the SNAP application in its own state.

A change in family makeup can switch the next step. USDA's WIC eligibility page notes that having a baby, becoming a foster parent or a change in income can affect WIC eligibility, so a new pregnancy or birth puts WIC on the list.

WIC no longer applies when the youngest child turns 5 and no one else fits a WIC category. SNAP eligibility continues to follow its own household and income rules.

Once benefits start, plan household food spending with SNAP and WIC entered as separate lines. You can also keep food assistance separate in your budget.

Check SNAP vs WIC requirements with your state SNAP agency and local WIC agency, then apply for each program your household fits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are WIC and SNAP the same thing?

No. SNAP gives an eligible household a monthly grocery allotment on an EBT card.

WIC gives pregnant, postpartum and breastfeeding participants, infants and children under 5 specific healthy foods. WIC also provides nutrition education, breastfeeding support and referrals.

They have separate eligibility rules, separate household definitions and separate applications, and a family can be enrolled in both.

Does SNAP or WIC cover diapers?

Neither food benefit covers diapers. USDA's list of items SNAP cannot buy includes paper products, household supplies and hygiene items.

WIC benefits buy foods on the WIC list, although WIC staff can refer families to community-based services.

What does SNAP and WIC cover?

SNAP covers any food for the household, including fruits, vegetables, meat, dairy, bread, snacks and seeds or plants that grow food. It excludes alcohol, tobacco, hot foods, supplements and nonfood items.

WIC covers listed foods such as fresh fruits and vegetables, baby food, milk, eggs, beans and cheese. WIC also includes nutrition counseling, breastfeeding support and referrals to health and community services at no cost.

Are food stamps the same as SNAP?

Yes. USAGov says the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) was previously known as food stamps. Each month, SNAP benefits are added to an Electronic Benefits Transfer (EBT) card.

Can I receive SNAP and WIC together?

Yes, a family that meets both programs' rules can receive SNAP and WIC at the same time. USDA says SNAP participants may already be income eligible for WIC.

The person enrolling in WIC still has to fit a WIC participant category, apply through a local WIC agency and complete a nutrition assessment with WIC staff.
