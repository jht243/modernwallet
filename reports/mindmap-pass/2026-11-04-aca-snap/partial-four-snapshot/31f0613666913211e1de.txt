The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) describes the Marketplace as the place to find private health insurance options, buy a plan, and get help with premiums and out-of-pocket costs for those who are eligible. Monthly premiums for a Marketplace plan go directly to the insurance company, not to the Marketplace. Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) provide free or low-cost coverage to eligible low-income adults, families and children, pregnant women, the elderly, and people with disabilities, according to HealthCare.gov. Medicaid programs must follow federal guidelines, but eligible income levels, coverage and costs differ from state to state. Some Medicaid programs pay for care directly, and others use private insurance companies to provide Medicaid coverage. Benefits also follow different rules. Medicaid benefits differ by state, though HealthCare.gov says all states provide comprehensive coverage. Every Marketplace plan covers 10 benefit categories, including prescription drugs, emergency services, hospitalization, laboratory services, and mental health and substance use disorder services, along with pre-existing conditions and free preventive services from in-network providers. Both paths count as qualifying health coverage. HealthCare.gov lists Marketplace plans, Medicaid and CHIP among the coverage types that meet the law's minimum requirements for comprehensive health coverage. Advertise With Us → Eligibility Determines Premium Savings Eligibility for full-benefit Medicaid generally prevents a person from receiving federal premium savings on a Marketplace plan. The IRS lists this as a premium tax credit condition: the person enrolled in Marketplace coverage must not be eligible for coverage through a government program such as Medicaid or CHIP. HealthCare.gov puts it plainly: "If you qualify for Medicaid, you aren't eligible for savings on a Marketplace plan." Declining Medicaid does not restore the savings. A person eligible for full Medicaid or CHIP who keeps a Marketplace plan pays full price for the premium and for covered services. A child may also lose CHIP eligibility if the family keeps the Marketplace plan. Limited-benefit Medicaid is one exception. Some limited types of Medicaid pay only for family planning, treatment of an emergency medical condition, or tuberculosis services. When a Medicaid program does not count as minimum essential coverage, the person may be eligible for premium tax credits and other Marketplace savings if they qualify based on income and other criteria. HealthCare.gov's Medicaid minimum essential coverage lookup shows, by state, whether a given Medicaid program counts and what action to take. People with limited Medicaid can also fill out a Marketplace application to find out whether they qualify for full-benefit Medicaid or a Marketplace plan with savings. Medicaid status is one of several federal tests. The IRS lists these premium tax credit requirements: Household income generally from 100 percent to 400 percent of the federal poverty level (FPL) for the family size. A filing status other than married filing separately, apart from a special rule for certain victims of domestic abuse or spousal abandonment. Not being claimable as a dependent by another person. No access to affordable employer coverage that provides minimum value for the person enrolled. Payment of the share of premiums not covered by advance credit payments. A timing rule applies when status changes mid-year. If the Marketplace found a person ineligible for Medicaid at enrollment and approved advance credit payments, the IRS generally treats that person as not eligible for Medicaid for the rest of the plan year, even if Medicaid later approves coverage. The IRS still tells anyone who may be enrolled in both Medicaid and a Marketplace plan with advance credit payments to contact the Marketplace immediately. Household Income Is Not the Only Test Household income alone does not decide whether a person gets Medicaid or Marketplace savings, because state rules and personal categories change the result. HealthCare.gov says each state's coverage options consider income, household size, family status such as pregnancy or caring for young children, disability, age and other factors. Some states expanded Medicaid to cover adults below a certain income level, and others did not. HealthCare.gov advises applying for Medicaid even when income looks too high. That advice applies especially to people who have children, are pregnant or have a disability. The federal poverty level is a yearly income measure used for both programs. HealthCare.gov's federal poverty level table lists 2026 amounts of $15,960 for one person and $33,000 for a family of four, with higher amounts in Alaska and Hawaii. Under its general bands, income below 138 percent of FPL may qualify for Medicaid in an expansion state, and income below 100 percent "probably won't qualify for savings on a Marketplace plan" but may qualify for Medicaid. Both programs measure income with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). HealthCare.gov's income guide defines MAGI as adjusted gross income plus untaxed foreign income, non-taxable Social Security benefits and tax-exempt interest, without Supplemental Security Income. Marketplace savings are based on expected household income for the coverage year rather than last year's income. Self-employed applicants count the income they expect to make from the business after expenses. For income that is hard to predict, HealthCare.gov says to report current income, review the yearly estimate it shows, and update the application as changes happen. A wrong estimate carries a tax cost: for tax years after 2025, the IRS applies no repayment cap, so the full amount of advance credit payments above the allowed credit is added to the tax bill. Members of one household can end up in different programs. HealthCare.gov notes that children may qualify for CHIP even when a parent does not qualify for Medicaid. In many cases, a parent who qualifies for Marketplace savings will have children who qualify for Medicaid or CHIP. Applying Through the Marketplace or Your State A household can apply for Medicaid through a Marketplace application or directly through its state Medicaid agency, and either route can lead to the other program. On a Marketplace application, if anyone in the household looks eligible for Medicaid or CHIP, the Marketplace securely sends that information to the state agency, which then contacts the household about enrollment. The same Marketplace application shows whether anyone qualifies for cost savings on a Marketplace plan. The direct route starts with the state. HealthCare.gov's Medicaid and CHIP coverage page lists contact information for each state's Medicaid agency. A state denial feeds back into the Marketplace. In most cases, the state securely sends the person's contact information to the Marketplace, which mails a letter about Marketplace coverage. A denied applicant can apply and enroll in Marketplace coverage without waiting for that letter. Enrollment timing differs between the two: **Medicaid and CHIP:** apply any time of year, and coverage can start immediately. **Marketplace Open Enrollment:** November 1 to January 15. Enroll by December 15 for coverage that starts January 1, or by January 15 for coverage that starts February 1. **Marketplace outside Open Enrollment:** a qualifying life change opens a Special Enrollment Period. Medicaid may also help pay for medical care from the last 3 months, even before enrollment. Payment depends on the family's income and medical expenses at the time care was received. Marketplace applications can be filed online, by phone, with help from someone in the community, through an agent or broker, through certified enrollment partner websites, or on paper. HealthCare.gov offers a checklist of what to gather before applying on its Marketplace quick guide . Rules to Check for 2027 Coverage Two rule changes shape 2027 coverage decisions: a Medicaid work requirement in some states and immigration-status rules for full Medicaid and Marketplace savings. Both notices appear on HealthCare.gov's Medicaid and CHIP coverage page and its Marketplace quick guide . Starting January 1, 2027, adults in states that provide Medicaid based on income alone may need to meet a new monthly work requirement to qualify for Medicaid or keep it. HealthCare.gov states that a person who lost or was denied Medicaid for not meeting the state's work requirement may be able to get Marketplace coverage but won't qualify for savings to lower Marketplace plan costs. After an ordinary Medicaid denial, by contrast, HealthCare.gov says a person may qualify for the premium tax credit or other cost savings. The reason for a Medicaid denial therefore matters for the Marketplace budget. A household comparing plans after a work-requirement denial should expect full-price premiums when estimating monthly costs. HealthCare.gov says that due to a law change, only people with these immigration statuses are now eligible for full Medicaid or CHIP coverage: Lawful Permanent Resident (LPR, Green Card holder) Cuban or Haitian entrant Compact of Free Association (COFA) migrant Depending on the state, lawfully residing pregnant women and children may also be eligible for full Medicaid or CHIP. For Marketplace plans, starting January 1, 2027, only certain immigration statuses qualify for savings. People with other statuses may still be eligible for Marketplace coverage without savings. For 2027 Marketplace coverage starting January 1, the enrollment deadline is December 15. Medicaid applications remain open year-round. The Next Step Before Choosing a Plan The next step is an official eligibility result for each person in the household, from a Marketplace application on HealthCare.gov or from the state Medicaid agency. That result answers the ACA vs Medicaid question person by person. Full-benefit Medicaid eligibility removes Marketplace savings, while limited-benefit Medicaid, an ordinary Medicaid denial and a work-requirement denial each lead to a different savings answer. Neither option is the better choice for every household, because eligibility rules decide which coverage comes with financial help. If the result points to a Marketplace plan, compare each plan's monthly premium and provider network in the Marketplace before enrolling. A Marketplace plan starts only after the first premium is paid, if there is one. Keep the current plan until replacement coverage is confirmed. If Medicaid is approved while a Marketplace plan is active, HealthCare.gov says to end the Marketplace plan when Medicaid starts, unless you tell the state agency you want to keep both. Once the premium amount is known, plan the monthly premium alongside other bills, and if income changes month to month, budget insurance with irregular income before you update the Marketplace income estimate. Frequently Asked Questions What is Obamacare insurance? The Affordable Care Act is a law, and Marketplace plans are private health insurance bought through the Health Insurance Marketplace. HealthCare.gov uses the terms Marketplace and Marketplace plan. Every Marketplace plan covers 10 benefit categories, including prescription drugs, emergency services and hospitalization, and covers pre-existing conditions. Depending on expected household income, a buyer may qualify for a premium tax credit that lowers the monthly premium, plus extra savings on deductibles, copayments and coinsurance. Medicaid is a separate public program run jointly by the federal government and the states. What is a coverage gap in health insurance? The cited HealthCare.gov pages do not define a coverage gap, but they describe situations where a person may get neither Medicaid nor Marketplace savings. HealthCare.gov says that in a state that has not expanded Medicaid, a person who is not eligible may have fewer coverage options and, depending on income, might not qualify for savings on a private plan. Its poverty-level guidance adds that income below 100 percent of FPL probably won't qualify for Marketplace savings. Starting in 2027, a work-requirement denial can produce a similar result: Marketplace access without savings. HealthCare.gov still advises applying for Medicaid in non-expansion states, because each state considers factors beyond income. What disqualifies you for Medicaid? No single factor disqualifies everyone, because each state sets its own Medicaid income levels and categories within federal guidelines. Income too high for Medicaid or an immigration status outside the eligible list can block full coverage, and living in a state that has not expanded Medicaid can mean fewer coverage options. Starting January 1, 2027, adults in states that cover people based on income alone may lose or be denied Medicaid for not meeting a monthly work requirement. HealthCare.gov still recommends applying when income looks too high, especially for people with children, a pregnancy or a disability. Is ACA or Medicaid better? Neither is better for every household, because eligibility rules decide which option comes with financial help. A person eligible for full-benefit Medicaid gets free or low-cost coverage there, and would pay full price for a Marketplace plan. A person denied Medicaid for ordinary reasons may qualify for a premium tax credit on a Marketplace plan, while a 2027 work-requirement denial allows Marketplace coverage without savings. Medicaid benefits differ by state but are comprehensive in all states, and every Marketplace plan covers the same 10 benefit categories. Compare actual premiums and provider networks after the eligibility result arrives. Can you have Medicaid and Marketplace insurance at the same time? Yes, a person can keep both, but full-benefit Medicaid means paying full price for the Marketplace plan premium and covered services. HealthCare.gov says to tell the state agency if you want to keep both, and notes that a child may no longer qualify for CHIP if the family keeps the Marketplace plan. Limited-benefit Medicaid is different: a person may still qualify for Marketplace savings based on income and other factors. The IRS tells anyone who may be enrolled in both Medicaid and a Marketplace plan with advance credit payments to contact the Marketplace immediately.
