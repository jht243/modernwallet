CLOSED FACT LIST:
- Eight states tax some Social Security benefits in tax year 2026: Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont. West Virginia fully exempts benefits from tax year 2026. Every other state with an income tax exempts benefits, and states with no broad income tax do not tax them. (CT Office of Legislative Research report 2026-R-0106, July 30, 2026, agrees on the eight.)
- Colorado: age 65+ may subtract all federally taxable Social Security. Ages 55 to 64 may subtract all of it if AGI is $75,000 or less (single) or $95,000 or less (joint); above that, the combined pension and Social Security subtraction is capped at $20,000. Rule applies 2025 onward. [HB24-1142 signed act]
- Connecticut: fully exempt if federal AGI is under $75,000 (single or married filing separately) or under $100,000 (joint, head of household, qualifying widow(er)); above that, no more than 25% of benefits is taxable by CT. [CT DRS; CT OLR 2026-R-0106]
- Minnesota: full subtraction below an AGI threshold, reduced by 10% for each $4,000 of AGI over it ($2,000 married filing separately). 2026 thresholds: $110,780 joint, $86,410 single or head of household, $55,390 married filing separately. (2025: $108,320 / $84,490 / $54,160.) An alternative method allows up to $5,840 joint / $4,560 single or HOH / $2,920 MFS. [MN DOR]
- Montana: taxes the portion of benefits included in federal taxable income (from tax year 2024). Taxpayers 65+ get a senior subtraction: $5,500 ($11,000 if both joint filers are 65+) for 2024, adjusted for inflation from 2025 — state that it is inflation-adjusted and do NOT state a 2025 or 2026 figure. [MT DOR 2024 Form 2 instructions]
- New Mexico: fully exempt with income under $100,000 (single), $150,000 (joint, surviving spouse, head of household) or $75,000 (married filing separately); no exemption above those levels. Since 2022. [NM TRD]
- Rhode Island: benefits are exempt only if the taxpayer has reached Social Security full retirement age AND federal AGI is below an inflation-adjusted threshold; above it, no exemption. 2025 threshold for single/HOH/MFS: $107,000. The 2026 thresholds were not yet published as of this writing — tell readers to check the RI Division of Taxation. Do NOT state a joint figure (the Division's documents conflict). A 2026 budget law removes the full-retirement-age requirement starting tax year 2027. [RI ADV 2025-22; RI 2026 legislative summary]
- Utah: benefits are included in income but a nonrefundable Social Security credit equals the state tax rate times the benefits included in Utah taxable income; the credit shrinks by 2.5 cents per dollar of modified AGI above $54,000 (single), $90,000 (joint or HOH) or $45,000 (married filing separately). 2025 onward. Do NOT state Utah's tax rate. [Utah Code 59-10-1042]
- Vermont: single/HOH/MFS/surviving spouse — full exclusion at AGI of $55,000 or less, proportional phase-out between $55,000 and $65,000, none at $65,000 or more. Joint — full at $70,000 or less, phase-out $70,000 to $80,000, none at $80,000 or more. Act 71 of 2025, retroactive to January 1, 2025. [VT legislature; VT Tax]
- West Virginia: Social Security is 100% exempt from state tax starting tax year 2026. In 2025, filers above $50,000 AGI (single) or $100,000 (joint) could exempt only 65%. [WV Tax]
- California excludes Social Security benefits from taxable income. [CA FTB]
- Pennsylvania lists Social Security payments as income never taxable. [PA DOR]
- New York lets taxpayers subtract Social Security included in federal AGI. [NY DTF]
- New Jersey lists Social Security as exempt income. [NJ Taxation]
- Massachusetts excludes Social Security from gross income. [MA FY27 tax expenditure budget]
- State rules change often; readers should confirm with their state revenue department before filing.
- DO NOT USE: RI 2026 thresholds; Montana 2025/2026 subtraction amounts; Utah tax rate; Tax Foundation list (stale, 2023).
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a threshold, rate, or state. Do NOT list states with no income tax by name (not on the fact list). Do NOT cite the Tax Foundation.
