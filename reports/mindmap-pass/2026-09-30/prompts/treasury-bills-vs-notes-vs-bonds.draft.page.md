# Treasury Bills vs Notes vs Bonds: Which Fits Your Timeline?

Treasury bills mature in 4 to 52 weeks and sell at a discount, while Treasury notes and bonds run from 2 to 30 years and pay cash interest every six months. At ModernWallet, we evaluate cash and fixed-income options by matching maturities to withdrawal goals so savers avoid forced early sales. All three options share direct U.S. government backing, a $100 purchase minimum, and an exemption from state income tax.

The choice comes down to when you need your money back and how much price risk you can accept if you sell early. Shorter terms shield you from secondary market price drops, while longer terms lock in a coupon rate across market cycles.

## Treasury Bills vs Notes vs Bonds at a Glance

Treasury bills, notes, and bonds differ in two ways: how long they run and how they pay you. [Treasury bills](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/) cover terms from 4 to 52 weeks. They are zero-coupon instruments sold at a discount or at par, paying their full face value when the bill reaches its maturity date.

[Treasury notes](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-notes/) span intermediate durations of 2, 3, 5, 7, or 10 years. [Treasury bonds](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bonds/) extend across long horizons of 20 or 30 years. Both notes and bonds pay a fixed interest rate every six months until maturity, when the government returns the full par principal.

All three instruments carry the direct backing of the full faith and credit of the U.S. government. They require a modest $100 entry point through [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/) or a brokerage account. ## How Each Security Pays Returns to Investors

Treasury bills generate income through an upfront purchase discount, whereas Treasury notes and bonds pay periodic cash coupons twice per year. When you purchase a bill, you generally pay less than its stated face value. For instance, using made-up round numbers, you might buy a $1,000 bill for $970, and the U.S. Treasury delivers the full $1,000 to your account upon maturity.

Treasury notes and bonds operate under fixed semiannual payment schedules. When you buy a 10-year note or a 30-year bond at auction, you lock in a stated coupon rate. Every six months, the government deposits that interest payment directly into your designated bank account or brokerage sweep fund.

This structural distinction changes cash flow planning. A retiree who wants a payment every six months might prefer note coupons. A saver closing on a home in eight months can buy a 26-week bill and have the full face value back before closing.

## Maturity Horizons and Secondary Market Price Risk

Holding any Treasury security to maturity guarantees return of your full face value plus all stated interest, but selling before maturity exposes you to market price risk. As detailed by [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds), when market interest rates rise, existing bonds trading on the secondary market fall in price. That dynamic means you might have to sell at a discount if you cannot wait for the maturity date.

Maturity length dictates the severity of price swings. A 4-week or 13-week Treasury bill experiences minimal price fluctuation because its maturity date sits around the corner. A bill sold early through a broker usually fetches close to what you paid, because little time remains before it pays face value.

A 10-year note or 30-year bond has years of fixed coupons left, so its market price falls further than a bill's when interest rates rise. ## Reinvestment and Rate Locks

Treasury bills offer flexible automated rolling for cash management, whereas notes and bonds deliver long-term certainty by locking your interest rate for years. Savers using short bills face reinvestment risk, which occurs when a bill matures and newly issued replacement bills offer lower yields than the original purchase.

TreasuryDirect provides built-in tools to automate continuous bill purchases. You can schedule automatic reinvestment at the time of purchase or up to four business days before maturity. A bill automatically reinvests into the same term length, such as a 52-week bill rolling once or a 4-week bill reinvesting up to 25 consecutive times.

Notes and bonds eliminate the need to constantly monitor auction schedules. Once you purchase a 7-year note or a 20-year bond, your interest rate remains locked for the entire multi-year holding period. You avoid the operational overhead of rolling positions, though you surrender the chance to capture higher short-term rates if market yields rise.

## Taxes on Bills, Notes, and Bonds

Bills, notes, and bonds get the same tax treatment: the IRS taxes the interest as ordinary income, and states and cities cannot tax it. Under [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), interest earned on all marketable Treasury securities is exempt from state and local income taxes. For savers residing in high-income-tax states, this exemption provides a noticeable advantage over fully taxable corporate paper or standard bank deposits.

Reporting follows standardized tax documentation. Custodians and TreasuryDirect report your annual Treasury earnings on [Form 1099-INT](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) box 3, titled Interest on U.S. Savings Bonds and Treasury Obligations. You transfer this figure directly to your federal return while subtracting it from state taxable income calculations.

The timing of your tax liability varies slightly based on how returns are paid. Notes and bonds produce taxable coupon income in the calendar year payments are received. A discount Treasury bill bought in late autumn that matures the following February generates interest income reportable on your taxes in the year of maturity. ## Purchasing Channels and Early Liquidity Rules

Individual investors can buy Treasury securities directly from the federal government or through commercial brokerage firms. TreasuryDirect permits non-competitive bids only, guaranteeing you receive the security in the amount requested at the final auction clearing yield. To learn the exact account setup steps, consult our tutorial on [How to Buy Treasury Bills](/guides/how-to-buy-treasury-bills/).

A brokerage account adds two things TreasuryDirect does not offer: competitive bids and the ability to sell before maturity. Online Treasury trades at [Charles Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/pricing) and [Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/trading/commissions-margin-rates) carry zero-dollar commission fees for auction and secondary orders, though representative-assisted orders carry service fees of $25 at Schwab and $19.95 at Fidelity.

If you hold a Treasury security in TreasuryDirect and decide to sell prior to maturity, you must hold the asset for at least 45 days before you can transfer it to a bank, broker, or dealer to execute the sale. ## Other Treasury Types: TIPS and Floating Rate Notes

The U.S. Department of the Treasury issues two additional marketable debt instruments designed for specialized inflation and interest rate environments. [Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tips/) are issued in terms of 5, 10, or 30 years. TIPS pay a fixed rate twice yearly on a principal value that adjusts upward or downward with inflation, and at maturity, the government pays the greater of the adjusted or original principal.

[Floating Rate Notes (FRNs)](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/floating-rate-notes/) address short-term interest rate shifts over a two-year maturity window. An FRN pays interest quarterly, with payments tied to the discount rate of the most recent 13-week Treasury bill auction plus an established spread.

These specialized structures serve different portfolio objectives. Investors who want inflation protection often compare TIPS against non-marketable savings bonds. ## How to Choose Based on Your Time Horizon

Matching your target spending date to the maturity of your Treasury security prevents forced sales and locks in expected returns. If you have an upcoming expense due in six months, such as tuition or property taxes, a 26-week bill matches your cash flow needs without exposing your principal to secondary market swings.

Intermediate goals spanning several years call for Treasury notes. A saver setting aside cash for a home down payment four years from now can buy a 3-year note, which pays a coupon every six months and returns the principal a year before the purchase. That position locks in a steady semiannual coupon payment while establishing an exact return date for the capital.

Long-term wealth preservation and permanent fixed-income portfolios benefit most from Treasury bonds. Holding 20-year or 30-year paper establishes guaranteed income decades into the future, providing balance against equities. Assess your required access date before placing an order to select the correct Treasury vehicle.
