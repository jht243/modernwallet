How it works The calculator follows Worksheet 1 of Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Publication 915 and starts with provisional income. Provisional income is all your other income plus tax-exempt interest plus half your benefits. Other income covers pensions, individual retirement account (IRA) withdrawals, wages, interest and dividends. For benefits, enter box 5 of your Social Security Administration (SSA) statement, Form SSA-1099, with both spouses' benefits on a joint return, or 12 times your monthly benefit before Medicare. Tax-exempt interest from municipal bonds counts too. Adding $5,000 of it to the joint example below raises provisional income to $56,000. Taxable benefits then go from $11,950 to $16,200, or 38.6% of the benefits. The calculator compares provisional income with a base amount and an upper amount for your filing status. The base amount is $25,000 if you are single, head of household, a qualifying surviving spouse, or married filing separately and lived apart all year. It is $32,000 for married filing jointly and $0 for married filing separately if you lived with your spouse at any time in the year. At or below the base amount, none of your benefits is taxable. Between the base amount and $34,000 ($44,000 joint), the taxable part is the smaller of half the benefits or half the income above the base. Above $34,000 ($44,000 joint), the taxable part is the smaller of 85% of the benefits or a second figure. That second figure is 85% of the income above the upper amount, plus the smaller of half the benefits or $4,500 ($6,000 joint). Take the joint couple with $42,000 of benefits and $30,000 of other income. Half the benefits is $21,000, so provisional income is $51,000. That is above the $44,000 joint upper amount, so the 85% tier applies. The income above $44,000 is $7,000. Taking 85% of that gives $5,950. The smaller of half the benefits or $6,000 is $6,000, and adding it brings the total to $11,950. That total is under the cap of 85% of benefits, or $35,700, so $11,950 is taxable. The base and upper amounts are written into Title 26 of the United States Code, section 86, and are not indexed to inflation. Benefits rise with each cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) while those thresholds stay fixed, so a raise can push provisional income over a threshold and make more of your benefits taxable. The deduction for people 65 and older is also separate. It is worth up to $6,000 per person for tax years 2025 through 2028. It lowers taxable income but does not change how much of your benefits counts as taxable, as the senior deduction guide explains. The calculator stops at the taxable amount and does not compute the tax you owe on it. If you want your total federal tax bill, this result is only the first step. The taxable part is added to your other income and taxed at your normal rates, and the tax bracket calculator shows the rate on that amount. Married filing separately while living with your spouse has a $0 base amount, so benefits are taxable from the first dollar of provisional income. The calculator leaves out worksheet adjustments such as student loan interest, the foreign earned income exclusion and lump-sum back payments for earlier years. For returns with those items, the IRS worksheet in tax software gives the exact figure.
