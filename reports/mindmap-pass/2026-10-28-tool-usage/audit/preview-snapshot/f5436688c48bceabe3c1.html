Salary Inflation Calculator

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated October 28, 2026

A raise of about 3.7% keeps your salary even with this year's prices. That figure is the price change over the last 12 months. To see whether earlier raises kept up, multiply your old salary by the consumer price index for September 2026 and divide by the index for the year you earned it. Pay at or above that result kept up with prices. Pay below it lost buying power, even if the paycheck grew. The salary inflation calculator below runs that math with Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data back to 1913.

For example, take a salary of $52,000 earned in 2010. In September 2026 dollars, that pay equals about $80,350. Prices are up 54.5% since 2010. Against a $78,000 salary today, the worker is $2,350 behind, which is 2.9% less buying power. A 3.7% raise on that $78,000 adds $2,915 and keeps pay even with the last year of prices.

How it works

The calculator turns an old salary into September 2026 dollars with one price index from the BLS. It uses the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), U.S. city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted, series CUUR0000SA0. The adjusted salary equals the old salary times the CPI-U for September 2026, divided by the CPI-U annual average for the year you earned it. The September 2026 index is 336.94, the latest month in the data. The 2010 annual average is 218.056. In our internal calculation, we applied the BLS CPI-U annual averages for every year from 1913 to 2025 to sample salaries and checked each result in two separate programs. Run through that formula, a $52,000 salary from 2010 becomes $80,350. Both programs agreed on that result and on every other scenario on this page. These scenarios are calculations. They are not survey data or client cases, and you can enter the same numbers in the calculator above to check them.

The result shows whether today's pay is behind, even with, or ahead of prices. The gap equals today's salary minus the adjusted salary. In the default case, $78,000 minus $80,350 leaves a $2,350 shortfall. That is 2.9% less buying power than the 2010 salary had. The calculator also shows two yearly rates. The average raise is the yearly growth in dollar pay, about 2.5% in this case. The real change takes inflation out, and here it is a loss of about 0.2% a year. Both rates treat an annual average as mid-year, so 2010 to September 2026 counts as about 16.2 years. A raise in dollars can still be a pay cut in buying power. Take $60,000 earned in 2020, which equals $78,113 today. A $64,000 salary now is a 6.7% raise in dollars. Measured against prices, the same $64,000 is 18.1% behind, or $14,113 short. The start year matters because prices rose at very different speeds. From 1979 to 1981, the CPI-U annual average rose more than 10% every year. The peak was 13.5% in 1980. In 2009 the average fell 0.4%, so pay held flat that year did not lose buying power. The CPI-U average rose 4.7% in 2021. The next year it rose 8.0%. In 2023 it rose another 4.1%. Those jumps show up in the salary figures: a $50,000 salary from 2019 equals $65,897 today. The same $50,000 from 2022 equals $57,566. Those start years sit three years apart, yet the adjusted figures differ by $8,331. Go back to 1980 and $50,000 equals $204,454.

The keep-pace raise equals today's salary times the 12-month CPI-U change. From September 2025 to September 2026, the CPI-U rose about 3.7%. On a $78,000 salary, that raise is $2,915. The new salary would be $80,915. Use 3.7% as the minimum to ask for in a raise conversation. A smaller raise leaves your new pay buying less than your current pay bought a year earlier. Bring the CPI-adjusted figure too, so you can show how far your pay sits behind or ahead of the old salary in today's dollars.

The CPI-U is a national average for all items, so it can miss changes in your own costs. If your rent or city costs rose faster than the national index, the raise you need is larger than the calculator shows. If they rose slower, it is smaller. For the same reason, this calculator is the wrong tool for comparing pay across two cities, because a national index does not measure local costs. Count a promotion or a new job separately from an inflation raise, since pay for a bigger role is a different gain from pay that keeps up with prices. Both salaries are gross annual pay, and taxes, benefits and local cost of living are left out. Taxes take part of any raise, so look up your rate with the tax bracket calculator. A different index, such as the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W), gives slightly different figures. The figures also move each time BLS publishes a new month, and September 2026 is the latest month used here. For an amount that is not a salary, such as savings or a past price, use the inflation calculator.

Compare today's pay with the CPI-adjusted figure the calculator shows. Matching it means the same buying power as the old salary. Pay above it is a real raise. Pay below it is a pay cut in real terms, even if the paycheck grew. Asking for at least the latest 12-month change, about 3.7%, keeps pace with prices this year. To see where a raise would go each month, plan it with the household budget calculator. This calculator is not career or financial advice. Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my salary with inflation?

Multiply your old salary by the CPI-U for September 2026, then divide by the CPI-U annual average for the year you earned it. For a 2010 salary, that means multiplying by 336.94 and dividing by 218.056. On $52,000, the result is about $80,350 in today's dollars. Compare that figure with what you earn now.

How much should my salary go up with inflation?

To keep pace with the last 12 months of prices, your salary should rise about 3.7%. That is the CPI-U change from September 2025 to September 2026. On a $78,000 salary, the raise is $2,915. To cover earlier years, compare today's pay with the CPI-adjusted figure for the year you earned the old salary.

How much is a $100,000 salary in 2000 worth today?

A $100,000 salary from 2000 equals about $195,668 today. Prices are up 95.7% since 2000. Today here means September 2026, the latest month in the BLS data. Half that salary, $50,000 from 2000, equals $97,834.

How much is a $50,000 salary in 1996 worth today?

A $50,000 salary from 1996 equals about $107,374 today. That is more than double the original pay. The 1996 CPI-U annual average used for this result is 156.9. For a later reference point, $50,000 from 2005 equals $86,262.

What is an $80,000 salary in 1990 worth today?

An $80,000 salary from 1990 equals about $206,237 today. Prices are up 157.8% since 1990. The 1990 CPI-U annual average is 130.7. A $50,000 salary from that year equals $128,898.

Did my salary keep up with inflation if I got a raise every year?

Not always, because yearly raises only kept up if they added up to at least the price rise since your start year. Take pay of $60,000 in 2020. At $64,000 today, that pay is up 6.7% in dollars but 18.1% behind prices. It would need to reach $78,113 to match the 2020 salary's buying power.

Which inflation measure does this calculator use?

The calculator uses the CPI-U, U.S. city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted, published by BLS as series CUUR0000SA0. A different index, such as the CPI-W, gives slightly different figures. BLS did not publish an October 2025 index. The 12-month change runs from September 2025 to September 2026.

Why do inflation calculators start in 1913?

The BLS CPI-U data used here begins in 1913, so that is the earliest year in the calculator. The 1913 annual average is 9.9. By that index, $1 in 1913 equals about $34.03 today.
