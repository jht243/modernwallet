Paying half your payment every two weeks adds up to 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 full payments instead of 12. That one extra payment a year cuts years off the loan and lowers the interest you pay. You can get almost the same saving by adding 1/12 of your payment each month yourself, with no plan and no fee. The biweekly mortgage calculator below shows your interest saved, how much sooner the loan ends, and your net saving after any plan fees. For example, take a $300,000 loan at 6.5% with 30 years left. The monthly payment is $1,896.20, so the biweekly payment is $948.10 every two weeks. Paid biweekly, the loan ends in about 24 years and 2 months. Total interest drops from $382,633 to $294,512, a saving of $88,122. Adding $158 to each monthly payment saves $87,256, only $866 less, with no fee. On this loan, any plan fee above $866 leaves you with less than the free 1/12 approach. How it works Biweekly payments save money because they add one extra monthly payment each year. A year has 52 weeks, so paying every two weeks makes 26 half-payments. Those 26 half-payments equal 13 full monthly payments instead of 12. In our internal calculation, we ran a range of balances, rates and terms through three payment schedules: monthly, biweekly, and monthly plus 1/12. We ran each schedule in two separate programs and compared the interest each one pays. Both programs agreed on every scenario on this page, and you can enter the same numbers in the calculator above to get the same results. These are calculated scenarios. They do not test any lender's plan and do not come from client cases. The default case is a $300,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years. Paid biweekly, it takes 628 half-payments and saves $88,122 of interest. The calculator runs three schedules on your loan and shows the payoff time and total interest for each one. The monthly payment uses this formula: balance × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n). In that formula, r is the annual rate ÷ 12 and n is the number of months left. The biweekly schedule pays half that amount every two weeks, 26 times a year, and charges interest at the annual rate ÷ 26 for each two-week period. The third schedule pays the monthly payment plus one twelfth of it each month. On the default loan, the monthly schedule makes 360 payments and pays $382,633 of interest. The 1/12 schedule adds $158 a month. It ends in 290 months and saves $87,256. The model assumes a fixed-rate loan and counts principal and interest only, leaving out taxes, insurance and private mortgage insurance (PMI). Because it assumes a fixed rate, its results do not fit an adjustable-rate loan. To see your monthly payment, use the mortgage payment calculator . Plan fees can wipe out the gap between a paid biweekly plan and the free 1/12 approach. The calculator does not know any lender's fees, so enter the setup fee and per-payment fee from your plan's terms. It adds the setup fee to the per-payment fee times the number of biweekly payments. With a $395 setup fee and $2.50 per payment, the default loan pays $1,965 in fees. The net saving falls to $86,157, which is $1,099 less than adding 1/12 yourself. With $9.95 per payment and no setup fee, fees total $6,249. The net saving drops to $81,873. That is $5,383 less than the $87,256 you save with the free 1/12 approach. Some mortgages also charge a fee for paying off all or part of the loan early, so read the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explainer on prepayment penalties and your loan documents before you pay extra. Biweekly payments save less when fewer years are left on the loan. Take a $250,000 loan at 6.5% with 15 years left. Paid biweekly, it saves $19,887 and ends about 1 year and 10 months sooner. A $150,000 loan at 6% with 10 years left gains even less. It saves $5,717 and ends 1 year sooner. The calculator also assumes the servicer applies each half-payment when it arrives. A servicer that holds half-payments until a full payment builds up saves you less than the calculator shows. At a 0% rate, biweekly payments save no interest at all. On a $300,000 balance, they only shorten the loan. It ends in about 27 years and 8 months instead of 30. Frequently asked questions Is a biweekly mortgage payment worth it? It is worth it when the plan is free or its fees are small. Take a $300,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years. Biweekly payments save $88,122 of interest, and adding 1/12 a month saves $87,256 with no fee. On that loan, a plan fee above $866 leaves you worse off than the free approach. Do biweekly mortgage payments really work? Yes, as long as the servicer applies each half-payment when it arrives. Paying every two weeks makes 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 full payments instead of 12. A servicer that holds half-payments until a full payment builds up saves you less than the calculator shows. Are biweekly mortgage payments better than one extra payment a year? Biweekly payments add one extra monthly payment over a year. The calculator does not model a single lump payment once a year, so it compares biweekly with adding 1/12 of the payment each month. On the default loan, biweekly saves $88,122 and the 1/12 approach saves $87,256. You can make extra payments yourself without a paid plan, as long as your loan has no prepayment penalty. How many years do biweekly payments take off a 30-year mortgage? It depends on your rate and loan. Take the default loan at 6.5%. Paid biweekly, it ends after about 24 years and 2 months. In our $200,000 at 4% scenario, the loan ends about 4 years and 1 month sooner, and in our $400,000 at 7% scenario it ends about 6 years and 3 months sooner. Does paying biweekly lower my monthly payment? No, each biweekly payment is half your monthly payment, and you make more payments over a year. On the default loan, $948.10 every two weeks replaces $1,896.20 a month. To lower the payment itself with a lump sum, use the mortgage recast calculator . What is the difference between biweekly and bi-monthly payments? Biweekly means every two weeks, which makes 26 half-payments a year. Bi-monthly means twice a month, which makes 24 half-payments a year. Those 24 half-payments equal 12 full payments, so a bi-monthly schedule adds no extra payment. Only the biweekly schedule makes the 13th payment. Should I pay a fee for a biweekly payment plan? Only if the fee is small next to the interest saved. On the default loan, a $395 setup fee plus $2.50 per payment costs $1,965. A $9.95 fee per payment costs $6,249 and leaves less saving than adding 1/12 yourself for free. What should I check before switching to biweekly payments? Look at your loan documents for a prepayment penalty, a fee some mortgages charge for paying off all or part of the loan early ( CFPB: What is a prepayment penalty? ). Ask your servicer whether it applies each half-payment when it arrives or holds it until a full payment builds up.
