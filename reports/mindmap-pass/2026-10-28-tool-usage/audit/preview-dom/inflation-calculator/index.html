Inflation Calculator

By Jonathan Velez, Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated October 28, 2026

An inflation calculator shows what an amount of money will cost or buy in the future at an average annual inflation rate you choose. Enter an amount in today's dollars, a number of years, and an inflation rate in the calculator above. You can also add an optional annual return if the money is invested. To see what an amount from any year since 1913 is worth today, switch to "From a past year", which uses Consumer Price Index (CPI) data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).

For example, enter $10,000, a 3% inflation rate, and 10 years. The same purchases then cost $13,439. Held unchanged, the $10,000 buys only what $7,441 buys today. That is a 25.6% loss of buying power. Kept as cash, the money has a real return of -2.91% a year. Add a 5% annual return and the real return after inflation becomes 1.94% a year. The balance grows to $16,289, which is worth $12,121 in today's dollars.

How it works

The calculator takes an amount in today's dollars and raises the price of what it buys by your inflation rate, compounded once a year for the number of years you enter. The formula is future cost = amount x (1 + inflation)^years. Enter $10,000, a 3% rate, and 10 years. The same purchases then cost $13,439 at the end of the period.

The "From a past year" mode uses the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), series CUUR0000SA0, which covers all items for the U.S. city average and is not seasonally adjusted. To find today's value, the calculator multiplies your amount by the CPI-U for September 2026, the latest month in the data (336.94), and divides by the CPI-U annual average for the year you choose. For $10,000 in 2010, it divides by that year's annual average of 218.056. The result is $15,452 in September 2026 dollars, with prices up 54.5%. The calculator treats an annual average as mid-year, so that gap spans about 16.2 years and works out to about 2.7% a year on average. In our internal calculation, we checked this example in two separate programs, and you can enter $10,000 and 2010 above to see the same numbers.

Buying power uses the same math in reverse. The calculator divides your amount by (1 + inflation)^years to show what it will buy later, measured in today's dollars. With the same inputs, $10,000 held unchanged buys what $7,441 buys today, a 25.6% loss of buying power. The calculator also reports how long buying power takes to halve, using ln 2 / ln(1 + inflation), which comes to about 23.4 years at 3%.

Real return is what money earns each year after inflation is taken out, and the calculator computes it as (1 + nominal return) / (1 + inflation) - 1. Held as cash at 0%, the money has a real return of -2.91% a year against 3% inflation. At a 5% annual return, the real return rises to 1.94% a year. The $10,000 then grows to $16,289 in future dollars, which is worth $12,121 in today's dollars. Compare that $12,121 with the $7,441 the same money buys after sitting as cash.

The inflation rate is an input because one rate cannot describe every year or every budget. Real inflation changes from year to year, and the prices of different things rise at different speeds. The 3% in the tool is a placeholder for you to replace, so run several rates and watch how far the future cost and real return move. Measured price changes come from the CPI program the BLS publishes, and the past-year mode uses that data.

The calculator assumes one steady inflation rate in every year of the period. It does not model taxes or fees on the return, so the ending balance and the real return are both shown before those costs. To work out a return figure first, use the compound interest calculator, the high-yield savings calculator, or the CD calculator, then enter that rate here to see what it earns after inflation.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the effect of inflation on money?

Multiply the amount by (1 + inflation rate) raised to the number of years to get the future cost of the same purchases. Divide the amount by that same factor to see what it buys later in today's dollars. For example, enter $10,000 and 3% for 10 years in the calculator above. The future cost is $13,439, and the unchanged amount buys what $7,441 buys today.

What is the formula for future cost with inflation?

Future cost equals the amount times (1 + inflation) raised to the number of years. In the formula the rate is written as a decimal, so 3% becomes 0.03, while the calculator takes it as a percent. Applied to $10,000 over 10 years, the formula gives $13,439.

What inflation rate should I enter?

The calculator does not pick a rate for you. The 3% in the tool is a placeholder, and you can test several rates to see how much the future cost and buying power change. For measured price changes, use the Consumer Price Index data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

What does real return mean?

Real return is the yearly growth of your money after inflation is removed. The calculator uses (1 + nominal return) / (1 + inflation) - 1. A 5% return with 3% inflation gives a real return of 1.94% a year. For a second explanation, read the real rate of return entry on Investor.gov, the investor site of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).

Why does money held as cash lose value?

Cash in this calculator earns 0%, so prices rise each year while the balance stays the same. At 3% inflation, cash has a real return of -2.91% a year. Over 10 years, $10,000 held as cash buys what $7,441 buys today. That is 25.6% less buying power.

How long does it take for inflation to halve my buying power?

At 3% inflation, buying power halves in about 23.4 years. The calculator finds this with ln 2 / ln(1 + inflation).

Does this calculator use historical CPI data?

Yes, the "From a past year" mode uses historical Consumer Price Index (CPI) data. It pulls from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), U.S. city average, all items, series CUUR0000SA0. You can pick any year from 1913 to 2025, and the latest month in the data is September 2026. The default future-cost mode still uses the rate you enter and has no forecast built in.

Can I use this for retirement planning?

It covers one part of retirement planning. The calculator shows how a fixed amount loses buying power over a retirement-length horizon at the rate you enter, and the optional return shows the ending balance in today's dollars. It does not model taxes, fees, withdrawals, or a rate that changes over time. For a full projection, use the retirement planning pages.
