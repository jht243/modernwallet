## 10% APR Cap Scenario

A 10% interest cap on credit cards is not law, and the cap remains a proposal. Per the [Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12861.html) (CRS) report updated March 10, 2026 and the text of S.381 on [govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s381is/html/BILLS-119s381is.htm), S.381 and H.R.1944 are proposals in the 119th Congress that would cap credit card annual percentage rates (APRs) at 10% until January 1, 2031. S.381 was introduced February 4, 2025 and referred to the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs, and committee status after those records is not confirmed here.

In our internal modeling, we compared one billing cycle at 24% and at 10% on the $5,000 example used above.

| APR | One 30-Day Cycle | One Year, Flat Balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 24% | $98.63 | $1,200.00 |
| 10% | $41.10 | $500.00 |
| Difference | $57.53 | $700.00 |

These numbers assume simple interest on a flat $5,000 balance, a 30-day cycle in a 365-day year, no payments made during the cycle, no new fees added, and the card staying open at 10%. To evaluate your current rate against common ranges, read our guide on [what counts as a good APR for a credit card](/guides/good-apr-for-credit-card/).

The CRS report describes several practical tradeoffs if a cap takes effect:

* Access to credit from traditional sources could be affected.
* Banks may choose to limit credit products or add auxiliary fees.
* Borrowers may shift to higher-APR sources outside banking.
* Some economists state that reduced credit availability could benefit some consumers.

A single new fee of $57.53 or more per cycle would wipe out the entire monthly interest savings on this $5,000 balance, and losing access to an existing card line could cost more than the rate reduction saves.

Based on our internal modeling, a cardholder who keeps a $5,000 balance at 10% with no added charges would save about $57.53 per 30-day cycle. If card issuers react by adding auxiliary fees or providing less access, that calculated benefit quickly disappears. Per CRS, the proposed limits apply generally to consumer cards rather than business credit lines.

This scenario does not apply to anyone who pays their full statement balance each month, because carrying no balance generates zero interest charges. Passage of a federal bill with altered caps, different fee rules, or another expiration date would change these numbers.
