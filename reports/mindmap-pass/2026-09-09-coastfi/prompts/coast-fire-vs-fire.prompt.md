### Definitions
- Full FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early): you already have enough invested to live off
  withdrawals now. You stop working.
- Coast FIRE: your current savings, left untouched with zero further contributions, will compound
  into your full FIRE number by your target retirement age. You keep earning; you stop saving for
  retirement. You do NOT retire at the moment you reach it.
- Coast FIRE is also written "Coast FI". Same milestone, two spellings.

### The formulas
- FIRE number = desired annual retirement spending / safe withdrawal rate. At 4% that is 25 times
  annual spending.
- Coast number today = FIRE number / (1 + r)^t, where r is the expected annual return and t is the
  years until the target retirement age.

### THE CENTRAL WORKED COMPARISON. Use these figures exactly; do not recompute or extend them.
A 35-year-old who wants $60,000 a year in retirement and plans to retire at 65, assuming a 7%
expected annual return and a 4% safe withdrawal rate:
- Full FIRE number: $1,500,000 ($60,000 / 0.04).
- Coast FIRE number: $197,051 ($1,500,000 / 1.07^30, where 1.07^30 = 7.6123).
- The Coast FIRE number is therefore about 13% of the full FIRE number for this saver, roughly
  one seventh.
The gap narrows as the saver ages, because there are fewer compounding years left. Same $60,000
spending goal, same 7% return, same retirement at 65:
- At age 25 the coast number is $100,171 against the same $1,500,000 full FIRE number.
- At age 45 it is $387,629.
- At age 55 it is $762,524, roughly half the full number.

### The variants
- Lean FIRE is commonly modelled at 20 times annual expenses (about a 5% withdrawal rate).
- Standard FIRE is 25 times annual expenses (a 4% withdrawal rate).
- Fat FIRE is 33 times annual expenses or more.
- These multiples change the FIRE number, and therefore change the coast number that discounts
  back from it. Lean, standard and fat are different TARGETS; coast is a different MILESTONE on
  the way to whichever target you picked. They are not alternatives to each other. This
  distinction is the single most useful thing this page can explain.
- Barista FIRE means stepping back to part-time work now, often specifically for health benefits,
  and beginning portfolio withdrawals immediately. Because it funds withdrawals now instead of
  leaving the portfolio untouched, it requires a meaningfully larger portfolio than Coast FIRE.
  This site already has a dedicated page on that comparison; mention it in one sentence and link,
  do not re-explain it.

### The 4% basis and its limits
- The 4% figure comes from the 1998 Trinity Study, which tested withdrawal rates across historical
  30-year market periods.
- A retirement longer than 30 years exceeds what that research modelled; a 3% to 3.5% withdrawal
  rate is more appropriate for a 35-year-plus horizon. A lower withdrawal rate raises the FIRE
  number and therefore raises the coast number too.

### The real risks, which belong in this comparison
- Sequence-of-returns risk hits the two paths differently. A full FIRE retiree is withdrawing
  during a downturn, which permanently shrinks the base. A coaster is not withdrawing, but has
  also stopped contributing, so there is no new money buying the dip.
- Health insurance is a full FIRE problem long before it is a Coast FIRE problem: a coaster
  usually still has employer coverage because they are still working. A full FIRE retiree
  leaving before Medicare at 65 must budget for individual coverage.
- Coast FIRE's own risk is assumption drift. It is a bet on a return rate and a spending target
  held for decades. Full FIRE, by contrast, is verified at the moment you stop: the money is
  either there or it is not.
