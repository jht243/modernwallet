By Jonathan Velez , Personal Finance Writer & Editor · Reviewed against primary sources · Updated October 17, 2026 With a Roth 401(k), you contribute after-tax dollars and make tax-free withdrawals in retirement. A Traditional 401(k), by contrast, reduces your taxable income today. The right choice depends on whether your tax rate is higher now or in retirement. Pick Roth 401(k) if you're early in your career or expect your tax rate to be higher in retirement (very common given potential Social Security income and tax law uncertainty). Pick Traditional 401(k) in your peak earning years when today's tax rate is likely your highest, and you're confident you'll be in a lower bracket in retirement. Tax math: which version saves more? The decision is a tax-rate bet. If your rate is 22% today and 12% in retirement, every Traditional 401(k) dollar saves 22 cents in tax now and costs only 12 cents when withdrawn — a 10-cent savings per dollar. Roth 401(k) would have been the wrong choice in that scenario. Flip it: if you're 28, earning $55,000, and in the 12% bracket today — but expect a 22% rate in retirement with Social Security, pension income, and required withdrawals from pre-tax accounts — the Roth 401(k) saves 10 cents per dollar. The honest answer is nobody knows for certain what future tax rates will be. That's why many financial planners recommend diversifying: split contributions between Roth and Traditional to hedge the uncertainty. The employer match goes in pre-tax by default Employer matching contributions go into the pre-tax (Traditional) side of a 401(k) by default, even if all of your own deferrals go to the Roth 401(k) option. A 100% Roth 401(k) saver can therefore build a Traditional balance from the match. Plan for that mixed tax treatment. Since the SECURE 2.0 Act (2022), a plan may let you take your match as Roth. Section 604 makes this optional, so employers are not required to offer it. Only vested match money qualifies, and a Roth match is not excludable from your gross income. To see whether your plan offers a Roth match, check the plan document or ask HR. The IRS sets out the rule in Notice 2024-2, Part L . Roth 401(k) and required minimum distributions Roth 401(k) balances have no required minimum distributions (RMDs) while the owner is alive. The IRS RMD FAQ states that RMD rules do not apply to Roth IRAs or designated Roth accounts, such as a Roth 401(k), during the owner's lifetime. Traditional 401(k) balances follow different rules. RMDs on those generally begin at age 73. In a plan that holds both kinds of money, RMDs apply to the traditional balance and not to the Roth balance. Beneficiaries are a separate case. Anyone who inherits a Roth 401(k) or a Roth IRA is still subject to RMD rules. Rolling a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA is optional and is not required to avoid lifetime RMDs. For the full side-by-side, see Roth IRA vs Roth 401(k) . When to use both at the same time If your plan allows split contributions, you can put some dollars in Traditional and some in Roth each year. In 2026, as long as the total doesn't exceed $24,500, you can split it any way you like. A practical approach: contribute enough Traditional 401(k) to stay in your current bracket's lower band, then put additional contributions in Roth. For example, if you're $5,000 below the top of the 22% bracket, put $5,000 Traditional (saving at 22%) and the rest Roth. Frequently asked questions Should I choose Roth or Traditional 401(k)? Choose Roth 401(k) if you're early in your career and expect a higher tax rate in retirement. Choose Traditional 401(k) in your peak earning years when your current rate is high. Many financial planners recommend splitting contributions to hedge tax uncertainty. For more detail, see 401(k) vs Roth IRA . Can I have both a Roth and Traditional 401(k) in the same plan? Yes, if your employer's plan allows it. You can split your contributions between Roth and Traditional in any proportion, as long as the combined total doesn't exceed the annual limit ($24,500 in 2026). For more detail, see 401(k) Contribution Limits for 2026 . Do Roth 401(k) contributions reduce my taxable income? No. Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax money, so they don't reduce your current year's taxable income. Traditional 401(k) contributions do reduce your taxable income. Is the Roth 401(k) better than a Roth IRA? The Roth 401(k) has no income limits and a much higher contribution limit ($24,500 vs $7,500 in 2026). The Roth IRA often has more investment options. Most people should use both: max the Roth IRA, then use the Roth 401(k) for additional savings. When can I withdraw Roth 401(k) money tax-free? Roth 401(k) money comes out fully tax-free only as a qualified distribution, which generally means a 5-year participation period has passed and the withdrawal happens on or after age 59½, after death, or due to disability. The 5-year period starts on the first day of the taxable year of your first designated Roth contribution and ends after five consecutive taxable years. In a nonqualified withdrawal, only the contribution share is tax-free: in the IRS example , $5,000 taken from an account holding $9,400 of contributions and $600 of earnings splits into $4,700 tax-free and $300 of taxable earnings.
