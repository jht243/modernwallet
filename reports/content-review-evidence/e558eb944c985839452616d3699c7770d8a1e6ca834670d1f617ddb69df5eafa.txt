Medicaid Work Requirements 2027: Will You Lose Coverage? By ModernWallet Research Desk · Reviewed by Jonathan Teplitsky, MBA · Updated November 25, 2026 · How we review You will not automatically lose Medicaid coverage on January 1, 2027, if your state adopts the federal hold-harmless option once CMS approves it, or if you qualify for a statutory exemption. Under federal guidance from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), states adopting the transition relief may pause denials and terminations for community engagement noncompliance through June 30, 2027. Beneficiaries must still complete scheduled renewals and report qualifying hours or exemption circumstances to keep their files in good standing. You will not automatically lose Medicaid on January 1, 2027, for missing the community engagement benchmark, but your immediate protection depends directly on whether your state chooses to pause disenrollments. Under section 71119 of Public Law 119-21, often called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation, the federal baseline requires states to implement community engagement rules no later than January 1, 2027. However, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services ( CMS ) released State Medicaid Director Letter SMD# 26-006 on November 13, 2026, granting states an optional hold-harmless transition window. In a state that formally elects this hold-harmless period and has CMS approval, the Medicaid agency can suspend all eligibility denials and disenrollments based on community engagement noncompliance through June 30, 2027. This suspension applies to initial applications submitted and redetermination renewals initiated through that mid-year date. Even in participating states, administrators must still collect information and assess whether members meet the engagement rules. Enrollees must continue submitting paperwork, but coverage cannot be terminated strictly for failing the work requirement during this window. In states that do not adopt the hold-harmless policy, the federal requirement applies from January 1, 2027 unless the state has another CMS-approved mitigation, so ask your state Medicaid agency. If an individual in an enforcement state fails to demonstrate required hours or an exemption, the state agency cannot terminate benefits without warning. Federal regulations under the CMS Interim Final Rule (IFR) require the agency to send a formal noncompliance notice. Beneficiaries then receive a window of 30 calendar days to submit proof of qualifying activities or claim an exemption before disenrollment takes effect, as the CMS interim rule describes it. States implement the notice, and the Oct 29, 2026 court ruling partly vacated parts of that rule. Who the Rule Covers and Who Is Exempt The federal community engagement mandate applies strictly to non-pregnant adults ages 19 through 64 who are enrolled in the Medicaid expansion adult group or certain section 1115 minimum essential coverage demonstrations across 43 states and the District of Columbia. United States territories are not subject to the mandate, and individuals entitled to or enrolled in Medicare are excluded from these requirements. To satisfy the standard, covered adults must log at least 80 hours per month of qualifying activities or earn wages equal to at least 80 times the federal minimum wage, which equals $580 per month in 2026. Qualifying activities can be combined to reach the 80-hour monthly threshold. Approved activities include work, community service, a work program, or half-time education, or a combination. When checking compliance, state agencies review past records: applicants must demonstrate compliance for at least one month prior to application, while existing enrollees are checked across one or more months between redetermination cycles as determined by individual state rules. Federal policy provides explicit statutory exemptions that protect vulnerable groups from disenrollment. According to official CMS documentation, you are exempt from the 80-hour monthly rule if you meet any of the following criteria: * You are pregnant or postpartum. * You are a parent or primary caretaker responsible for a child age 13 or younger, or you care for an individual with a recognized disability. * You are a former foster youth. * You are an American Indian or Alaska Native individual. * You are a totally disabled veteran. * You are deemed medically frail. * You are in substance use disorder treatment. * You are currently complying with existing work requirements under the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) program or the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). * You are currently incarcerated in a correctional facility. If you qualify for any exemption, you must notify your state agency to ensure your administrative record is coded appropriately. What a Hold-Harmless State Can and Cannot Do State Medicaid Director Letter SMD# 26-006 establishes clear boundaries regarding state authority during the transition. A state that elects the hold-harmless option receives federal protection against CMS enforcement actions once CMS approves the period in its mitigation plan if it chooses to halt adverse eligibility decisions. Under this mechanism, the state Medicaid program can suspend both application denials and mid-year disenrollments for individuals who do not show compliance with community engagement standards. States have the flexibility to implement this relief for the entire period through June 30, 2027, or choose a shorter calendar duration that suits their technical rollout schedules. Electing the hold-harmless policy does not allow a state to pause normal Medicaid verification standards. States are expressly required to build reporting mechanisms, gather activity documentation, and evaluate whether applicants qualify for exemptions. States intending to use the hold-harmless option starting January 1, 2027, were asked by CMS to submit notifications by December 11, 2026, through their formal community engagement mitigation plans. The hold-harmless period applies only to community engagement standards under section 1902(xx)(1) of the Social Security Act and 42 CFR 435.559. A state may not suspend denials or disenrollments for other eligibility factors, such as age or household income. As verified on November 23, 2026, by the Oregon Health Authority (OHA), Oregon announced on Nov 23, 2026 that it will opt into the hold-harmless period, and it is the one state this page verified from a primary source. Other states may also opt in, so check your own state Medicaid agency. OHA said no Oregon Health Plan member will face coverage denials or disenrollment for community engagement noncompliance through June 30, 2027. Oregon state officials noted that more than 600,000 members received informational notices in September 2026, and Governor Tina Kotek's office said an initial analysis projected that up to a third of affected Oregonians would be likely to lose coverage even if they qualify. Implementation also occurs alongside ongoing legal developments: on October 29, 2026, the federal district court in Massachusetts v. Oz partially vacated specific CMS regulatory limits regarding medical condition exemptions, which CMS formally acknowledged in footnote three of SMD# 26-006. What to Do Now Take these steps whether or not your state has adopted the hold harmless period. Ask your state Medicaid agency whether your state has adopted the hold harmless period and when it ends. Decide how you will document compliance before your next renewal. If you work, collect pay stubs, earnings statements or tax records showing at least $580 in gross monthly income or 80 logged hours. If you meet an exemption, gather your records now. Watch your mail for notices. In a state that has not delayed enforcement, a noncompliance notice gives you 30 calendar days to respond. If your income rises above Medicaid limits or you lose coverage, compare Marketplace plans. Our Takeaway on the 2027 Work Requirement For most Medicaid expansion adults ages 19 through 64, the January 1, 2027 deadline poses the most immediate risk if your state has not adopted CMS hold-harmless relief and you cannot demonstrate 80 monthly activity hours, $580 in gross earnings, or an approved exemption category. In a state with an approved hold harmless period, such as Oregon, which announced it will opt in, the Medicaid agency cannot deny applications or disenroll enrollees for noncompliance before July 1, 2027, but your legal duty to report hours and complete redetermination forms remains active. This landscape would shift if CMS alters national transition guidelines, if additional states withdraw or submit mitigation plans, or if federal litigation in Massachusetts v. Oz expands court-ordered injunctions against enforcement. Frequently Asked Questions Does missing the work requirement cause immediate cancellation on January 1? No. In states that adopt the CMS hold-harmless transition with CMS approval, disenrollment for community engagement noncompliance is paused through June 30, 2027. In non-participating states, federal rules require the agency to send a written noncompliance notice providing 30 calendar days to submit documentation or claim an exemption before terminating coverage. Can I combine part-time employment with school or volunteering? Yes. Federal guidelines let you combine qualifying activities, such as work, community service, a work program or half-time education, to reach the 80-hour monthly minimum. What earnings level satisfies the Medicaid community engagement requirement without tracking hours? Earning gross monthly wages equal to at least 80 times the federal minimum wage satisfies the requirement without logging separate hours. In 2026, earning at least $580 per month fulfills the standard. Does the hold-harmless delay protect me if my income exceeds Medicaid limits? No. The hold harmless period in CMS State Medicaid Director Letter SMD# 26-006 applies only to community engagement. States may not suspend denials or disenrollments for other eligibility factors, such as age or household income. How do I confirm whether my state opted into the hold-harmless extension? Check with your state Medicaid agency.
