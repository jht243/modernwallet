A loan from a qualified employer plan is treated as a distribution under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(1)(A) unless it stays within the amount limit, is repaid within 5 years (longer if it buys your main home), and is repaid in substantially level payments at least quarterly. The first limit is $50,000, reduced by the excess (if any) of your highest outstanding loan balance during the one-year period ending the day before the new loan is made over your outstanding balance on the day it is made. The second limit is the greater of one-half of your vested balance (your nonforfeitable accrued benefit under the plan) or $10,000. These three illustrations of the formula assume no prior loans. They are examples, not advice. A participant with a $15,000 vested balance: one-half of the balance is $7,500. Because the formula uses the greater of one-half of the vested balance or $10,000, the second limit is $10,000. Comparing $10,000 to the $50,000 maximum, the lesser figure is $10,000. The borrowing cap is $10,000. A participant with a $60,000 vested balance: one-half of the vested balance is $30,000. That figure is greater than $10,000. Comparing $30,000 to the $50,000 ceiling, the lesser figure is $30,000. The borrowing cap is $30,000. A participant with a $200,000 vested balance: one-half of the vested balance is $100,000. That figure exceeds $10,000, but federal law caps the maximum borrowing amount at the lesser of that figure or $50,000. The borrowing cap is $50,000. Yes, a solo 401(k) can lend to its owner when the plan document provides for loans, and the same 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2) limits apply. Lending between a plan and a disqualified person is generally a prohibited transaction under 26 U.S.C. § 4975(c)(1)(B) . The statute provides an exception for a loan to a disqualified person who is a plan participant or beneficiary, but a provision allowing loans is only one part of that exception. Under § 4975(d)(1), the loan must meet all five conditions: The loan must be available to all such participants or beneficiaries on a reasonably equivalent basis. Highly compensated employees must not be able to borrow larger amounts than other employees can borrow. The loan must follow specific provisions for loans set out in the plan. The loan must bear a reasonable rate of interest. The loan must be adequately secured. The loan exception is unavailable for lending to an owner of an individual retirement plan under § 4975(f)(6). A SEP, SIMPLE, or traditional individual retirement account (IRA) therefore cannot lend to its owner. That restriction does not categorically bar a solo 401(k) loan. Nothing in these conditions provides a workaround for borrowing from an IRA. For a different borrowing source, read 401(k) loan vs HELOC . An owner is generally a key employee because 26 U.S.C. § 416(i)(1)(A) includes a 5-percent owner in that category. Under § 72(p)(3), interest on the owner's own plan loan is not deductible during a period when the owner is a key employee. The plan document and its administrator determine whether a given loan meets the loan conditions. Run your balance through the 401(k) loan calculator . Repayment Rules and the Principal Residence Exception Federal law requires a 401(k) loan to be repaid within 5 years under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(B)(i) . The terms of the loan agreement must require full repayment within that 5-year window from the date the loan is made. A loan term longer than 5 years fails the repayment rule unless the home purchase exception applies. The 5-year limit does not apply to a loan used to acquire a dwelling unit that is to be used within a reasonable time as your principal residence ( 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(B)(ii) ). That is determined when the loan is made. The longer term for such a loan is set by your plan, so ask your plan administrator. In addition to the term limit, 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(C) requires substantially level amortization of the loan over its entire term. Payments must be made not less frequently than quarterly. What Happens If a 401(k) Loan Is Not Repaid A plan loan is treated as having been received as a distribution under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(1)(A) if it does not meet the amount, 5-year term, and quarterly level-payment rules. A distribution can bring federal income tax. In addition to regular income tax, an early distribution can trigger an extra tax penalty. According to Internal Revenue Service (IRS) Topic 558 , the law imposes a 10% additional tax on certain early distributions from retirement plans. Early distributions are generally distributions received before age 59½, though exceptions exist as detailed by the IRS. If a 401(k) loan balance is treated as a distribution to a borrower who has not reached age 59½, that borrower can face both income tax and the 10% additional tax on the unpaid amount. A separate rollover rule applies to a qualified plan loan offset amount. Under 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(3)(A) , the ordinary rollover window to deposit distributed funds into an eligible retirement plan or Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is 60 days after the distribution is received. However, for a qualified plan loan offset amount, 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(3)(C)(i) provides an extended rollover deadline. You can complete a rollover of a qualified plan loan offset amount up to the due date, including tax extensions, for filing your federal income tax return for the tax year in which the amount is treated as distributed from the plan. Whether an offset qualifies requires specific analysis, so ask your plan administrator or a professional tax advisor about your situation. Plan-Specific Rules and Interest Deductibility Restrictions Federal statute authorizes employer plans to offer loans, but it does not require any plan to do so. Whether an employer retirement plan permits borrowing at all is decided entirely by the employer's plan document. If your plan does permit loans, the plan document also determines the application fees, ongoing administrative charges, the specific interest rate charged, the maximum number of loans you may have outstanding at one time, and the exact default procedures. Interest on a plan loan is not deductible in some cases, such as any period when you are a key employee or when the loan is secured by certain elective deferrals ( 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(3)(A) ). Ask your plan administrator or a tax professional whether either applies to you. Frequently asked questions How much can I borrow from my 401(k)? Under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(A), your loan plus all other outstanding plan loans must not exceed the lesser of $50,000 (reduced by any excess of your highest loan balance in the past year over your balance on the day the loan is made) or the greater of $10,000 or half your vested balance. With no prior loans, a $15,000 vested balance gives a $10,000 cap, $60,000 gives $30,000, and $200,000 gives $50,000. How long do I have to repay a 401(k) loan? Under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(B)(i), you must repay a 401(k) loan within 5 years from the date the loan is made. Repayment must occur through substantially level payments made at least quarterly over the term of the loan under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(C). The 5-year limit does not apply when loan proceeds are used to acquire a dwelling unit that is to be used within a reasonable time as your principal residence. Do I pay taxes on a 401(k) loan? Under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(1)(A), a loan from a qualified plan is treated as a distribution unless it meets the amount limit, the 5-year term, and the level quarterly payment rules. A distribution can bring regular income tax. If you are under age 59½, it can also bring the 10% additional tax described in IRS Topic 558, though exceptions exist. What happens to a 401(k) loan if I leave my job? What happens when you leave depends on your plan's default rules, so ask your plan administrator. If an unpaid balance is treated as a distribution, the ordinary rollover window is 60 days after the distribution is received (26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(3)(A)). For a qualified plan loan offset amount, a rollover can be made up to the due date, including extensions, for filing the tax return for the year the amount is treated as distributed (26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(3)(C)(i)). Ask your plan administrator or a tax professional whether your offset qualifies. Can I borrow from my 401(k) to buy a house? Yes, if your employer plan allows it. Under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(2)(B)(ii), the 5-year repayment limit does not apply to a loan used to acquire a dwelling unit that is to be used within a reasonable time as your principal residence. The amount limit still applies, and repayment must still be made in substantially level payments at least quarterly. Can I take a loan from a solo 401(k)? Yes, you can take a loan from a solo 401(k) if its plan document provides for loans, and the same limits under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p) apply. Avoiding a prohibited transaction under 26 U.S.C. § 4975(d)(1) takes more than a plan provision. The loan must also meet the other conditions in that section, and the plan document and its administrator decide whether a given loan does. An IRA-based plan, including a SEP, SIMPLE or traditional IRA, cannot lend to its owner under 26 U.S.C. § 4975(f)(6). For the full list of conditions, see the section "Can a Solo 401(k) Lend to Its Owner?" above. How does a solo 401(k) loan work? A solo 401(k) loan is possible if the plan document provides for loans. It must follow the same amount, term, and payment rules under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p) and meet the other conditions in 26 U.S.C. § 4975(d)(1). A loan provision in the plan document alone does not satisfy those conditions. An owner is generally a key employee under 26 U.S.C. § 416(i)(1)(A)(ii). Under 26 U.S.C. § 72(p)(3), interest on the owner's own plan loan is not deductible. Use the 401(k) loan calculator to see the limit and payment for your balance.
