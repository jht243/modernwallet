In a tenancy in common, each co-owner holds a distinct, separate legal stake in the real estate. When an owner dies, that individual share does not transfer to the remaining co-owners. Instead, the decedent's ownership interest passes into their estate, transferring according to the instructions laid out in a last will and testament, a living trust, or the default distribution rules of state intestacy law. Joint tenancy with right of survivorship works differently. When an owner who holds real property as a joint tenant with right of survivorship dies, the interest passes to the surviving joint tenants. State law establishes the default presumption for all real estate conveyances and determines whether survivorship applies. As one clear state example, the Florida Senate publishes Section 689.15 of the 2024 Florida Statutes, which explicitly abolishes the common-law doctrine of survivorship as a baseline presumption. Under Florida law, a conveyance made to two or more individuals automatically creates a tenancy in common unless the deed expressly states a right of survivorship. Federal tax law then applies its own rules to the property once an owner dies. Neither form is a tax winner. Under IRC Section 1014, property inherited from a decedent receives an adjusted tax basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death. However, how that adjustment applies across the whole property depends heavily on whether the co-owners were married, how much financial consideration each owner provided, and whether the parcel is situated in a community property jurisdiction. Tenants in Common Joint Tenancy Right of Survivorship No right of survivorship exists. When a tenant in common dies, that owner's property share passes through an estate by will or intestacy law. Full right of survivorship applies. When a joint tenant dies, that owner's interest transfers directly to the surviving joint owners. Default Classification Under State Law Under the Florida Senate 2024 Florida Statutes Section 689.15, a conveyance to two or more people creates a tenancy in common unless the deed expressly provides for survivorship. Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS) requires specific express wording in the deed under Section 689.15 of the 2024 Florida Statutes. Spouse Co-Ownership Rules Section 689.15 of the 2024 Florida Statutes treats tenancy by the entirety, which spouses hold, separately from a tenancy in common. 26 U.S.C. 2040 names tenancy by the entirety as held by the decedent and the decedent's spouse. Under Florida Section 689.15, tenants by the entirety become tenants in common upon dissolution of marriage. Federal Estate Gross Value Inclusion for Spouses The inclusion rules the sources here cover (26 U.S.C. 2040) address joint interests with survivorship. Under 26 U.S.C. 2040(b) at the Legal Information Institute at Cornell Law School (LII), a qualified joint interest held solely by spouses includes exactly one-half of the property value in the gross estate. Federal Estate Gross Value Inclusion for Non-Spouses 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(1) treats property acquired by bequest, devise, or inheritance as acquired from the decedent. Under 26 U.S.C. 2040(a), the full property value enters the gross estate except any portion proven to have originally belonged to the surviving owner. Federal Tax Basis Adjustment at Death Property passing by bequest, devise, or inheritance receives a stepped-up fair market value (FMV) basis under 26 U.S.C. 1014(a)(1) . Under 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(9) and IRS Publication 551 from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), the survivor's new basis depends on the percentage included in the gross estate and past depreciation. Community Property State Exception Under 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6), community property states allow both halves of qualifying marital community property to receive a fair market value basis adjustment at the first death. In community property states such as California, Texas, and Washington, the total value of community property generally becomes the basis of the entire property if at least half is includible in the gross estate, per IRS Publication 551. Advertise With Us → How Tenancy in Common Works A tenancy in common allows two or more co-owners to hold undivided fractional interests in a single parcel of real estate. Under this structure, there is no automatic right of survivorship. Because a tenancy in common does not include a right of survivorship, a deceased owner's share passes through that owner's estate rather than to the co-owner. State laws govern rules outside federal tax and survivorship defaults, meaning these matters are state-specific. How Joint Tenancy Works A joint tenancy with right of survivorship is the form of ownership that 26 U.S.C. 2040 describes for property held by the decedent and another person. The defining feature of joint tenancy is the right of survivorship. When one joint tenant dies, the surviving joint tenants take the decedent's interest under the terms of the instrument. State statutes heavily regulate how joint tenancies are formed and maintained. Section 689.15 of the 2024 Florida Statutes provides an informative statutory model. The statute states that the common-law doctrine of survivorship does not prevail in Florida real estate unless the instrument creating the estate expressly provides for the right of survivorship. To establish survivorship rights in Florida, the instrument creating the estate must expressly provide for the right of survivorship. Florida Section 689.15 also addresses marital estates, providing an explicit statutory exception for tenancy by the entirety. 26 U.S.C. 2040 names tenancy by the entirety as held by the decedent and the decedent's spouse. Furthermore, Section 689.15 specifies that in cases of estates by the entirety, the tenants automatically become tenants in common upon the legal dissolution of their marriage. When a married couple divorces, tenants by the entirety become tenants in common by operation of Section 689.15. Tax Basis When an Owner Dies Section 2040 of the Internal Revenue Code decides how much of a joint interest the gross estate includes, and Section 1014 sets the basis of property acquired from a decedent, as IRS Publication 551 and IRS Topic no. 409 explain. When an owner sells real property, the taxable capital gain is calculated as the net sales proceeds minus the property's adjusted tax basis. When property passes through death, the basis is frequently adjusted to the fair market value of the property at the date of death under Section 1014(a)(1), commonly known as a basis step-up. The federal tax basis rules treat married co-owners and non-spouse co-owners under vastly different standards. For married individuals holding property as a qualified joint interest, 26 U.S.C. 2040(b) establishes a clean fifty-percent rule. A qualified joint interest includes property held by married spouses as tenants by the entirety or as joint tenants with right of survivorship, provided the spouses are the only co-owners. Under Section 2040(b)(1), exactly one-half of the property's fair market value is included in the deceased spouse's gross estate, regardless of how much money each spouse contributed toward the original purchase price. Under IRS Publication 551, the surviving spouse calculates a new adjusted basis by combining their existing basis in their retained half with the fair market value of the inherited half. Non-spouse joint tenants face an entirely different mechanism known as the consideration-furnished rule under 26 U.S.C. 2040(a). When joint tenants are not married, the entire fair market value of the property enters the deceased owner's gross estate under Section 2040(a), except for whatever portion can be proven to have originally belonged to the surviving tenant. The surviving tenant must produce documentation showing their financial contribution toward the acquisition cost. IRS Publication 551 provides an authoritative example of this non-spouse rule featuring two joint owners, John and Jim. John and Jim purchased commercial property for $30,000 as joint tenants with right of survivorship. John furnished two-thirds of the purchase price ($20,000), while Jim furnished one-third ($10,000). Depreciation deductions allowed before John's death were $12,000, and under local law each had a half interest in the income from the property. When John died, the property had reached a fair market value of $60,000. Because John paid two-thirds of the acquisition cost, two-thirds of the property's value ($40,000) was includible in John's gross estate under Section 2040(a). Jim's new basis at John's death became $44,000. This figure is calculated by taking Jim's original $10,000 cost basis, subtracting his $6,000 allowed depreciation deductions to reach an adjusted basis of $4,000, and adding the $40,000 value included in John's estate. Publication 551 says that if Jim had not contributed any part of the purchase price, his basis would be $54,000, figured by subtracting the $6,000 depreciation allocated to his half from the $60,000 fair market value. If local law gave Jim no income interest and he paid nothing, Jim's basis would step up to the full $60,000 fair market value. Community property states provide another critical statutory exception under 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6). In the nine community property states identified in IRS Publication 551, Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin, married spouses are each treated as owning half the community estate. When one spouse dies, 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6) provides that the surviving spouse's half share of community property also receives an adjusted fair market value basis, as long as at least half the total community interest was includible in the deceased spouse's gross estate. Who Should Choose Each Option Choosing between tenancy in common and joint tenancy requires matching legal deed mechanics to family relationships, financial contributions, and long-term estate distribution goals. Neither option serves every ownership group. Joint tenancy with right of survivorship fits co-owners who share equal financial investments, possess identical ownership goals, and want the interest to pass to the surviving owner under the survivorship right. It fits married couples purchasing a residence who intend for the surviving spouse to hold outright ownership. Tenancy in common fits business partners, unrelated co-investors, or family members who contribute unequal amounts of capital toward a property purchase. It allows each party to maintain a proportionate financial stake and ensures that each investor can direct their equity to chosen beneficiaries rather than forfeiting their investment to a surviving co-investor. This guidance is not suitable for unmarried partners making unequal financial contributions toward a home purchase, blended families with children from prior relationships, or co-owners residing in states where statutory survivorship defaults differ. In blended families, survivorship moves a deceased owner's interest to the surviving owner under the instrument, so the survivorship wording matters. For joint tenants who are not spouses, 26 U.S.C. 2040(a) includes the full value in the decedent's gross estate except the part the survivor can show originally belonged to the survivor. Co-owners in these complex categories should evaluate customized estate planning alternatives rather than relying solely on deed titling. Property owners can review how to fund a trust to see how placing real estate inside a revocable living trust provides probate avoidance while preserving granular inheritance instructions. Reviewing our comparison on a living trust vs will also helps co-owners evaluate whether a comprehensive trust structure protects their heirs better than relying on survivorship deeds. Several legal conditions would alter this structural recommendation. If your state's statutory default presumes survivorship rather than tenancy in common, deed drafting requirements shift. If a married couple lives in a community property state, 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6) and IRS Publication 551 describe a different basis rule for the surviving spouse's half. Our Takeaway for Tenants in Common vs Joint Tenancy Joint tenancy with right of survivorship fits co-owners who want their real estate to pass automatically to surviving owners, while tenancy in common fits co-owners who want their ownership stake to pass through an estate plan to chosen beneficiaries. Based on reported statutory authorities including 26 U.S.C. 2040, 26 U.S.C. 1014, IRS Publication 551, and Section 689.15 of the 2024 Florida Statutes, neither structure offers a universal tax advantage. Federal tax basis adjustments depend heavily on whether co-owners are married spouses, how much each non-spouse owner paid toward the purchase price, and whether the real estate sits in a community property state. This framework changes if your state statute establishes a different survivorship presumption, if you reside in a community property jurisdiction, or if co-owners contribute unequal purchase capital. Frequently Asked Questions What happens to a tenant in common when they die? When a tenant in common dies, their ownership share passes through their estate rather than transferring to the surviving co-owners. The deceased owner's fractional interest transfers according to the terms of their will, a trust, or statutory state intestacy laws, and the inherited interest generally receives a stepped-up fair market value basis under 26 U.S.C. 1014(a)(1). What happens to a joint tenant when they die? When a joint tenant dies, their ownership interest extinguishes immediately and passes automatically to the surviving joint tenants by right of survivorship. The surviving owners' adjusted tax basis is calculated under 26 U.S.C. 1014 and 2040 based on marital status and past financial contributions. Which is better for taxes, tenants in common or joint tenancy? Neither title structure is an inherent tax winner, because the federal tax outcome depends on marriage status, acquisition funding, and community property rules. One-half of a qualified joint interest held by spouses is included in the decedent's gross estate under 26 U.S.C. 2040(b), non-spouse joint tenants are evaluated based on financial contributions under Section 2040(a), and community property couples follow the basis rule in 26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(6). What are the disadvantages of joint tenancy? The primary disadvantage of joint tenancy is that the right of survivorship removes your ability to leave your property share to chosen heirs through a will. Furthermore, non-spouse joint owners face strict financial contribution tracing under 26 U.S.C. 2040(a) to avoid having the entire asset included in a deceased owner's gross estate, and individual state statutes such as Florida Section 689.15 require precise deed language to create survivorship rights. Which is better, tenants in common or joint tenancy? Joint tenancy is better for co-owners who want their property share to pass to surviving owners under the survivorship right, whereas tenancy in common is better for co-owners who hold unequal stakes or want their share to pass to designated children or heirs. State statutory defaults and estate planning goals dictate which title form functions best for your situation.
