## CLOSED FACT LIST — the withdrawal-longevity math below was computed with the standard finite-annuity depletion formula N = ln(W / (W − r×P)) / ln(1 + r), where P = starting balance, W = fixed annual withdrawal, r = annual investment return, N = years until depletion. Use these exact figures; do not recompute or alter them.

- Starting balance for every scenario: $150,000.
- Scenario 1 — kept in cash, no investment growth, withdrawing $1,000/month ($12,000/year): lasts exactly 12.5 years (150,000 ÷ 12,000).
- Scenario 2 — invested at a conservative 4% average annual real (after-inflation) return, withdrawing $1,000/month ($12,000/year): lasts approximately 17.7 years.
- Scenario 3 — invested at a conservative 4% average annual real return, withdrawing $750/month ($9,000/year): lasts approximately 28 years.
- Scenario 4 — withdrawing at the "4% rule" rate itself, i.e. $500/month ($6,000/year, 4% of $150,000): per the Trinity Study research the 4% rule is built on (Cooley, Hubbard & Walz, AAII Journal, 1998), a 4% initial withdrawal rate, adjusted for inflation, has historically held up for roughly 30 years in worst-case historical market sequences.
- The average monthly Social Security retirement benefit for a retired worker in 2026 is about $2,071 (about $24,850/year), after the January 2026 2.8% COLA. Source: SSA (ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html). A retiree relying mainly on $150,000 plus Social Security is, in effect, combining Scenario 2 or 3's portfolio income with this benefit amount — use this combination explicitly when addressing "can you retire with no [other] money," i.e. relying primarily on Social Security with a smaller portfolio as a supplement, since $150,000 is a modest supplement rather than a full replacement for wage income on its own.
- Claiming Social Security at 62 instead of full retirement age (67 for those born 1960 or later) permanently reduces the monthly benefit; delaying past full retirement age up to 70 increases it via delayed retirement credits (roughly 8% more per year of delay). Source: ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html.
- Relocating to a lower cost-of-living area is one of the most effective, non-obvious levers for stretching a modest portfolio like $150,000, since housing is typically the largest line item in most retirement budgets. (General, uncontested mechanism.)
- "Strategic withdrawals" commonly means sequencing which account to draw from first for tax efficiency: taxable brokerage funds first (to let tax-advantaged accounts keep compounding), then traditional tax-deferred accounts, then Roth accounts last (since Roth withdrawals are generally tax-free and benefit most from staying invested longest) — a common ordering discussed in retirement-planning guidance, not a legal requirement.
- Additional income streams that extend a $150,000 balance: continued part-time work, rental income from a property, or an annuity that converts part of the balance into guaranteed income; none of these have a specific dollar figure to state here without a source, so name the mechanism, not an invented payout.
