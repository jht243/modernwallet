## Differentiation from the sibling $3M-at-40 guide (mandatory)
The $3M/age-40 guide's central tension is "is this enough to cover the basics for a 50-plus-year horizon." At $10M/age 50, that tension does not exist — $10M comfortably clears any reasonable household's basic and even luxury spending, and the horizon (a 50-year-old retiring, versus a 40-year-old) is shorter. The genuinely different, higher-value questions at this asset level are: what withdrawal rate is defensible for a ~40-to-45-year horizon (not the more extreme 50-plus-year horizon the $3M/40 guide used); the risks that actually matter at $10M (concentration risk in a single stock/business-sale position, lifestyle inflation, estate and tax planning) rather than the risks that matter at $3M (running out of money, health-insurance-bridge cost as a share of budget); and federal estate-tax exposure, since $10M is actually BELOW the 2026 federal exemption amount for an individual — a genuinely non-obvious point the $3M/40 guide never needed to raise. ## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure.

- A 50-year-old retiree has a shorter withdrawal horizon than a 40-year-old retiree (roughly 40 to 45 years to a life expectancy in the high 80s/low 90s, versus 50-plus years for a 40-year-old), which supports a somewhat less conservative withdrawal rate than the 3% to 3.5% used for a 40-year-old's horizon, while still being more conservative than the classic 30-year-horizon 4% rule. A 3.5% to 4% initial withdrawal rate is a defensible range for a 50-year-old's horizon. Source basis: Trinity Study research (Cooley, Hubbard & Walz, AAII Journal, 1998) on how withdrawal-rate sustainability varies with horizon length.
- At 4%, $10,000,000 supports $400,000 a year before tax. At a more conservative 3.5%, $10,000,000 supports $350,000 a year before tax. Both figures are pre-tax and assume the portfolio stays invested.
- At this income level, essentially every household's basic and discretionary spending is covered even in a high-cost-of-living city with dependents — the single/family-size and location distinctions that matter at $3M are not the binding constraint at $10M. State this plainly rather than manufacturing a false tension.
- The 2026 federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 per individual (up from $13,990,000 for 2025), made permanent (no longer scheduled to revert to a lower amount) by Section 70106 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and confirmed in IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. For a married couple, the combined federal exemption is effectively $30,000,000. This means a $10 million individual estate sits BELOW the federal exemption and would generally owe no federal estate tax as of 2026 rules, though the exemption applies at death (net of any lifetime taxable gifts already used) and is scheduled to keep adjusting for inflation, not a fixed number forever. Source: IRS (irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill and irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/whats-new-estate-and-gift-tax).
- Separate from the federal exemption, several U.S. states levy their own estate or inheritance tax with exemption thresholds well below the federal level — state rules vary by state of residence, so this guide should flag the issue and tell the reader to check their own state's rules rather than stating a specific state figure (none is on this fact list).
- Concentration risk: a $10 million net worth built largely from a single company's stock (equity compensation, a business sale with a note or earnout, concentrated founder shares) carries a different risk profile than the same amount already diversified across a broad portfolio — a market or company-specific decline in a concentrated position can meaningfully change the retirement math even though the aggregate number looks secure. Diversifying a concentrated position over time (subject to tax-loss/tax-gain planning and, where relevant, insider-trading windows) is a standard risk-management step. (General, uncontested mechanism — no invented figures.)
- Lifestyle inflation is a documented behavioral risk at high asset levels: spending tends to expand to match perceived wealth, and a household that scales its $400,000/year withdrawal upward without revisiting the underlying withdrawal-rate math can erode the safety margin the number originally had. (General, uncontested planning caveat.)
- Sequence-of-returns risk (a downturn early in retirement shrinking the portfolio more than the same downturn later) still applies at any asset level, though the ABSOLUTE dollar cushion at $10M gives more room to hold a temporary downturn without forced selling than a smaller portfolio has.
