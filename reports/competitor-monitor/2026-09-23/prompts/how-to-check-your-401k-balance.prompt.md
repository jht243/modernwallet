## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a company name, fee, or figure not stated here; say it is unpublished and link the reader to the primary source to verify.

- A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement account; the current balance for an active account is normally checked through the plan recordkeeper's online portal or mobile app, or by calling the number on plan statements. (General, uncontested mechanism — no invented provider names.)
- The Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) operates a Missing Participants Program / unclaimed-benefits search. If a former employer's private-sector plan lost track of a participant when the plan ended, benefits may have been transferred to PBGC. The PBGC search tool lets a person search by last name and the last four digits of their Social Security number; the database is described by PBGC as updated periodically. Source: pbgc.gov.
- The U.S. Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA) runs an Abandoned Plan Program database for plans whose sponsoring employer went out of business, and a separate, newer Retirement Savings Lost and Found Database designed to help workers locate lost retirement accounts. Source: dol.gov/agencies/ebsa.
- The National Registry of Unclaimed Retirement Benefits (NRURB) is a private (non-government) registry employers can use to report separated employees with vested account balances; a person can search it by Social Security number at unclaimedretirementbenefits.com. It is privately operated, not a government database — state that distinction plainly since a reader should know which registries are official and which are private.
- Every state also runs its own unclaimed-property database, which can hold retirement balances an old employer turned over to the state after being unable to locate a former employee; searching these state databases is free. (General, uncontested mechanism — do not name a specific state site since ModernWallet is a national site.)
- To find which plan(s) a person has actually had, the reliable paper trail is: old pay stubs and W-2s (the plan/company name usually appears there), a former employer's HR or benefits department, and the plan's Form 5500 filing, which is searchable through the Department of Labor's EFAST2 system (efast.dol.gov) once you know the plan or sponsor name.
- Once a plan and its recordkeeper are identified, checking the balance itself normally requires identity verification (Social Security number, date of birth, and sometimes an old account or employee ID) before the recordkeeper will disclose a balance by phone or reissue online-portal access.
- Federal law (ERISA) generally requires 401(k) plans to provide participants an annual account statement (often quarterly for participant-directed plans); a former employee is normally still entitled to that statement until the balance is distributed, rolled over, or (for small balances) automatically cashed out/rolled into an IRA by the plan under IRS/DOL small-balance cash-out rules — do not state a specific dollar threshold for automatic cash-out since it should be verified plan-by-plan; describe the mechanism without inventing the exact number.
