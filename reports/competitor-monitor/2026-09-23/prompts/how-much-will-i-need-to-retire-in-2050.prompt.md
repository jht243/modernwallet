## CLOSED FACT LIST — the inflation and savings-target math below is a WORKED EXAMPLE computed with standard compounding math: FV = PV × (1 + inflation)^years, using 24 years (2026 to 2050), and the 25x/33x "savings multiple" convention (25x annual spending = a 4% withdrawal rate; 33x annual spending ≈ a 3% withdrawal rate — the same convention this site already uses elsewhere, e.g. in its "retire in 10 years" guide). Use these exact figures; do not recompute or alter them, and present them explicitly as one illustrative example, not a universal target.

- Worked example: a person who wants $60,000 a year in TODAY'S (2026) purchasing power, retiring in 2050 (24 years away).
- At 2.5% average annual inflation (near the lower end of the roughly 2.5%-to-3% range the U.S. has averaged over the past few decades per BLS CPI data), $60,000 in today's dollars is equivalent to about $108,500 a year in 2050 dollars.
- At 3% average annual inflation, that same $60,000 today is equivalent to about $122,000 a year in 2050 dollars.
- Applying the 25x rule (4% withdrawal rate) to the 3% inflation scenario: about $122,000 × 25 ≈ $3.05 million needed by 2050, in 2050 dollars.
- Applying the more conservative 33x rule (roughly 3% withdrawal rate) to the same 3% inflation scenario: about $122,000 × 33 ≈ $4.03 million needed by 2050, in 2050 dollars.
- These are 2050-dollar targets, not today's-dollar targets — the whole point of the calculation is that a "comfortable" number in today's dollars understates what will actually be needed by 2050 because of inflation between now and then. Source for the inflation range: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI data (bls.gov/cpi/); the Federal Reserve's stated long-run inflation target is 2% (federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm), though actual realized inflation has run somewhat above that over multi-decade averages.
- Social Security by 2050: per the Social Security Board of Trustees' 2026 report (which the trustees stated is consistent with the 2025 report's projections), the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) trust fund is projected to deplete its reserves in 2033, after which incoming payroll tax revenue is projected to cover about 77% of scheduled retirement benefits unless Congress acts before then; the combined Social Security trust funds (OASI + Disability Insurance) are projected to deplete in 2034, with about 81% of combined benefits payable from ongoing revenue after that point. Source: SSA (ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-06-09.html and ssa.gov/oact/TR/2025/X1_trLOT.html). A 2050 retirement plan falls well after these projected depletion dates, so this guide should state this caveat plainly rather than assuming full scheduled benefits are guaranteed, while also noting benefits are not projected to disappear entirely — some level of ongoing payroll-tax-funded benefit is projected to continue.
- Historical long-run U.S. stock market returns (S&P 500, dividends reinvested) have averaged roughly 10% nominal / roughly 7% real (inflation-adjusted) annually over long periods, per S&P Dow Jones Indices SPIVA data — already used elsewhere on this site (e.g. the "how to invest $100k" guide) and safe to reuse here for savings-growth framing, but do not state this as a guaranteed future return.
- Adjusting contributions: because the 2050 target itself moves with the inflation assumption used, a saver's contribution rate should be revisited periodically (e.g., with each raise, or at least every few years) rather than fixed once and left alone — this is a general, uncontested planning practice, not a specific statistic.
- Healthcare costs are widely cited as a category that has historically grown faster than general CPI inflation; do not state a specific healthcare-inflation percentage here since none is confirmed on this fact list — describe it qualitatively (a cost category to budget separately and conservatively) rather than inventing a rate.
