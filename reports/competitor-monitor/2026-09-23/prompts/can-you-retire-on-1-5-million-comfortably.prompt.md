## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure.

- Standard retirement-age framing (not early retirement): a 30-year retirement horizon is what the classic "4% rule" was built around, based on the Trinity Study research (Cooley, Hubbard & Walz, "Retirement Savings: Choosing a Withdrawal Rate That Is Sustainable," AAII Journal, 1998). At a 4% initial withdrawal rate, $1,500,000 supports $60,000 a year before tax. At a more conservative 3.5% (a common adjustment for extra safety margin or a longer-than-30-year horizon), $1,500,000 supports $52,500 a year before tax.
- These are pre-tax, portfolio-only figures. Actual spendable income also depends on Social Security (which supplements portfolio withdrawals for nearly all retirees) and on which account type the money sits in (a traditional 401(k)/IRA withdrawal is taxed as ordinary income; a Roth withdrawal generally is not; a taxable brokerage withdrawal is taxed partly as capital gains).
- The average monthly Social Security retirement benefit for a retired worker in 2026 is about $2,071 (about $24,850 a year), after the 2.8% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) that took effect in January 2026. Source: SSA COLA announcement (widely reported; official source ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html). Use this only as an illustrative average — a reader's own benefit depends on their own earnings record and claiming age.
- A single person or couple with no dependents in a moderate-to-lower cost-of-living area finds $60,000-plus a year (portfolio) plus Social Security stretches meaningfully further than the same household in a high-cost metro area, where housing alone can consume a large share of that budget. (General, uncontested mechanism — do not invent a specific city or number.)
- Sequence-of-returns risk (a downturn early in retirement, while the portfolio is largest and withdrawals are happening, can permanently shrink the balance more than the same downturn occurring later) is the standard risk cited for any portfolio-funded retirement, at any withdrawal rate. Holding one to two years of spending in cash or short-term reserves is a commonly cited way to reduce forced selling into a downturn.
- Extending a $1.5 million portfolio's safety margin: lowering the withdrawal rate (spending less initially), delaying Social Security past full retirement age to increase the eventual monthly benefit (delayed retirement credits add rougly 8% per year of delay between full retirement age and age 70), relocating to a lower cost-of-living area, and maintaining some equity exposure through retirement rather than moving entirely to cash (since a 20-30 year retirement still needs some growth to outpace inflation) are all standard, defensible levers. Source for delayed retirement credits: ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html (already used elsewhere on this site).
