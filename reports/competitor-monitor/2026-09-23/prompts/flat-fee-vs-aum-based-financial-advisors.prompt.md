## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific advisor firm's price; cite only the figures below, which are from named third-party studies/orgs, never from the SmartAsset competitor page.

- AUM (assets-under-management) fee: advisors who charge this way typically use a graduated ("tiered") fee schedule where the percentage rate declines as assets grow. A commonly cited average/median AUM rate across the industry is roughly 1% for a portfolio in the $500,000-to-$1,000,000 range, with typical published ranges spanning about 0.75% to 1.5% annually depending on the advisor and portfolio size. Because the fee is a percentage, the dollar cost rises automatically as the portfolio grows, even if the advisor's workload does not change proportionally.
- Flat-fee / retainer advisors: per the 2026 State of Financial Planning Fees study (Envestnet), the average ANNUAL RETAINER fee among advisors who charge separately for financial planning was $6,815, up from $4,484 in 2023 (a 52% increase over that period). The same study found the average ONE-TIME financial-plan fee was $2,926, and the average MONTHLY subscription fee was $595/month.
- NAPFA (the National Association of Personal Financial Advisors, whose members are exclusively fee-only) states that fee-only advisors may structure their compensation as an hourly rate, a retainer, a flat fee, or a percentage of assets under management — flat-fee and AUM are both fee-only structures; the distinguishing feature of "fee-only" is the ABSENCE of commissions, not the flat-vs-AUM choice itself. Source: napfa.org.
- Because a flat/retainer fee does not scale with portfolio size, it becomes relatively cheaper than an AUM percentage as assets grow past a certain point, while an AUM fee can be cheaper in dollar terms for a smaller portfolio where a flat retainer might otherwise represent a larger percentage of assets. Neither structure is "always cheaper" — the crossover point depends on the specific flat fee and AUM rate being compared.
- Incentive alignment: an AUM-based advisor's compensation rises as the client's portfolio value rises, which several fiduciary-focused industry commentators note creates an incentive tied to asset growth — meaning the advisor's interests are more directly tied to keeping assets invested and growing than to other advice that might reduce AUM (e.g., recommending a client pay off a low-rate mortgage with invested funds, or hold more cash). A flat-fee advisor's compensation does not change based on how much is invested, which removes that specific incentive but does not, by itself, guarantee better advice — the SEC and other regulators note that a genuine fiduciary duty (a legal duty to act in the client's best interest) is what matters most, regardless of fee structure, and both flat-fee and AUM advisors can be fiduciaries. Source: investor.gov (SEC), general regulatory framing.
- A Registered Investment Adviser (RIA) that provides ongoing investment advice is generally held to a fiduciary standard under the Investment Advisers Act, requiring a duty of care and a duty of loyalty to the client — this fiduciary status is not determined by whether the advisor charges flat or AUM fees. Source: investor.gov (SEC).
- Both structures can be paired with either a fiduciary duty or (less commonly today) a suitability-only standard; the fee STRUCTURE (flat vs. AUM) is a separate question from the LEGAL STANDARD (fiduciary vs. suitability) the advisor operates under, and readers should check both, not assume one implies the other.
