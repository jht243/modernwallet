## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure.

- A Roth 403(b) is offered only through an eligible employer's plan (typically public schools, nonprofits, and certain hospital/ministerial employers); a Roth IRA is an individual account opened directly with a brokerage or custodian, with no employer involved at all.
- 2026 contribution limits: a Roth 403(b) shares the same employee elective-deferral limit as a 401(k)/457(b) under IRC 402(g) — $24,500 for 2026, with an $8,000 catch-up for participants 50 and older, and an enhanced $11,250 catch-up for participants aged 60 to 63 under SECURE 2.0. A Roth IRA's 2026 contribution limit is $7,500, with a $1,100 catch-up for those 50 and older — roughly a third of the Roth 403(b)'s room. Source: IRS COLA increases page (irs.gov/retirement-plans/cola-increases-for-dollar-limitations-on-benefits-and-contributions).
- Income limits: a designated Roth 403(b) account has NO income limit — any participant in the plan can make Roth contributions regardless of income. A Roth IRA DOES have an income limit: for 2026, a single filer can contribute the full amount below $153,000 modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), a reduced amount between $153,000 and $168,000, and nothing above $168,000; a married-filing-jointly couple can contribute the full amount below $242,000 MAGI, a reduced amount between $242,000 and $252,000, and nothing above $252,000. Source: IRS (irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500).
- Because the Roth 403(b) has no income limit, a high earner who is phased out of contributing directly to a Roth IRA can still make Roth contributions through their employer's Roth 403(b) — this is one of the most practically important, non-obvious differences between the two accounts.
- Employer contributions: a Roth 403(b) can receive an employer match. Historically, employer matching contributions were deposited on a pre-tax basis even when the employee's own deferrals were Roth; SECURE 2.0 now permits (but does not require) an employer to let vested matching contributions be made directly into the employee's Roth account instead, so the match may be pre-tax or Roth depending on the specific employer's plan design. A Roth IRA never has an employer match, since no employer is involved.
- Required minimum distributions (RMDs): as of the 2024 tax year (per SECURE 2.0 Section 325), a designated Roth account inside an employer plan — including a Roth 403(b) — no longer has a lifetime RMD requirement for the original account owner, matching the Roth IRA, which has never had a lifetime RMD requirement for the original owner. This change applies to the Roth 403(b) itself; the PRE-TAX portion of the same employer plan, if any, still has its own RMD rules. A person who INHERITS either a Roth 403(b) or a Roth IRA is still subject to beneficiary distribution rules, which differ from the original-owner rule stated here.
- Investment options: a Roth 403(b) is limited to whatever menu of funds/annuities the specific employer's plan offers (403(b) plans have historically offered more annuity-based products than 401(k) plans, alongside mutual funds, though the exact menu is entirely plan-specific). A Roth IRA is self-directed at whichever brokerage the account is opened with, with a materially wider range of available investments (individual stocks, a broad menu of funds, bonds, etc.).
- Withdrawal access to contributions: a Roth IRA allows an account owner to withdraw their own CONTRIBUTIONS (not earnings) at any time, for any reason, without tax or penalty, because IRS ordering rules treat a Roth IRA distribution as coming from contributions first. A Roth 403(b), by contrast, follows employer-plan distribution rules; a withdrawal is generally NOT permitted simply because the employee wants access to it (in-service withdrawals before a triggering event — severance, age 59 1/2, hardship, etc. — are typically restricted, and when a distribution IS permitted it is generally taxed and penalty-tested on a pro-rata basis across contributions and earnings together, unlike a Roth IRA's contributions-first ordering).
- The 5-year rule (earnings become qualified/tax-free only after the account has been open 5 tax years AND the owner is 59 1/2 or meets another qualifying condition) applies separately to each account type — meeting it in a Roth 403(b) does not satisfy it for a separate Roth IRA, and vice versa. A rollover from a Roth 403(b) into a Roth IRA generally carries over the ORIGINAL Roth 403(b)'s start date for this purpose rather than resetting the clock, per IRS ordering/aggregation rules for Roth rollovers — state this generally without inventing a specific case example.
- Both are compatible with each other: nothing prevents an eligible employee from contributing to BOTH a Roth 403(b) (if their employer offers one) and a Roth IRA (subject to the Roth IRA's own income limit) in the same year, since the two contribution limits are entirely separate and do not share a combined cap.
