## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a figure.

- The Social Security "earnings test" (retirement earnings test) applies ONLY to a beneficiary who has claimed benefits and is still working before reaching full retirement age (FRA). It counts EARNED income — wages from a job and net self-employment income — not unearned/retirement income. Source: SSA (ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/whileworking.html).
- Income that does NOT count toward the earnings test: 401(k) and IRA withdrawals, pension payments, annuity income, investment income (dividends, interest, capital gains), rental income, and other retirement/investment income. Only wages and net self-employment earnings count. (Same SSA source.)
- 2026 earnings test limits: for a beneficiary who will be under full retirement age for all of 2026, the limit is $24,480 a year in earned income; benefits are reduced $1 for every $2 earned above that limit. For a beneficiary who reaches full retirement age at some point in 2026, a higher limit of $65,160 applies to earnings in the months before the month FRA is reached, with benefits reduced $1 for every $3 earned above that limit (only counting earnings before the FRA month). Once a beneficiary reaches full retirement age, there is no earnings limit at all, for any month afterward. Source: SSA 2026 COLA fact sheet (ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html).
- Full retirement age (FRA) is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later. Source: SSA (ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html).
- Withholding under the earnings test is NOT a permanent loss: SSA recalculates the benefit at full retirement age to credit back the months benefits were withheld, which raises the ongoing monthly benefit going forward. (SSA describes this recalculation; do not invent a specific dollar recalculation example, describe the mechanism only.)
- Separately from the earnings test, retirement/investment income DOES matter for a different question: whether Social Security benefits themselves are taxable. Up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be subject to federal income tax if a retiree's "combined income" (adjusted gross income + nontaxable interest + half of Social Security benefits) exceeds set thresholds: for a single filer, 50% of benefits may be taxable above $25,000 combined income and up to 85% above $34,000; for a married-filing-jointly couple, the thresholds are $32,000 and $44,000. These thresholds are NOT indexed for inflation and have been unchanged since they were enacted. Source: IRS (irs.gov/faqs/social-security-income) and SSA (ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/taxes.html).
- Working in retirement (before FRA) can therefore have two separate, different effects that are often confused: (1) it can temporarily reduce the Social Security check itself via the earnings test (wages only), and (2) drawing more retirement income of any kind (working income OR withdrawals) can push "combined income" over the taxation thresholds, making more of the Social Security benefit taxable. These are two different rules, not one.
- Coordinating withdrawals with Social Security: because 401(k)/IRA/pension withdrawals never count toward the earnings test, a retiree who has already claimed Social Security before FRA and is also still working can draw retirement-account income freely without it reducing the SS check itself, though that added income can still raise the taxable-benefit calculation above. Multiple simultaneous income streams (part-time wages under the limit, retirement withdrawals, rental income) can be combined without ANY of the non-wage streams touching the earnings test.
