## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify at irs.gov.

- Residential rental buildings are depreciated using the straight-line method over a fixed 27.5-year recovery period under MACRS, with a mid-month convention (property placed in service mid-month for partial-year depreciation in the first and last year). Source: IRS Publication 527.
- Mortgage interest paid on a rental property loan is deductible as a rental expense. If a refinance pulls out more than the previous loan balance, interest tied to the portion used for non-rental purposes is NOT deductible as a rental expense. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- Local property taxes on the rental are deductible. Insurance premiums are deductible, but a prepaid multi-year policy must be spread across the years it actually covers, not deducted in full in the year paid. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- Repairs (fixing something back to its prior working condition — patching a roof leak, repainting) are currently deductible in the year paid. Improvements (something that betters, restores, or adapts the property to a new use — a new roof, a room addition) must be capitalized and depreciated over time, not deducted immediately. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- Legal and other professional fees connected to the rental — including tax-preparation fees for the portion of a return covering the rental (Schedule E) — are deductible rental expenses. Source: IRS Publication 527, quote: "You can deduct, as a rental expense, legal and other professional expenses such as tax return preparation fees you paid to prepare Schedule E."
- Travel to the rental property to manage, maintain, or show it is deductible if managing the property is the primary purpose of the trip. For vehicle use, a landlord can deduct either actual vehicle expenses or the IRS standard mileage rate. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- The IRS 2026 standard mileage rate for business use of a vehicle is 72.5 cents per mile for travel from January 1 through June 30, 2026, rising to 76 cents per mile for travel from July 1 through December 31, 2026 (a mid-year increase). Source: IRS newsroom releases (irs.gov/newsroom/irs-sets-2026-business-standard-mileage-rate-at-725-cents-per-mile-up-25-cents and the mid-year update).
- Utilities a landlord pays on behalf of tenants (not utilities the tenant pays directly) are deductible. Property management fees paid to a third-party manager are deductible. Advertising, cleaning, and maintenance costs are deductible. Office supplies used for the rental business are deductible when ordinary and necessary. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- Landlords report rental income and expenses on Schedule E (Form 1040), Supplemental Income and Loss. Depreciation is calculated on Form 4562 and the result carries to Schedule E. Source: IRS Publication 527.
- A de minimis safe harbor election lets a landlord expense (rather than capitalize and depreciate) the cost of a lower-cost item, up to a per-item/per-invoice dollar threshold set by IRS tangible-property regulations; the exact current threshold should be verified at the IRS Tangible Property Regulations FAQ page, since this guide should not state a specific dollar figure that isn't independently confirmed here — describe the safe harbor by mechanism and link the reader to the IRS FAQ to confirm the current threshold rather than stating a number.
- Depreciation recapture: when a rental property is sold, the portion of the gain attributable to depreciation already claimed ("unrecaptured Section 1250 gain") is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%, separate from ordinary capital-gains rates on the rest of the gain. Source: IRS Topic 703 / Publication 544.
- Sequence guidance only, no invented figures: 40% to 50% of gross rental income going to operating costs is described elsewhere on this site (can-i-retire-on-rental-income guide) as "a commonly cited investor rule of thumb," not a statutory figure — you may reference this same framing if useful, but do not present it as an IRS rule.
