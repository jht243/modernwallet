answer: Gains and losses on most equity options are capital gains, short-term if held one year or less and long-term if held longer than one year, but exercise, assignment and expiration each change the result: an exercised call adds the premium to the stock's cost basis, while a short option's premium is a short-term gain when it expires or is closed.

- A long option that is sold at a profit or loss produces a capital gain or loss; the holding period is measured from purchase to sale, one year or less is short-term, more than one year is long-term. Source: IRS Publication 550.
- A long option that expires worthless is treated as a capital loss on the expiration date. Source: IRS Publication 550.
- If you exercise a call you bought, the premium paid is added to the basis of the stock acquired and no gain or loss is recognized on the option at exercise. If you exercise a put you bought, the premium reduces the amount realized on the stock sale. Source: IRS Publication 550.
- For a short option (one you wrote), the premium received is not taxed when received; it is reported as a short-term capital gain when the option expires, is closed out, or is exercised and the premium is folded into the stock's sale or basis calculation. Source: IRS Publication 550.
- Section 1256 contracts, which include nonequity options such as broad-based index options, are taxed 60% as long-term and 40% as short-term capital gain regardless of holding period, are marked to market at year end, and are reported on Form 6781. Equity options on single stocks and most ETFs are not Section 1256 contracts. Source: IRS Publication 550 and Form 6781 instructions.
- The wash sale rule disallows a loss if you buy substantially identical stock or securities (including options) within 30 days before or after the sale at a loss; the disallowed loss is added to the basis of the new position. Source: IRS Publication 550.
- Hypothetical worked example: a $10,000 gain on a Section 1256 index option is taxed as $6,000 long-term and $4,000 short-term. A 22% ordinary bracket and a 15% long-term rate give 0.22 x 4,000 + 0.15 x 6,000 = $880 + $900 = $1,780, or 17.8% blended. The same $10,000 on a single-stock option held 6 months at 22% is $2,200. Rates are illustrative only; the reader must verify current brackets.
- Brokers report option proceeds on Form 1099-B; the taxpayer reports sales on Form 8949 and Schedule D. Qualified covered calls and some spreads have special rules; the page must tell the reader to read Publication 550 or consult a tax professional for straddles and qualified covered calls.
