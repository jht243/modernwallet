- Payment history and amounts owed (credit utilization) are the two largest categories of a FICO score: payment history is about 35% and amounts owed about 30%. Length of history is about 15%, new credit about 10%, and credit mix about 10%. Source: myFICO.
- Credit utilization is the share of your revolving limits that you are using. A commonly cited guideline is to stay under 30%, and lower is generally better. There is no official cutoff.
- Card issuers typically report your balance to the bureaus once a month, often around the statement closing date, so a balance you pay down after the statement closes can still report as high. Timing varies by issuer; check your statement date.
- Hypothetical worked example: one card with a $5,000 limit and a $2,000 statement balance is 40% utilization ($2,000 / $5,000). Paying it down to $1,000 before the statement closes reports 20% ($1,000 / $5,000). With a second card at $5,000 limit and $0 balance, total utilization is $1,000 / $10,000 = 10%.
- Errors on credit reports can be disputed free with each bureau and with the furnisher. Source: CFPB.
- Free credit reports are available weekly from each bureau at AnnualCreditReport.com. Source: CFPB.
- A late payment is generally reported once an account is 30 days past due, and it can stay on your report for seven years. Source: CFPB.
- Asking for a higher credit limit lowers utilization if your balance stays the same, but some issuers run a hard inquiry for the request; ask the issuer which type they use before you apply.
- Hypothetical example: raising a $5,000 limit to $8,000 with a $2,000 balance moves utilization from 40% to 25% ($2,000 / $8,000).
- An authorized user is added to someone else's card. Whether the account is reported on the authorized user's credit file is the issuer's practice, and the primary account holder's late payments and balance can affect the authorized user too.
- Some rent and utility payment reporting services can add payments to your credit file, and which scoring models count them varies by model and by lender.
- A secured credit card takes a refundable deposit that usually becomes the credit limit, and the issuer reports payments to the bureaus. Whether and when it graduates to an unsecured card is the issuer's policy.
- No legitimate service can remove accurate negative information in exchange for a fee. Source: CFPB credit repair guidance.
- Closing a card removes its limit from your utilization calculation, which can raise your utilization ratio if you carry balances elsewhere.
