## CLOSED FACT LIST — the ONLY facts this page may state. Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL; say it is unpublished and tell the reader to verify at the source.

1. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), the Federal Reserve's monetary-policy body, sets a target range for the federal funds rate — the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans — at regularly scheduled meetings held eight times a year. [Federal Reserve — Federal Open Market Committee](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm)
2. When the Fed raises the federal funds rate, borrowing costs across the economy tend to rise, including rates on credit cards, home equity lines of credit, and new auto and personal loans, because many of those products are priced off short-term benchmark rates tied to the federal funds rate. [Federal Reserve — Monetary Policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)
3. Bond prices and bond yields move in opposite directions: when prevailing interest rates rise, the price of an existing bond with a lower, fixed coupon rate falls, because new bonds are being issued at the higher, more attractive rate. When rates fall, existing higher-coupon bonds become more valuable and their prices rise. [SEC Investor.gov — Bonds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds)
4. This means a rate hike creates a paper loss on a bond (or bond fund) an investor already owns and plans to sell before maturity, but it does not change the fixed interest payments and final principal repayment for a bond held to maturity (assuming the issuer does not default). [SEC Investor.gov — Bonds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds)
5. A newly issued bond, Treasury bill, or certificate of deposit (CD) sold after a rate hike typically carries a higher yield than one issued before the hike, since issuers must compete with the new, higher going rate to attract buyers. [TreasuryDirect — Treasury Securities & Interest Rates](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/)
6. Bank deposit rates on savings accounts, high-yield savings accounts, and money market accounts are not set directly by the Fed; each bank sets its own rate and typically adjusts it only partway toward a federal funds rate change, and often with a delay, rather than moving in lockstep. [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — Saving](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/save/)
7. A certificate of deposit (CD) locks in its rate for the full term at purchase. Opening a CD shortly before a rate hike locks in the pre-hike rate for the whole term; opening one after the hike locks in the new, higher rate for the term instead — the tradeoff is the certainty of a locked rate against the chance rates move further before maturity. [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — Saving](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/save/)
8. A shorter-duration bond or bond fund loses less value from a given rate change than a longer-duration one, because its fixed payments are locked in for less time; this is the standard fixed-income concept of duration risk. [SEC Investor.gov — Bonds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds)
9. Stock valuations are also sensitive to interest rates, because a higher rate raises the discount rate used to value a company's future earnings, which can pressure stock prices independent of that company's own performance; this is one reason stocks and bonds can move together during a rate-driven shock instead of offsetting each other the way they typically do. [Federal Reserve — Monetary Policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)
10. The FOMC publishes a "dot plot" — the Summary of Economic Projections — showing individual members' expectations for the future path of the federal funds rate, which the market watches to gauge whether a single hike is likely to be a one-off or the start of a series of hikes; the Fed does not commit in advance to a specific future path. [Federal Reserve — Summary of Economic Projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl.htm)
