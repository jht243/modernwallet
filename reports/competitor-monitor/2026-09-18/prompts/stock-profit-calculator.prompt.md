## CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific brokerage's commission
schedule or a specific real stock's price — use clearly-labeled hypothetical numbers.

1. Realized profit or loss on a stock trade equals net sale proceeds (shares sold times sale
   price, minus any sell-side commission) minus net cost (shares bought times buy price, plus any
   buy-side commission).
2. The break-even sale price is the price per share at which net proceeds exactly equal net cost;
   selling above that price produces a profit, selling below it produces a loss, once both
   commissions are counted.
3. A gain on shares held for one year or less is generally taxed as a short-term capital gain, at
   the investor's ordinary income tax rate; a gain on shares held for more than one year generally
   qualifies for the lower long-term capital gains tax rate, per IRS guidance.
4. A capital loss can generally be used to offset capital gains, and up to $3,000 of any remaining
   net capital loss can generally be deducted against ordinary income per year, with any excess
   carried forward to future tax years, per IRS guidance.
5. Many online brokerages today charge $0 commission on standard online stock trades, though some
   brokers, order types, or account types (e.g. broker-assisted trades) can still carry a fee, so
   confirming the actual commission schedule for the specific trade matters more than assuming a
   flat rate.

## commonMistakes (4-6, concrete)

- Forgetting to subtract commissions on both the buy and sell side before calling it "profit."
- Confusing gross proceeds (shares × sale price) with net profit (proceeds minus cost).
- Not knowing your holding period and how it changes your expected tax treatment.
- Assuming every broker charges the same commission structure.
