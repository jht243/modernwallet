## CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific real company's historical
split ratio or split date — describe the mechanism generically or use a clearly-labeled
hypothetical example instead.

1. A forward stock split (e.g. "2-for-1") increases the number of shares a holder owns by the
   split ratio and divides the pre-split price per share and the pre-split cost basis per share by
   that same ratio, so the total dollar value of the position and the total dollar cost basis are
   unchanged by the split itself.
2. A reverse stock split (e.g. "1-for-10") decreases the number of shares a holder owns and
   multiplies the price per share and the cost basis per share by the inverse of the ratio, again
   leaving total position value and total cost basis unchanged.
3. Brokerages automatically adjust a shareholder's account for a split; a holder does not need to
   take any action for the adjustment itself to happen, though checking the new numbers against a
   brokerage statement is a reasonable sanity check.
4. A stock split does not change a company's total market capitalization, and it is not, by
   itself, a taxable event for the shareholder — the IRS treats a stock split as adjusting the
   number of shares and their basis, not as a sale or exchange.
5. Options contracts, if held, are also adjusted for a split by the options clearing process (the
   contract's deliverable share count and strike price are adjusted so the contract's total value
   is preserved), though the exact adjustment mechanics are handled by the option's clearing
   corporation, not by this calculator.

## commonMistakes (4-6, concrete)

- Assuming a split changes how much your position is worth (it doesn't).
- Forgetting to adjust your OWN manually-tracked cost basis after a split, leading to a wrong
  capital gain/loss calculation at sale.
- Confusing a forward split with a reverse split when entering the ratio into a calculator.
- Not checking whether held options contracts were adjusted separately.
