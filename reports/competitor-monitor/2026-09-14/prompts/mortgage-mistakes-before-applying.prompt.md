Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, benchmark, or URL;

1. Lenders re-verify credit and, in many cases, re-check employment and account balances close to
   the closing date, not only at initial approval — a change between approval and closing can
   change or cancel the loan. (CFPB, "Your Home Loan Toolkit")
2. A common underwriting guideline for total (back-end) debt-to-income ratio (all monthly debt,
   including the new house payment, divided by gross monthly income) is roughly 36% to 43% for
   many conventional loans, though some programs and compensating factors allow higher. This
   varies by lender and loan program — present as a range, not a single hard rule. (CFPB, "What
   is a debt-to-income ratio?")
3. New debt taken on before closing (a car loan, a new credit card, furniture financing, a
   co-signed loan) raises the DTI ratio the lender re-checks at closing and can push the file over
   the loan program's limit.
4. FICO's scoring model treats multiple mortgage (or auto) rate-shopping inquiries made within a
   short window (industry practice is commonly 14 to 45 days depending on the FICO version in
   use) as a single inquiry for scoring purposes — but this de-duplication window does not apply
   the same way across every score model, so a reader should not assume it covers a longer
   shopping period. (myFICO, "What's in my FICO Scores")
5. Credit utilization (the share of available revolving credit currently used) is one of the
   largest components of a FICO score; running up balances on existing cards before applying can
   lower a score even with no new account opened. (myFICO)
6. Missing or late payments in the months before applying can lower a credit score and stay on a
   credit report for up to seven years. (CFPB, "How long does information stay on my credit
   report?")
7. Closing a credit card account can raise your utilization ratio (by lowering total available
   credit) and can shorten your average account age — both credit-score factors — even though the
   account itself carried no balance.
8. Lenders generally review recent employment and income history and may re-verify employment
   again shortly before closing; a job change, a switch from salaried to commission/1099 income,
   or a gap in employment can require additional documentation or delay/derail an approval,
   though policies differ by lender and loan program.
9. A large, undocumented deposit into a bank account used for mortgage qualification can require
   the borrower to "source" it — provide paper documentation of where the money came from —
   before a lender will count it toward assets or a down payment. (CFPB, "Your Home Loan
   Toolkit")
10. On a joint mortgage application, lenders commonly consider both applicants' credit histories;
    conventional automated underwriting typically uses a "representative" score from each
    borrower and the loan may price off the lower-qualifying score of the two, and FHA loans
    generally use the lower of the two median scores — practice varies by lender and loan type,
    so present as a general pattern, not a universal rule. (CFPB general guidance on joint
    applications)
11. Co-signing any loan (for a child's car, a friend's apartment) adds that loan's full monthly
    payment to the co-signer's own DTI ratio for underwriting purposes, even though the co-signer
    never makes a payment on it.
12. The IRS annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per giver, per recipient, for 2026 (verify the
    current-year figure directly on IRS.gov before relying on it) — relevant because a down
    payment gift from a family member above that amount may require the giver to file a gift tax
    return, and the lender will still require a signed gift letter documenting the money is a
    gift, not a loan, regardless of the amount. (IRS, gift tax pages)
13. A rate lock has an expiration date; missing the closing date after a rate lock expires can
    require an extension fee or a new, possibly higher, rate — terms vary by lender.
14. The CFPB and HUD both publish free, first-party guidance on mortgage shopping and the closing
    process; a fee-only fiduciary financial advisor is required to act in the client's interest,
    unlike a commission-based loan officer.

## Section-by-section coverage (minimum — expand with real substance, do not pad)

1. Direct-answer intro: the core idea — approval is not final until closing, and the biggest
   mistakes happen in the weeks between preapproval and the closing table.
2. Racking up new debt before closing (fact 2, 3).
3. Not checking your credit early enough to fix errors (fact 6, plus practical timing — check
   months before shopping, not days).
4. Falling behind on existing bills (fact 6).
5. Maxing out or running up credit cards (fact 5).
6. Closing a credit card account (fact 7) — the non-obvious inversion of "paying off = good."
7. Switching jobs or income types mid-process (fact 8).
8. Making a major purchase (car, furniture, appliances) before closing (fact 3).
9. Marrying, or applying jointly with, someone with credit problems (fact 10) — the joint-file
   mechanics.
10. Co-signing a loan for someone else (fact 11).
11. Making a large, undocumented deposit (fact 9, 12 for the gift-letter/gift-tax angle).
12. What to do if you already made one of these mistakes — a practical recovery section (talk to
    your loan officer immediately, provide documentation, expect a delay rather than an automatic
    denial in many cases, rate-lock timing per fact 13).
13. When a fee-only financial advisor is worth bringing in (fact 14) — tie to
    /guides/how-to-choose-a-financial-advisor/.
14. Close on the concrete next action: run affordability/down payment numbers before touching a
    single dollar of the accounts a lender will check.
15. Who this is not for / what would change the answer: e.g., a cash buyer with no financing
    contingency is not exposed to most of these underwriting risks.
