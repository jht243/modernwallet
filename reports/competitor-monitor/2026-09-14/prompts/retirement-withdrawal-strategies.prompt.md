## CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a benchmark; hedge and tell the reader
to model their own plan with the linked calculator instead.

1. The "4% rule" is a widely cited retirement withdrawal guideline: withdraw about 4% of the
   portfolio balance in the first year of retirement, then adjust that dollar amount for
   inflation each year after, historically over roughly a 30-year horizon.
2. William Bengen, the financial researcher who originated the 4% rule in the 1990s, published
   updated research (reported publicly in 2025) raising his own worst-case historical starting
   withdrawal rate from 4.0% to about 4.7% for a 30-year horizon, based on an updated,
   more-diversified model portfolio rather than his original 50/50 stock-and-bond assumption.
   Present this as one researcher's updated finding, not a universal new standard — other
   analysts publish different rates for the same year, and the "right" number depends heavily on
   time horizon, portfolio mix, and how much spending flexibility a retiree actually has.
2b. Separately, other retirement-income researchers (e.g., Morningstar) have published lower
   starting-rate estimates in the same period for a FIXED, non-flexible spending plan, and
   notably higher rates (guardrails-style) for a retiree willing to cut spending after a bad
   market year and raise it after a good one. Present the range as varying by methodology and
   flexibility assumed, not as one settled number — never state a single rate as "the" correct one.
3. A fixed-dollar (fixed real) withdrawal strategy takes the same inflation-adjusted dollar
   amount every year regardless of portfolio performance. It is the easiest to budget around, but
   it does not adjust for a market downturn, which is the "sequence of returns" risk: a large
   fixed withdrawal taken during a market decline early in retirement locks in losses the
   portfolio may never fully recover from.
4. A total-return (percentage-of-portfolio) strategy withdraws a set PERCENTAGE of the current
   balance each year rather than a fixed dollar amount. The withdrawal automatically shrinks in a
   down year and grows in an up year, which reduces the sequence-of-returns risk of a fixed-dollar
   plan, but it means the retiree's actual spending income varies year to year.
5. A bucket strategy divides savings into separate pools by time horizon: a near-term bucket held
   in cash or cash-equivalents (commonly sized to cover roughly the next 1 to 2 years of living
   expenses, sometimes more), a mid-term bucket in more conservative investments (bonds), and a
   long-term bucket that stays invested for growth. The near-term bucket is spent down first,
   which lets the retiree avoid selling stocks during a downturn; the buckets are periodically
   refilled from investment gains when markets are up.
6. Account withdrawal order commonly follows one of two logics: (a) tax-efficient sequencing —
   draw from taxable accounts first, then tax-deferred (traditional 401(k)/IRA), then tax-free
   (Roth) last, to let tax-advantaged growth compound longest; or (b) a "fill the bracket"
   approach, where a retiree withdraws from tax-deferred accounts up to the top of a target
   marginal tax bracket each year (even before it's legally required), then covers any remaining
   spending from taxable or Roth accounts, to smooth taxable income across retirement instead of
   facing a spike later from Required Minimum Distributions. Which approach fits depends on the
   retiree's account mix, expected future tax bracket, and RMD horizon.
7. The IRS requires Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) from most traditional retirement
   accounts (traditional IRAs, most traditional 401(k)s) starting in the year the owner turns 73;
   the first RMD can be delayed until April 1 of the following year, but every RMD after that is
   due by December 31. Roth IRAs carry no RMDs during the original owner's lifetime.
8. Missing an RMD or taking less than required can trigger an IRS excise tax penalty on the
   shortfall; ModernWallet's own RMD calculator estimates the required amount for a given age and
   balance.
9. A worked example is allowed as an illustrative number, clearly framed as illustrative (e.g.,
   "Say a retiree has $800,000 split across a taxable brokerage account, a traditional IRA, and a
   Roth IRA...") — do not present an invented number as an official government or industry
   benchmark.
