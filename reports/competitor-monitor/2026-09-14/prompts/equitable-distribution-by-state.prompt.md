## CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price, limit, or docket-specific
detail; say state law varies and tell the reader to confirm with a local family law attorney.

1. Nine U.S. states use community property: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New
   Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. Every other state and the District of Columbia uses
   equitable distribution.
2. Community property generally starts from a legal presumption of an equal (50/50) split of
   marital property. Equitable distribution has no such presumption — a judge divides marital
   property based on what is fair under the state's statutory factors, which can produce an
   equal split, a close-to-equal split, or a meaningfully unequal one.
3. Equitable distribution statutes commonly list similar categories of factors, though the exact
   list and wording differs by state: the length of the marriage; each spouse's age, health, and
   earning capacity; each spouse's income and property at the start of the marriage versus now;
   contributions to the marriage, including as a homemaker or caregiver; which spouse will have
   custody of minor children and whether that spouse should keep the marital home; each spouse's
   separate property; the tax consequences of a proposed division; and, in some states, one
   spouse's dissipation or wasting of marital assets.
4. Virginia's equitable distribution statute (Va. Code §20-107.3) lists 11 statutory factors a
   court must consider. North Carolina's statute presumes an equal division of marital property
   but allows a court to divide unequally after weighing 12 statutory factors.
5. Only property and appreciation acquired DURING the marriage is generally "marital property"
   subject to division. Property owned before the marriage, and most inheritances and gifts
   received by one spouse individually, generally start as separate property in both community
   property and equitable distribution states — see ModernWallet's existing inheritance guide for
   how that separate-property status can be lost through commingling.
6. For a marital home, a court in an equitable distribution state has several common paths, not a
   single required outcome: order the home sold and the (equitable, not necessarily equal)
   proceeds divided; award the home to one spouse who buys out the other spouse's share of the
   equity, often by refinancing the mortgage into their name alone; or, especially when minor
   children are involved, defer the sale until a triggering event (the youngest child turning 18,
   for example) while one spouse retains use of the home in the meantime.
7. Dividing an employer retirement plan (a 401(k) or pension) generally requires a separate court
   order called a Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO), which instructs the plan
   administrator how to split the account without triggering the 10% early-withdrawal penalty
   that would otherwise apply. (IRS / ERISA — plan qualification rules)
8. An IRA is typically divided differently: it does not require a QDRO. A transfer of IRA assets
   made under a divorce or separation instrument, "incident to divorce," is not treated as a
   taxable distribution to either spouse under IRC §408(d)(6), provided it is done directly
   between the accounts under the terms of the decree.
9. Only the portion of a retirement account that grew or was contributed during the marriage is
   typically treated as marital property subject to division; contributions and growth from
   before the marriage are typically treated as separate property, though states differ on how
   that calculation (often called "coverture") is actually performed.
10. A family law attorney licensed in the reader's own state is the correct resource for how
    these general patterns apply to a specific case, because the exact factor list, presumption,
    and procedure differ by state statute.
