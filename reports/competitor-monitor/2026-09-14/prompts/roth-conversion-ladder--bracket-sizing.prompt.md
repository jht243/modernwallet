## Why this section, and how it differs from a sibling page

This site already has a guide "Roth Conversion Rules" (slug roth-conversion-rules) with a
general "how much room do you have before the next bracket" explainer and its OWN worked
example ($150,000 taxable income, $61,400 of room to the 24% bracket, married filing jointly).
Do NOT reuse that same household numbers or that same generic framing. This new section's job is
narrower and specific to the LADDER: an early retiree in the zero/low-income bridge years (the
exact situation this ladder guide is about) usually has far MORE bracket room than their bare
spending need, because they have little or no wage income during those years — so the sizing
question is "should I convert only what I plan to spend, or convert extra to use bracket room
that would otherwise go to waste before Social Security and RMDs arrive?" Write a fresh,
single-filer worked example using bridge-year dividend/interest income, not the $150,000
married-couple example from that sibling page. End the section with ONE sentence pointing to
`/guides/roth-conversion-rules/` for the general bracket-math mechanics, so the two pages divide
the topic instead of duplicating it.

## CLOSED FACT LIST

1. For 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer.
2. For 2026, the 22% federal bracket for a single filer runs from $50,400 to $105,700 of taxable
   income; the 24% bracket begins above $105,700.
3. Federal income tax brackets are marginal: only the income inside a bracket is taxed at that
   bracket's rate, not the whole amount.
4. A worked example using clearly-labeled illustrative numbers is allowed: an early retiree
   spending $50,000 a year (matching this page's own running example) who also receives some
   taxable interest or qualified dividend income from the taxable brokerage account funding their
   bridge years.
5. Converting more than the year's planned spending need is a real, available choice during a
   bridge year with little other income — it uses up "cheap" bracket room before Social Security
   and Required Minimum Distributions arrive later and raise the retiree's ordinary income floor.
   Present this as an option to weigh, not a universal recommendation — it still means paying a
   real tax bill today from outside the IRA, and it still raises that year's income for anything
   that keys off it (a point this page's prior section already covers, so do not re-explain it
   here, just note the tradeoff still applies).
6. State income tax, if any, is separate from and in addition to this federal bracket math, and
   varies by state.
