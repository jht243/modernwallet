1. Adding another person's name to a property deed while you are alive (for no payment, or for
   less than the share's fair market value) is generally treated by the IRS as a gift of that
   fractional ownership share, not a taxable sale.
2. A gift generally requires the giver to file IRS Form 709 (a gift tax return) only if the value
   of the gift to that one recipient in that year exceeds the annual gift tax exclusion — $19,000
   per giver, per recipient, for 2026 (verify the current-year figure on IRS.gov). Filing the
   return does not necessarily mean gift tax is owed; it can instead use up part of the giver's
   much larger lifetime gift and estate tax exemption.
3. The single biggest tax consequence, and the one most people miss, is the difference between
   carryover basis (a lifetime gift) and stepped-up basis (an inheritance at death). When you add
   someone to a deed as a gift during your life, IRC §1015 generally gives that person a
   "carryover" basis — they inherit YOUR original cost basis in their new share, not the current
   market value.
4. If that same person instead inherited the same share after your death, IRC §1014 generally
   gives them a "stepped-up" basis equal to the property's fair market value on the date of
   death. That erases, for tax purposes, all of the appreciation that happened during your
   lifetime — the heir could sell shortly after inheriting and owe little or no capital gains tax
   on that growth.
5. Because of fact 3 and 4, gifting a share of a highly appreciated property during life
   (carryover basis) can leave the recipient owing meaningfully MORE capital gains tax when they
   eventually sell than if they had simply inherited the same share at death (stepped-up basis) —
   present this as the central, most consequential tradeoff on the page, with a simple worked
   example using clearly-labeled illustrative numbers.
6. Adding a co-owner to a deed can also carry non-tax risks worth flagging alongside the tax
   analysis: the new co-owner's creditors, a divorce, or a lawsuit could in some circumstances
   reach their share of the property; the giver generally cannot sell, refinance, or mortgage the
   property afterward without the new co-owner's consent, since they now own a real legal
   interest; and if the new co-owner dies first, their share generally passes according to
   THEIR estate plan (or their state's intestacy law if they have none) rather than
   automatically back to the original owner, unless the deed specifically uses a
   right-of-survivorship structure.
7. Many states or counties reassess a property's assessed value (which can raise property taxes)
   when ownership changes hands, though most states provide some exclusion or exemption for
   certain family transfers (for example, parent-to-child transfers) — the exact rule differs
   significantly by state and even by county, so the reader must confirm the local reassessment
   rule before assuming a transfer is exempt.
8. There are situations where adding someone to a deed can still make sense despite these
   tradeoffs — for example, a spouse being added for co-ownership and survivorship purposes
   generally carries no gift tax at all, because transfers between U.S.-citizen spouses are
   unlimited under the marital deduction; or a parent who needs to add an adult child for
   practical reasons (a caregiver who needs authority, or immediate liquidity needs that outweigh
   the future basis cost) may reasonably accept the tradeoff.
9. Alternatives that avoid the carryover-basis problem while still addressing the underlying
   goal (avoiding probate, ensuring a smooth transfer) include: a revocable living trust (assets
   transferred through it can still get a stepped-up basis at death in most cases, since the
   grantor retains control during life); a transfer-on-death (beneficiary) deed, available in
   roughly 30 states, which passes the property at death outside probate while the original owner
   keeps full control and the recipient gets stepped-up basis, since no ownership interest
   transfers until death; and simply leaving the property through a will or trust.
10. A real estate attorney or estate planning attorney should review the specific deed language
    and state law before a family adds anyone to a deed, and a fee-only financial advisor or CPA
    can model the actual tax cost of each option side by side.
