## CLOSED FACT LIST

1. "Long-term care" describes a range of services for people who need ongoing help with daily
   activities (bathing, dressing, eating, mobility) because of a chronic illness, disability, or
   cognitive impairment — it can be delivered at home or in a facility.
2. A "long-term care facility" is a residential setting that delivers that care. It is an
   umbrella term covering several distinct facility types, not a synonym for any single one of
   them.
3. A nursing home (also called a skilled nursing facility) is the highest-acuity type of
   long-term care facility: it has licensed nurses on site around the clock and is built for
   residents who need ongoing clinical/medical care, not just help with daily activities.
4. Assisted living is a lower-acuity type of long-term care facility: residents generally live
   more independently, with staff help for daily activities (bathing, dressing, medication
   reminders) but not around-the-clock skilled nursing care.
5. Memory care is a specialized type of long-term care facility (often a secured wing within an
   assisted living community, or its own standalone facility) built specifically for residents
   with Alzheimer's disease or another dementia, with staffing and safety features assisted
   living alone does not provide.
6. In-home care (a home health aide or personal care aide) delivers the same category of
   long-term care services this page defines, but NOT inside a long-term care facility — it is
   care received at the person's own home instead.
7. This page's own cost/funding methodology (Medicare's 100-day Skilled Nursing Facility limit,
   the Genworth cost medians by setting) already appears elsewhere on this page and covers each
   of these settings — do not restate those figures here; this passage's job is only to fix the
   terminology before the reader hits that cost comparison.
