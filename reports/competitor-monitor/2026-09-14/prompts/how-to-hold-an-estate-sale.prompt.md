## CLOSED FACT LIST

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a price or percentage as a national
average; hedge ranges and tell the reader to get a written contract/quote.

1. An estate sale liquidates most or all of the contents of a home, typically after a death,
   divorce, downsize, or move into long-term care — distinct from a garage sale in scale (an
   entire household) and typically run over one or several consecutive days, in the home itself.
2. Estate sale companies typically charge a commission taken as a percentage of the gross sale
   proceeds rather than a flat up-front fee. Commission percentages commonly range from roughly
   25% to 50%+ of gross proceeds, and often run higher (toward the higher end of that range or
   beyond) for smaller estates or estates with lower-value, harder-to-sell items, since the
   company's labor cost is similar regardless of sale size. Present this as a range that a family
   should get in writing from any company before signing, not a fixed number.
3. DIY running an estate sale requires, at minimum: a full room-by-room inventory of items being
   sold; researching or appraising higher-value items (antiques, jewelry, collectibles, art) so
   they are not priced far under market value; pricing every remaining item, commonly starting
   near retail-comparable value and marking down as the sale continues (a common pattern is
   deeper discounts on the second or later day of a multi-day sale); advertising the sale
   (estate-sale-specific listing sites, local classifieds, yard signs, neighborhood postings);
   staging the home so items are organized and browsable; staffing the sale itself to handle
   payment, prevent theft, and answer questions; and deciding what to do with unsold items at the
   end (donate, consign, or arrange a buyout/cleanout).
4. Hiring an estate sale company typically covers the same steps (inventory, pricing,
   advertising, staging, running the sale, and handling leftovers) for the commission fee, plus
   the company's own buyer list/customer base and experience pricing collectibles; some
   companies also charge separate fees for costs like advertising, a dumpster/cleanout, or
   security, on top of the commission — get every fee in writing before signing.
5. A common mistake in DIY estate sales is under-pricing valuable items out of a desire to "just
   get rid of it," which specialists warn can cost the family real money on antiques, coins,
   jewelry, or collectibles that need a specialist appraisal before pricing.
6. A common mistake with hiring a company is skipping a written contract — the commission rate,
   any additional fees, the cleanout/disposal plan for unsold items, and insurance/liability for
   damage or theft during the sale should all be in writing before the sale date.
7. Tax treatment differs depending on WHO is selling. If the estate itself sells inherited
   property (through its executor, before distribution to heirs), the property generally
   receives a "step-up in basis" to its fair market value as of the date of death under federal
   tax rules, so selling promptly for close to that value typically produces little or no
   taxable gain for the estate to report. If a person is instead selling their OWN used personal
   belongings (not inherited, e.g., downsizing before a move), used household goods typically
   sell for less than their original cost, so there is usually no taxable gain to report — but a
   gain on any specific item sold above its basis is still technically taxable income.
8. Some states require collecting sales tax on items sold at an estate sale, though many states
   provide an exemption for occasional or isolated sales (as opposed to a recurring for-profit
   business) — this varies by state, and the reader should confirm with their own state's
   department of revenue before the sale.
9. Unsold items at the end of a sale are commonly donated to a qualified charity, which can
   produce a tax deduction for the donor if they itemize deductions and keep records of fair
   market value at the time of donation — consult a tax professional for a donation of
   meaningful value.
