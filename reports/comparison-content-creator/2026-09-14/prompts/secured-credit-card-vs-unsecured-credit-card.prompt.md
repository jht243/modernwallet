## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A secured credit card requires the cardholder to put down a cash security deposit, and the credit limit is typically set equal to (or based on) that deposit amount — the deposit is refundable, generally when the account is closed in good standing or when the issuer upgrades ("graduates") the account to an unsecured card.
- An unsecured credit card requires no security deposit; approval is based on the applicant's credit history and creditworthiness as assessed by the issuer, which is why unsecured cards are generally harder to qualify for with no credit history or poor credit.
- Secured cards are specifically designed and marketed for people building credit from scratch or rebuilding damaged credit; unsecured cards span the full range from basic no-frills cards to premium rewards cards, and are the more common product for someone with an established, healthy credit history.
- Most (not all) secured card issuers review the account periodically (commonly checked after several months of on-time payments) and may offer to "graduate" the cardholder to an unsecured card, refunding the security deposit at that point — the specific review timeline and graduation criteria vary by issuer and should not be stated as universal.
- Both secured and unsecured cards can report payment activity to the three major credit bureaus, but not every card of either type does — a reader specifically building credit should confirm the specific card reports to all three bureaus before applying, regardless of secured/unsecured status.
- Interest rates, annual fees, and specific rewards structures vary by issuer and card product on both sides, and are not stated as universal facts here.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a secured credit card requires a refundable cash deposit that typically sets the credit limit and exists mainly to build or rebuild credit, while an unsecured credit card requires no deposit but demands enough existing credit history to get approved — someone with no credit file or damaged credit usually starts with secured and works toward qualifying for unsecured.
2. **How a secured card actually works, mechanically** — the deposit-to-limit relationship, why the deposit lowers the issuer's risk (which is what makes approval easier), and the refund mechanic when the account closes in good standing or graduates.
3. **How an unsecured card differs and why it's harder to get started with** — approval based purely on creditworthiness, the wider range of unsecured products (from basic to premium rewards), and why someone with no credit history struggles to get approved without a deposit to offset the issuer's risk.
4. **The graduation path from secured to unsecured** — many (not all) issuers review the account after a period of on-time payments and may offer to convert it to unsecured, refunding the deposit; the specific timeline and criteria vary by issuer, so the reader should ask the specific issuer about their graduation policy before choosing a card.

6. **Verdict** — recommend a secured card for a reader with no credit history or a recent history of missed payments/collections, prioritizing one that reports to all three bureaus and has a clear graduation path; recommend going straight for an unsecured card for a reader who already has an established, reasonably healthy credit history, since tying up a deposit unnecessarily has an opportunity cost.
