## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- An LLC (limited liability company) is, by IRS default, a pass-through entity for tax purposes: a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship (Schedule C) and a multi-member LLC is taxed like a partnership (Form 1065 + K-1s), unless the LLC affirmatively elects corporate taxation.
- An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corp or a C corp by filing the relevant IRS election, but its default legal/tax treatment is pass-through.
- A C corporation pays federal corporate income tax on its own profits (a flat 21% federal rate since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act), and shareholders are taxed again on any dividends distributed — "double taxation" — but the corporation itself can retain earnings without passing tax through to owners' personal returns.
- A C corp has no limit on the number or nationality of shareholders and can issue multiple classes of stock, including the preferred stock that venture capital and institutional investors typically require as a condition of investment.
- An LLC generally cannot issue traditional preferred stock the way a corporation can; LLC ownership is represented by membership interests/units governed by an operating agreement, which is more flexible to customize but less standardized for institutional investors, who are generally set up to invest in C corp stock, not LLC membership interests.
- Both an LLC and a C corp provide limited liability protection to their owners, separate from the tax-treatment choice.
- Many venture-backed startups incorporate directly as Delaware C corps specifically because most U.S. venture capital funds are structured to invest in C corp stock, not LLC membership interests.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: an LLC is the simpler, pass-through-by-default structure best suited to a closely-held small business that isn't chasing venture funding, while a C corp accepts double taxation in exchange for the stock structure (unlimited shareholders, preferred stock) that institutional investors require — the decision usually comes down to whether the business plans to raise venture capital.
2. **Default taxation and paperwork** — LLC's pass-through-by-default tax treatment (Schedule C for single-member, partnership return for multi-member) vs a C corp's mandatory entity-level tax and more formal corporate governance requirements (board, bylaws, corporate formalities).
3. **Why venture-backed startups almost always choose C corp, not LLC** — VC funds are structured to invest in preferred stock, which only a corporation can issue; an LLC's membership-interest structure doesn't fit that investment model, which is why fundraising plans are the single biggest factor pushing founders toward C corp despite the double-taxation cost.
4. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: default taxation, ability to elect corporate tax treatment, stock/ownership structure, investor-readiness for VC funding, governance formality.
5. **Flexibility trade-offs** — LLC operating agreements can be customized far more freely than corporate bylaws/stock structures, which benefits a small, closely-held business with a simple ownership plan but becomes a liability once outside institutional investors need standardized terms.
6. **Verdict** — recommend LLC for a small, closely-held business (or a solo founder) that wants simple pass-through taxation and doesn't plan to raise institutional venture capital; recommend C corp (specifically for a founder actively planning to raise venture funding, issue employee stock options broadly, or bring on many outside investors, where the LLC structure would actively work against the fundraising plan. State who this doesn't serve well (a pre-revenue side project with no near-term funding or liability exposure, where either structure may be premature) and what would change the answer (a firm decision to pursue venture funding, which should trigger converting to or starting as a C corp before terms are negotiated).
7. **Closing** — a concrete next action (decide the funding plan first — VC-backed vs bootstrapped/closely-held — since that answer, more than the tax math alone, usually decides LLC vs C corp).
