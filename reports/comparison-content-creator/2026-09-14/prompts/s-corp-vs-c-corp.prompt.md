## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A C corporation is the default corporate tax status: the corporation itself pays federal corporate income tax on its profits (a flat 21% federal corporate rate, in effect since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017), and then shareholders pay tax again on any dividends distributed to them — commonly called "double taxation."
- An S corporation is a tax election (filed with the IRS via Form 2553) made by an eligible corporation (or LLC electing corporate tax treatment) so that profits and losses pass through to shareholders' personal tax returns instead of being taxed at the corporate level, avoiding double taxation on distributed profits.
- S corp eligibility restrictions: no more than 100 shareholders, shareholders must generally be U.S. citizens or residents (not nonresident aliens), only one class of stock is allowed, and most types of trusts/entities cannot be shareholders (with limited exceptions) — this restricts an S corp's ability to raise venture capital or issue preferred stock.
- A C corp has no shareholder-count limit, can have foreign/nonresident shareholders, and can issue multiple classes of stock (including preferred stock) — this is why venture-backed startups are almost always organized as C corps.
- S corp shareholder-employees must be paid "reasonable compensation" (a W-2 salary) for the services they provide before taking additional profit as a distribution; distributions beyond reasonable salary are not subject to Social Security/Medicare (self-employment-style) tax, which is the main payroll-tax savings mechanism S corp owners use.
- Both S corps and C corps provide limited liability protection to their owners, separate from the choice of tax treatment.
- Do NOT state a specific dollar break-even point at which an S corp election "pays for itself" — that depends on the individual business's profit and reasonable-salary facts, which this page does not have.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: an S corp avoids the double taxation a C corp has by passing profit through to the owners' personal returns, but trades that off against strict eligibility limits (100 shareholders max, one stock class, no foreign shareholders); a C corp accepts double taxation on distributed profits in exchange for unlimited shareholders, multiple stock classes, and easier outside investment — which one fits depends mostly on whether the business plans to raise venture capital or stay closely held.
2. **How each is taxed, mechanically** — C corp's two layers of tax (21% at the entity level, then tax again on dividends to shareholders) vs S corp's pass-through treatment (no entity-level federal income tax; profit and loss flow to shareholders' personal returns via Schedule K-1).
