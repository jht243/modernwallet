## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A sole proprietorship is the default structure for a one-owner business that takes no formal legal-entity action: the owner and the business are legally the same, business income and expenses are reported on Schedule C attached to the owner's personal Form 1040, and net profit is subject to self-employment tax.
- A general partnership is the default structure for a business with two or more owners who take no formal legal-entity action; the partnership itself files an informational return (Form 1065) and issues each partner a Schedule K-1 reporting their share of profit or loss, which the partner then reports on their own personal return — the partnership itself does not pay federal income tax.
- Neither a sole proprietorship nor a general partnership provides any liability shield: in a sole proprietorship, the owner is personally liable for all business debts and legal claims; in a general partnership, each general partner is personally liable for the business's debts and obligations, and — importantly — can also be held liable for the actions of the other partners taken in the course of the business.
- Neither structure requires filing formation paperwork with the state to come into existence (though a state or local "doing business as" / fictitious-name filing may be required to operate under a business name that isn't the owner's own legal name — this varies by state and is not detailed further here).
- A written partnership agreement is not legally required to form a general partnership, but is strongly recommended, since without one, state default partnership law governs profit-splitting, decision-making, and dissolution — and most state default rules split profits and control equally regardless of each partner's actual capital or work contribution.
- Both structures can convert later into an LLC or corporation by filing the appropriate formation paperwork with the state.

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific state filing fee or a specific state's default partnership-law provision; tell the reader default rules vary by state and to check their state's partnership statute or consult an attorney.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a sole proprietorship is the default for one owner and a general partnership is the default for two or more owners who take no formal legal-entity action — both share the same core weakness, unlimited personal liability, and the real decision most readers actually need to make is whether to form an LLC instead of either default structure.
2. **How each is taxed** — sole proprietorship's Schedule C pass-through to the owner's personal 1040 vs a general partnership's Form 1065 informational return plus K-1s issued to each partner, who then reports their share personally; neither structure pays federal income tax at the entity level.
3. **The liability problem both structures share — and the way a partnership makes it worse** — in a sole proprietorship only the owner's own actions create liability; in a general partnership, each partner can be held personally liable for the actions of the OTHER partners taken in the course of business, which is the single biggest risk a two-or-more-owner business takes on by staying an unformalized general partnership rather than forming an LLC.
4. **Comparison table** — at least 4-5 dimensions: number of owners, formation requirement, tax filing (Schedule C vs Form 1065 + K-1), personal liability exposure, need for a written agreement.
5. **Why a written partnership agreement matters even though it isn't legally required** — without one, state default partnership law splits profits/control and governs dissolution, usually in ways that don't reflect each partner's actual capital or work contribution; a written agreement lets partners set their own terms instead.
6. **Verdict** — recommend staying a sole proprietorship only for a very low-risk, one-owner side activity where forming an LLC isn't yet worth the cost/paperwork; recommend that any multi-owner business at minimum draft a written partnership agreement immediately, and strongly consider forming an LLC instead of remaining a general partnership specifically because of the shared-liability-for-partners'-actions risk. State who this doesn't serve well (any business carrying real liability risk — client contracts, employees, physical premises) and what would change the answer (bringing on a co-owner, taking on debt, or hiring employees, any of which should trigger reconsidering the default structure).
7. **Closing** — a concrete next action (before taking on a business partner, at minimum draft a written partnership agreement, and price out forming an LLC instead of defaulting into a general partnership).
