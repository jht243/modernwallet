## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A new car typically loses a large share of its value in the first year of ownership (commonly cited industry estimates put first-year depreciation in the rough range of 20%, and continuing to lose value steadily over the following few years), driven by the "drive-it-off-the-lot" mark-to-market drop plus the first year's mileage/wear — describe this directionally as "roughly a fifth or more in year one" rather than a single precise universal percentage, since the exact figure varies by make, model, and market conditions.
- A used car has already absorbed most of that steepest depreciation curve, so its value tends to decline more slowly going forward compared to a brand-new vehicle in its first few years.
- A new car comes with a full manufacturer warranty from day one; a used car may still have time/mileage remaining on its original factory warranty (if it's a recent model), may be sold with a separate certified pre-owned (CPO) warranty, or may have no remaining factory warranty at all if it's older — the buyer must check the specific vehicle's warranty status individually, since this varies car to car.
- Auto loan interest rates are generally higher for used cars than for new cars, all else equal, in part because lenders view used vehicles as higher risk and often price loan terms based on the vehicle's age/mileage; do NOT state a specific current interest-rate spread, since rates change with monetary policy and vary by lender and borrower credit.
- Sales tax on a vehicle purchase applies in most states regardless of whether the car is new or used, generally based on the purchase price — a lower used-car price generally means a lower sales-tax bill in dollar terms, though the tax rate itself doesn't change with vehicle age. Some states offer a trade-in tax credit that reduces the taxable amount by the trade-in value; rules vary by state.
- New cars come with the latest available safety features and technology for that model; a used car's features are fixed to whatever model year it is, so a buyer prioritizing the newest safety tech has less choice on the used market unless they buy a very recent used model.
- Do NOT state specific current loan APRs, specific state sales-tax rates, or a specific current average vehicle price for either new or used — these change constantly with market and monetary-policy conditions.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a used car is generally the lower-total-cost choice because it has already absorbed the steepest part of a new vehicle's depreciation curve, while a new car trades that cost for a full factory warranty, the newest safety/technology features, and financing that's typically easier to qualify for at a lower rate — the right choice depends on how much the buyer values warranty certainty and new features versus minimizing total cost.
2. **Depreciation: why "drive it off the lot" is a real cost** — the steep first-year depreciation curve on a new car, and why a used car (having already absorbed that drop) tends to hold its value more steadily going forward; note this is directional, not a fixed percentage.
3. **Warranty and reliability certainty** — a new car's full factory warranty from day one vs a used car's variable warranty status (remaining factory coverage, a CPO warranty, or none at all) — the buyer must check the specific used vehicle individually; link to the certified-pre-owned comparison for the middle-ground option.
4. **Financing differences** — used-car loans generally carry higher interest rates than new-car loans, all else equal, and lenders often price used-vehicle loans based on the car's age/mileage; direct readers to the site's own affordability/payoff calculators to run their real numbers rather than assuming a fixed rate spread.
5. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: typical first-year depreciation exposure, warranty status, typical financing rate (higher for used, directionally), access to latest safety/tech features, typical total upfront cost.
6. **Verdict** — recommend used for a buyer prioritizing lower total cost and slower ongoing depreciation, especially if they can find a well-documented, recently-off-lease vehicle with warranty time remaining; recommend new for a buyer who wants full warranty certainty from day one, the newest safety technology, and the easiest/lowest-rate financing, and who plans to keep the car long enough to make the depreciation hit less painful per year of ownership. State who this doesn't serve well (a buyer with poor credit shopping used specifically because they assume it's automatically cheaper to finance — it may not be, given typically higher used-loan rates) and what would change the answer (a manufacturer incentive/low-APR promotion on new models that closes the financing-cost gap).
7. **Closing** — a concrete next action (run the total cost, not just the sticker price, through the site's auto-loan affordability calculator for both a specific new and used option before deciding).
