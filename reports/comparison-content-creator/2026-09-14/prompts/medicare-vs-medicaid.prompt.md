## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- Medicare is a federal health insurance program administered by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), primarily for people age 65 or older, and also available to certain younger people with qualifying disabilities or specific conditions (such as End-Stage Renal Disease). Eligibility and coverage rules are uniform nationwide because it is a federal program.
- Medicaid is a joint federal-and-state health insurance program primarily for people with low income and limited assets; because states administer their own Medicaid programs within federal guidelines, income limits, covered benefits, and eligibility rules vary significantly from state to state.
- Medicare has multiple parts: Part A (hospital insurance), Part B (medical insurance/outpatient care), Part C (Medicare Advantage, private-plan alternative to Original Medicare), and Part D (prescription drug coverage) — this site already has a dedicated Medicare Advantage vs Medigap comparison, so this page should link to it rather than re-explaining Part C/Medigap mechanics in depth.
- Medicaid eligibility is generally based on income (often measured against the federal poverty level) and, in many states, asset limits; some states expanded Medicaid eligibility under the Affordable Care Act to cover more low-income adults, while other states did not adopt that expansion — coverage for the same income level can therefore differ by state of residence.
- A person can be enrolled in both Medicare and Medicaid at the same time — commonly called "dual eligibility" — typically because they are 65+ (or otherwise Medicare-eligible) AND meet their state's Medicaid income/asset limits; Medicaid can then help cover Medicare's premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance, and cover services Medicare doesn't, such as long-term custodial care.
- Medicare does not cover long-term custodial care (help with daily activities like bathing and dressing) in a nursing home or at home on an ongoing basis; Medicaid, after a person spends down assets to their state's limit, does cover long-term nursing home care in most states — see this site's existing long-term care cost calculator for the mechanics of that spend-down.
- Do NOT state specific current premium amounts, specific income thresholds in dollars, or a specific asset limit — these change annually and vary by state for Medicaid; direct the reader to Medicare.gov and their state Medicaid agency for current figures.
