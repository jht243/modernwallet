## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A CD (certificate of deposit) is a bank or credit union deposit product. It is FDIC-insured (bank) or NCUA-insured (credit union) up to $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category.
- A CD pays a fixed rate for a set term, commonly ranging from a few months to five years.
- Withdrawing from a CD before maturity usually triggers an early-withdrawal penalty, commonly quoted as a number of months of interest — the exact amount varies by bank and term (per the CFPB). Do not invent a specific penalty amount.
- A Treasury bill (T-bill) is a short-term debt security issued directly by the U.S. Department of the Treasury. It is backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government (not FDIC/NCUA insurance — a different kind of backing entirely).
- T-bills are sold in common terms of 4, 8, 13, 17, 26, and 52 weeks (per TreasuryDirect).
- A T-bill is sold at a discount to its face (par) value and pays no separate periodic interest coupon; the return is the difference between the discounted purchase price and the face value received at maturity.
- T-bills can be bought directly from the U.S. Treasury at TreasuryDirect.gov, or through a brokerage account, with a minimum purchase of $100 at TreasuryDirect, in $100 increments.
- Interest earned on T-bills (and other Treasury securities) is exempt from state and local income tax, though it is still subject to federal income tax. This is a federal-law exemption specific to Treasury securities and does NOT apply to CD interest, which is fully taxable at both the federal and state level.
- A T-bill bought through a brokerage can typically be sold on the secondary market before maturity; doing so means the price you get depends on where interest rates have moved since you bought it (you could get more or less than you paid), unlike a CD's fixed early-withdrawal-penalty structure.
- Neither product's exact current yield is on this closed fact list — yields on both CDs and T-bills move with the broader interest-rate environment and change week to week. State that rates for both should be checked at the time of the decision (a bank's own CD rate page, and TreasuryDirect.gov or the brokerage's own T-bill listings) rather than quoting a specific percentage.

Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific rate/percentage/price — hedge and tell the reader to verify at the vendor's or Treasury's own page.
