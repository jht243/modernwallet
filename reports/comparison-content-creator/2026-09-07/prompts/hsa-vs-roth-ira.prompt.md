## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- 2026 HSA contribution limit: $4,400 for self-only HDHP coverage, $8,750 for family HDHP coverage (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19, announced in 2025 for the 2026 tax year).
- 2026 HSA catch-up contribution: an extra $1,000 for account holders age 55 or older (this is a per-spouse catch-up; each spouse 55+ needs their own HSA to claim it).
- To contribute to an HSA you must be enrolled in an HDHP (high-deductible health plan) and have no other disqualifying health coverage.
- HSA tax treatment: pre-tax (or tax-deductible) contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals when the money is used for qualified medical expenses — this is commonly called the "triple tax advantage."
- After age 65, HSA funds withdrawn for any non-medical reason are taxed as ordinary income but do NOT incur the 20% early-withdrawal penalty (the penalty that applies to non-medical withdrawals before 65) — functionally similar to a traditional IRA at that point. Before 65, a non-medical withdrawal is taxed as income AND hit with a 20% penalty.
- HSA funds roll over year to year with no "use it or lose it" rule and no expiration, and the account is owned by the individual (portable between employers).
- Some HSA custodians let you invest the balance above a set cash threshold in mutual funds/ETFs; that threshold varies by HSA provider — do not state a specific dollar threshold, since it is provider-specific.
- 2026 Roth IRA contribution limit (already published on this site's roth-ira-vs-roth-401k comparison, reuse exactly): $7,500 ($8,600 if age 50 or older).
- 2026 Roth IRA income phase-out (same source, reuse exactly): contributions phase out between $153,000 and $168,000 of modified AGI for single filers, and between $242,000 and $252,000 for married filing jointly.
- Roth IRA tax treatment: after-tax contributions, tax-free qualified withdrawals (both contributions and earnings, once the 5-year rule and age 59½ are met).
- Roth IRA contributions (the amount you put in, not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time, tax- and penalty-free, since they were already taxed going in.
- Roth IRA has no employer match (it is an individual account, not an employer plan) and no required minimum distributions (RMDs) during the original owner's lifetime.
- HSA has no RMDs at any age.
- Neither account requires you to itemize deductions to get its tax break.
