- This page's own day-rate worked example: a contractor billing 3 days a week for 44 weeks (132
  billable days) who wants $85,000 take-home with $9,000 overhead needs $119,594 of revenue,
  which is $906 a day, or $113.25 an hour on an eight-hour day.
- Pricing the same $85,000 target across an imagined 260 billable days (the common mistake this
  page already warns against) would produce a day rate near $460, about half of what the work
  actually needs to earn.
- General, well-known mechanism facts already stated elsewhere on this page: an hourly rate prices
  scope changes automatically as they happen; a day rate requires defining what "a day" means
  (hours, availability, whether calls/revisions are included) in the contract up front, since a day
  is a unit of time, not a unit of work; a half day rarely costs a client half a day of your
  capacity because of context-switching, so half days are often priced above half the day rate or
  declined; a day rate makes multi-day and retainer bookings easier to quote than hourly does; a
  fixed project fee can beat both for a well-defined deliverable, since it decouples income from
  hours worked.
