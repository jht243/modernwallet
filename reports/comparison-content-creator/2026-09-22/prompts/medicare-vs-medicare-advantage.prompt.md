## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- "Original Medicare" refers to Parts A (hospital insurance) and B (medical insurance), the federal fee-for-service program administered directly by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). Part A generally has no monthly premium for those with 40 quarters of Medicare-covered work; Part B carries a standard monthly premium set annually by CMS.
- "Medicare Advantage" (Part C) is a private-insurance alternative to Original Medicare. CMS pays private insurers (such as UnitedHealthcare, Aetna, Humana) a set amount per enrollee to administer the same Part A and Part B benefits, usually bundled with Part D prescription drug coverage and often extra benefits like dental, vision, or hearing that Original Medicare does not cover.
- Original Medicare lets an enrollee see any doctor or hospital in the U.S. that accepts Medicare, with no provider network and no referral requirement. Medicare Advantage plans generally use a managed-care network (HMO or PPO), which can require staying in-network and, for HMOs, getting referrals to see specialists.
- Original Medicare has no annual out-of-pocket maximum on its own; Part A and Part B coinsurance and deductibles can keep adding up, which is why many Original Medicare enrollees pair it with a supplemental Medigap policy (see this site's existing Medicare Advantage vs Medigap comparison for how Medigap works) or retiree/employer coverage. Medicare Advantage plans are required by CMS to cap in-network annual out-of-pocket spending; the exact dollar cap is set by CMS and varies by plan and year, so do not state a specific dollar figure — direct the reader to Medicare.gov and the plan's own Summary of Benefits for the current cap.
- Medicare Advantage enrollees generally cannot also buy a Medigap policy while enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan (the two are not meant to be combined); someone who wants Medigap protection needs to be on Original Medicare.
- Switching FROM Medicare Advantage BACK TO Original Medicare is allowed during the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (January 1 to March 31 each year) or the Annual Election Period (October 15 to December 7), but picking up a Medigap policy at that point may require medical underwriting outside a federally guaranteed-issue window, so a switch back is not always guaranteed to be affordable — direct the reader to Medicare.gov / SSA rather than stating specific state guaranteed-issue rules, which vary.
- Do NOT state a specific current Part B premium dollar amount, a specific Medicare Advantage out-of-pocket maximum dollar amount, or specific plan premiums — these change annually and vary by plan/insurer/county; tell the reader to check Medicare.gov's Plan Finder or their state's SHIP (State Health Insurance Assistance Program) counselor for current, local numbers.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: Original Medicare is the federal fee-for-service program (Parts A and B) that lets you see any Medicare-accepting provider nationwide with no network, while Medicare Advantage (Part C) is a private-insurer alternative that usually bundles drug coverage and extra benefits inside a managed-care network with a capped annual out-of-pocket maximum — the two are not add-ons to each other, they are two different ways to receive your Medicare benefit, and you generally have to pick one path per enrollment period.
2. **How Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage Actually Differ** (answering section — place the working answer here, before any history/definitions) — network and provider choice, how each is administered (CMS directly vs a private insurer paid by CMS), what's bundled in (Part D and extras vs needing a separate Part D plan), and the annual out-of-pocket-maximum difference.
3. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: who administers it (CMS directly vs private insurer under CMS contract), provider network (any Medicare provider nationwide vs in-network/referral rules), drug coverage (separate Part D plan needed vs usually bundled), extra benefits (none built in vs dental/vision/hearing common), annual out-of-pocket cap (none built in, Medigap fills the gap vs CMS-mandated cap on in-network costs).
4. **Why someone chooses Medicare Advantage** — bundled extra benefits, a built-in annual cap on in-network costs, often lower or $0 premium beyond Part B, tradeoff of network restriction.
5. **Why someone chooses Original Medicare (often with Medigap)** — nationwide provider access with no referrals, useful for snowbirds/frequent travelers or anyone with a specialist/hospital preference outside a local network, paired with a Medigap policy (link to the Medigap comparison) to fill the gap Original Medicare's lack of an out-of-pocket cap leaves open.
6. **Verdict** — commit to a real recommendation: choose Medicare Advantage if you want bundled extra benefits and a capped annual cost and you're comfortable with a local network; choose Original Medicare (paired with Medigap if you want cost predictability) if nationwide provider access without referrals matters more, you travel often, or you have specific doctors/hospitals outside likely local networks. Say who this doesn't serve well (someone who assumes they can freely switch back later without medical underwriting on a Medigap policy — that is not guaranteed everywhere) and what would change the answer (a move to a new county with different plan availability, a new diagnosis that makes network access to a specific specialist critical, or CMS changing the out-of-pocket cap rules).
7. **Closing** — a concrete next action: compare actual plans available in the reader's own county using Medicare.gov's Plan Finder, or call a free SHIP counselor, before their enrollment window closes.
