## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A checking account is a transaction account used for deposits, debit card spending, bill pay, checks, and ATM access.
- A savings account is a deposit account meant for holding money you are not spending soon, and it typically pays more interest than a checking account.
- Both are insured by the FDIC at an FDIC-insured bank (or by the NCUA at a federally insured credit union) up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category.
- Federal Regulation D used to limit certain savings withdrawals and transfers to six per month; the Federal Reserve removed that requirement in April 2020, but some banks still set their own limits or fees, so the reader should check their bank's terms.
- Checking accounts commonly carry overdraft or monthly maintenance fees depending on the bank and the account; many banks waive maintenance fees when you meet a direct deposit or minimum balance requirement. Do not state specific fee amounts.
- Savings accounts at traditional large banks often pay very low interest, while high-yield savings accounts (often online) usually pay much more; rates change, so do not state any specific APY.
- Some banks link a savings account to a checking account as overdraft protection, so a shortfall pulls from savings instead of triggering an overdraft fee; terms vary by bank.
- A common household setup uses checking for bills and spending and savings for an emergency fund and short-term goals; this site covers emergency funds and sinking funds in its comparison of those two.
- Contested/unknown: do not state specific interest rates, fees, minimums, or how many months of expenses an emergency fund must hold (sources differ; say it varies).
