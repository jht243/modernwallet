## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A general partnership forms automatically, with no state filing required, the moment two or more people go into business together for profit; there is no filing fee to create one.
- A multi-member LLC must be formed by filing Articles of Organization with the state and paying a filing fee, commonly $50 to $500 depending on the state; many states also require an annual report or franchise fee afterward.
- By default, a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership for federal purposes — this is the LLC's automatic classification unless the members file IRS Form 8832 to elect corporate taxation instead. That means, by default, a general partnership and a multi-member LLC are taxed identically: both file Form 1065 as an informational partnership return, and both pass profit and loss through to the owners' personal returns via Schedule K-1.
- In a general partnership, each partner has unlimited personal liability for the business's debts, and generally also for the actions of the other partners taken on the business's behalf — this is called joint and several liability.
- An LLC creates a legal separation between the business and its owners' personal assets; creditors of the LLC generally can reach only the LLC's assets, not each member's personal assets, provided the members maintain the separation (a dedicated business bank account, no commingling of funds, and compliance with state filing requirements). Courts can disregard this protection ("piercing the veil") when owners fail to maintain it.
- Partners in a general partnership pay self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on their share of partnership earnings; members of a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership pay the same self-employment tax on their share by default, since the underlying tax treatment is identical.
- An LLC (but not a general partnership directly) can later elect S-corporation tax treatment by filing IRS Form 2553, which can reduce self-employment tax once profit clears a level where a defensible reasonable salary leaves meaningful room above it — roughly $35,000 of profit at a 50% salary, or around $52,000 at a 60% salary, per this site's own S-corp election math; a general partnership would need to first convert to an LLC or incorporate before it could make this election.
- Written partnership and operating agreements are not always legally required, but both a general partnership and an LLC benefit from one in writing — it documents how profit, loss, and decision-making are split, and reduces disputes between co-owners.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a general partnership and a multi-member LLC are taxed exactly the same way by default, both passing profit through to the owners' personal returns, so the real decision isn't tax, it's liability — an LLC separates the owners' personal assets from the business's debts, and a general partnership does not.
2. **What Actually Changes Between a Partnership and an LLC** (answering section, place first) — the identical default tax treatment (Form 1065, Schedule K-1, self-employment tax for both), then the liability difference that actually distinguishes them.
3. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: how it's formed (automatic, no filing vs state filing + fee), default federal tax treatment (partnership pass-through vs identical partnership pass-through by default), personal liability (unlimited, joint and several vs limited to the LLC's assets if formalities are maintained), ongoing state requirements (typically none vs annual report/franchise fee in many states), route to an S-corp election (not directly available vs available via Form 2553).
4. **Why the Tax Question Is a Red Herring** — reinforce with the Form 1065/Schedule K-1/self-employment-tax facts that co-founders often assume an LLC saves them tax by default, and it does not; the S-corp election, not the LLC itself, is what can eventually change the tax math.
5. **Why the Liability Question Is the One That Matters** — unlimited joint-and-several liability in a general partnership (including liability for a partner's own mistakes) versus the LLC's asset-separation, with the veil-piercing caveat about maintaining formalities.
6. **What Forming Each One Actually Involves** — no cost or filing for a general partnership versus articles of organization, a filing fee, and (recommended) an operating agreement for an LLC.
7. **Verdict** — commit to a real recommendation: two or more co-founders should default to an LLC unless the business carries genuinely minimal liability risk and they want to avoid the filing fee and paperwork entirely; a general partnership is a reasonable, low-cost way to test whether a business idea works before formalizing it into an LLC. Say who this doesn't serve well (co-founders in a business with real liability exposure — client-facing work, contracts, inventory, or employees — who stay a general partnership to save the filing fee) and what would change the answer (one partner wanting the S-corp election, which requires converting to an LLC or corporation first).
8. **Closing** — a concrete next action: if you're forming a business with a co-founder and any real liability exposure, file for an LLC in your state before you start operating, and run the numbers on an eventual S-corp election with the site's [S corp tax calculator](/s-corp-tax/) once profit starts to climb.
