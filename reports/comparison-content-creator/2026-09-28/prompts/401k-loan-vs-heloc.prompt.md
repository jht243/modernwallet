## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A 401(k) loan lets a plan participant borrow against their own vested 401(k) balance, up to the IRS limit of the lesser of 50% of the vested balance or $50,000, under Internal Revenue Code Section 72(p). Not every employer plan allows loans; the participant must check their specific plan's rules.
- A 401(k) loan is typically repaid through payroll deduction, generally within five years, except that a loan used to buy a primary residence can have a longer repayment term set by the plan.
- Interest paid on a 401(k) loan goes back into the borrower's own account rather than to a bank, at a rate the plan sets (commonly tied to the prime rate); do not state a specific interest rate — it is plan-specific.
- A 401(k) loan requires no credit check and does not appear on a credit report, because the participant is borrowing from their own account rather than from a lender.
- The biggest risk of a 401(k) loan is job separation: if the borrower leaves or loses their job with an outstanding balance, current tax law generally gives them until the due date of that year's federal tax return (including extensions) to repay the balance in full; any amount not repaid by then is treated as a taxable distribution, subject to ordinary income tax and, if the borrower is under age 59½, a 10% early-withdrawal penalty.
- Money borrowed from a 401(k) stops earning investment returns while it's out of the account, which is an opportunity cost distinct from the interest paid back to the account.
- A HELOC is a revolving line of credit secured by home equity, with a variable interest rate tied to the prime rate; per the [CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf), 2026 HELOC rates run roughly 8.5% to 9.5%. A HELOC typically has a draw period (often up to 10 years) followed by a repayment period, and requires a credit check and a home appraisal to open.
- HELOC interest is only federally tax-deductible if the funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the loan, per IRS Publication 936; a 401(k) loan's interest is never tax-deductible, since it's interest paid to yourself.
- Defaulting on a HELOC risks foreclosure, since the home is the collateral; defaulting on a 401(k) loan (through job loss and non-repayment) triggers tax consequences rather than losing an asset, since there's no separate collateral being foreclosed on.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a 401(k) loan skips the credit check and doesn't touch your home, but it risks your retirement savings and comes due fast if you lose your job, while a HELOC leaves your retirement account alone but puts your home up as collateral and requires good credit and equity to qualify.
2. **What Actually Decides a 401(k) Loan vs a HELOC** (answering section, place first) — what's at risk if you default (retirement tax hit vs foreclosure), qualification requirements (none vs credit/equity), and what you give up either way (investment growth on the borrowed amount vs equity headroom in your home).
3. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: qualification (no credit check vs credit check + appraisal), borrowing limit (lesser of 50% of vested balance or $50,000 vs based on home equity/LTV), interest destination (paid back to your own account vs paid to the lender), what happens on job loss/default (accelerated repayment or taxable distribution vs foreclosure risk), tax deductibility (interest never deductible vs deductible only for home improvement use per IRS Pub 936).
4. **The Job-Loss Risk a 401(k) Loan Carries That a HELOC Doesn't** — the accelerated repayment deadline (tax-filing deadline of that year) and the tax/penalty consequence if it's missed.
5. **The Home-Risk a HELOC Carries That a 401(k) Loan Doesn't** — foreclosure exposure, plus the credit and appraisal requirements that a 401(k) loan skips entirely.
6. **Verdict** — commit to a real recommendation: choose a 401(k) loan if your job is stable, you can't qualify for or don't want a HELOC, and you can repay it well before any risk of job change; choose a HELOC if you have strong home equity and credit, want to keep retirement savings fully invested, and are using the funds for home improvement where the interest can be tax-deductible. Say who this doesn't serve well (someone with an unstable job situation, for whom a 401(k) loan's accelerated-repayment risk is too high) and what would change the answer (a plan that doesn't allow loans at all, or insufficient home equity to open a meaningful HELOC).
