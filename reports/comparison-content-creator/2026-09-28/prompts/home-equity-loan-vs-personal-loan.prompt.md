## THE CLOSED FACT LIST — every number/date/limit this page may state

- A home equity loan is a fixed-rate installment loan secured by the borrower's home equity, sometimes called a second mortgage; the borrower receives the full amount as a lump sum at closing and repays it in equal monthly installments over a set term.
- A personal loan is typically an unsecured, fixed-rate installment loan; the lender does not place a lien on any specific asset, so approval and pricing depend mainly on the borrower's credit profile and income rather than home equity.
- A home equity loan closes more slowly than a personal loan because it requires a home appraisal and title work; a personal loan can often fund within one to a few business days once approved.
- A home equity loan commonly carries closing costs of roughly 2% to 5% of the amount borrowed; a personal loan typically carries no closing costs, though some lenders charge an origination fee deducted from the loan proceeds.
- Personal loan interest rates for borrowers with credit scores above 700 typically run about 8.5% to 15%, and roughly 15% to 25% for fair-credit borrowers in the 640–699 range, per the [CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf). A home equity loan's fixed rate is generally lower than an unsecured personal loan's rate for a comparable borrower, because the home itself backs the loan and lowers the lender's risk — do not state a specific home equity loan rate figure; direct the reader to compare live rates from at least three lenders.
- Because a home equity loan is secured by the home, missing payments risks foreclosure; a personal loan being unsecured means a missed payment damages credit and can lead to collections, but does not put the home directly at risk.
- Interest on a home equity loan is only federally tax-deductible if the loan proceeds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures it, per the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and IRS Publication 936; the deduction is capped at $750,000 of combined mortgage debt ($375,000 if married filing separately), and the borrower must itemize deductions to claim it. Personal loan interest for personal expenses is never tax-deductible.
- Most lenders require a homeowner to retain roughly 15% to 20% equity after the home equity loan, capping combined loan-to-value at about 80% to 85%; a personal loan has no such equity requirement since it isn't secured by the home.

## What to cover, section by section

1. **Intro** — direct answer: a home equity loan usually costs less in interest because it's secured by your home, while a personal loan costs more but keeps your home out of the transaction entirely and closes faster; the right pick depends on how much you're borrowing, how fast you need it, and how much risk you're willing to put on your house.
2. **What Actually Decides Home Equity Loan vs Personal Loan** (answering section, place first) — rate and cost tradeoff (secured, lower rate, closing costs vs unsecured, higher rate, faster/cheaper to close), and the risk tradeoff (foreclosure risk vs credit/collections risk).
3. **Comparison table** — at least 5 dimensions: collateral (home equity vs none), typical rate level (lower, secured vs higher, unsecured), closing speed (appraisal required, slower vs days, faster), closing costs (roughly 2%-5% vs typically none or a small origination fee), what happens on default (foreclosure risk vs credit damage/collections).
4. **When a Home Equity Loan Is the Better Fit** — larger, known expenses (major renovation, debt consolidation of a large balance) where the lower rate saves meaningfully more than the closing costs and slower timeline cost you.
5. **When a Personal Loan Is the Better Fit** — smaller amounts, a need for cash fast, insufficient home equity, or an unwillingness to risk the home for the expense.
6. **The Tax Deduction Most Borrowers Miss** — home equity loan interest is deductible only when the money buys, builds, or substantially improves the home securing it, per IRS Publication 936, and the borrower must itemize; using either loan for debt consolidation or general expenses does not qualify.
7. **Verdict** — commit to a real recommendation: choose a home equity loan for a large, known expense where you have enough equity and can wait through underwriting; choose a personal loan when you need funds within days, the amount is smaller, or you don't want your home tied to the debt.
