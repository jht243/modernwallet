reader question: "Can an LLC own a C corp, and can a C corp own an LLC?"
answer: "Yes to both. A C corporation has no restrictions on who its shareholders can be, so an LLC can hold shares in a C corp; and state LLC statutes generally allow any legal person, including a corporation, to be an LLC member, so a C corp can own an LLC too — most commonly as a wholly-owned subsidiary."

## CLOSED FACT LIST
Anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a percentage, a statute citation you are unsure of, or a URL not listed here.

1. Yes, an LLC can own shares of a C corporation. A C corporation has NO restriction on who may be a shareholder — any legal "person" (an individual, an LLC, a partnership, a trust, or another corporation) can hold C-corp stock.
2. This is a meaningful contrast with an S corporation: an S corp's shareholders are restricted by the tax code to individuals, certain trusts, and estates — an LLC or another corporation generally CANNOT own S-corp stock the way it can own C-corp stock. This is one of the most common points of confusion, and it is the single biggest practical difference between "can an LLC own a C corp" and "can an LLC own an S corp."
3. Yes, a C corporation can own an LLC. Under state LLC statutes, any person or entity — including a corporation — can generally be a "member" of an LLC. A corporation owning a single-member LLC as a wholly-owned subsidiary is a common structure.
4. Federal tax mechanics of a corporation-owned single-member LLC: by default, a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes (under the check-the-box regulations, the same regulations behind IRS Form 8832). This means the subsidiary LLC's activity is not taxed separately — it is reported directly on its parent corporation's own federal tax return, as if the LLC did not exist as a separate taxpayer.
5. Common business reasons a corporation forms an LLC subsidiary: to keep a specific product line, piece of real estate, or intellectual property in its own liability container, separate from the main operating business, or to structure a joint venture as a separately owned LLC with another company.
6. Is an LLC legally "a C corp"? No, not by default. "LLC" is a state-law legal entity type; "C corp" describes a federal tax classification (taxation under Subchapter C of the Internal Revenue Code). By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a disregarded entity and a multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership — neither is C-corp tax treatment. However, an LLC CAN elect to be taxed as a C corp by filing IRS Form 8832, while remaining legally an LLC under state law the whole time. So the accurate answer is: not automatically, but yes if it elects that federal tax treatment.
8. No — electing C-corp tax treatment via Form 8832 does not, by itself, create actual shares of stock. The election changes only how the LLC is taxed at the federal level; the LLC continues to be owned through membership interests under its state-law operating agreement, not stock certificates. To actually issue stock, the business must complete a full legal conversion into a corporation under state law (a separate step from the Form 8832 tax election).

7. Why an LLC (or its members) might hold C-corp stock: as a holding vehicle — e.g., a family investment LLC or a holding-company LLC that owns equity stakes in one or more C corporations. This is different from a startup's own choice of entity: institutional venture capital funds almost always require the OPERATING company itself to be a C corporation (not an LLC), because pooled venture funds with tax-exempt or foreign limited partners cannot cleanly hold pass-through LLC interests, and because a corporation issues the standardized preferred stock institutional investors require. An LLC used as an investment vehicle that then holds C-corp shares is a different situation from an operating startup trying to raise venture capital as an LLC.
