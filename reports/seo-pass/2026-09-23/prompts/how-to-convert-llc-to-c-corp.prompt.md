reader question: "How do you convert an LLC to a C corp?"
answer: "There are two different ways to do it, and they are not the same thing: (1) a pure federal tax election (Form 8832) that keeps the LLC's legal form but changes how it is taxed, or (2) an actual legal conversion under state law that turns the LLC into a corporation. Which one you need depends on why you are converting."

1. There are two distinct paths, and they solve different problems:
   a. TAX ELECTION ONLY (Form 8832): An LLC can file IRS Form 8832 ("Entity Classification Election") to elect to be taxed as a C corporation (an "association taxable as a corporation"), while remaining an LLC under state law. This is often called "checking the box." The LLC does not become a corporation as a legal matter — it keeps its LLC name, its state LLC registration, and its operating agreement. Only its FEDERAL TAX treatment changes: it starts filing Form 1120 and paying the flat 21% federal corporate income tax rate (set by the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act) instead of passing income through to its owners' personal returns.
   b. LEGAL CONVERSION: To actually become a corporation as a legal entity (which is what a venture capital investor or an institutional investor requires, since they need real shares of stock and Delaware/state corporate law protections), the business must complete a conversion under state law. This does NOT happen by filing Form 8832 alone.
2. The two common state-law mechanisms for a full legal conversion:
   a. Statutory conversion — where the state's law allows it, the LLC files a certificate/articles of conversion with the Secretary of State alongside new corporate formation documents. This converts the LLC into a corporation by operation of law in one filing, automatically carrying over the entity's existing contracts, bank accounts, and liabilities to the new corporation, without a separate dissolution step.
   b. Statutory merger (used where statutory conversion is unavailable, or when converting into a different state, e.g. into a new Delaware corporation): the founders form a brand-new corporation, then merge the existing LLC into that new corporation under the state's merger statute, with LLC membership interests converting into shares of the surviving corporation.
3. Not every state offers the statutory-conversion mechanism for every entity-type pairing — availability varies by state, so a business should confirm directly with its state's Secretary of State (or with counsel) which mechanism its state supports before assuming a straightforward one-filing conversion is available.
4. EIN consequence: per IRS guidance ("Do You Need a New EIN?"), a partnership that incorporates is required to obtain a NEW Employer Identification Number — the EIN follows the entity's federal tax classification, not just its state paperwork. A business converting an LLC into a corporation should expect to need a new EIN in this scenario, and should not assume the old EIN automatically carries over.
5. Tax-cost warning: transferring an LLC's assets to a corporation (whether through a statutory conversion, merger, or asset transfer) can trigger a taxable gain if the liabilities being assumed by the new corporation exceed the tax basis of the assets contributed — this is a real risk worth confirming with a CPA before initiating a conversion, especially for a business that has taken on debt.
6. Professional cost: because a conversion involves drafting conversion or merger documents, forming the new corporate entity, issuing stock, and adopting bylaws, founders should expect real legal fees — commonly running into the thousands of dollars — rather than treating it as a routine, no-cost paperwork filing.
7. Form 8832's effective-date window: an election made on Form 8832 can take effect no more than 75 days before the date the form is filed, and no more than 12 months after the date it is filed.
8. Once filed, a Form 8832 election generally cannot be changed again by a new election for 60 months (5 years) from the date the election took effect, subject to limited exceptions described in the form's instructions.
9. Form 8832 (tax election) is separate from Form 2553 (the S-corporation election) — a business that wants pass-through S-corp taxation instead of C-corp taxation files Form 2553, which carries its own shareholder-number and shareholder-type eligibility limits, not covered on this page.
10. Why founders convert at all: institutional venture capital funds overwhelmingly require a C corporation (not an LLC) because pooled funds with tax-exempt or foreign limited partners cannot cleanly hold pass-through LLC interests, and because only a corporation issues the standardized preferred stock institutional investors require.
