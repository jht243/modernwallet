## answer placement

contributions are never federally deductible), but roughly 30 states plus DC DO offer a state
income tax deduction or credit, and whether the reader's own state's plan is required to get it
depends on which state they live in. Do NOT open with a definition of what a 529 plan is (that's

## CLOSED FACT LIST — anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a state's exact
dollar cap, credit percentage, or income limit that is not listed here; say the cap "varies by
state — confirm on your state's own 529 plan site" rather than inventing a number.

1. Contributions to a 529 plan are NEVER deductible on a federal tax return. Earnings grow
   federally tax-free, and withdrawals are federally tax-free when used for qualified education
   expenses. [Source: IRS Topic No. 313, Qualified Tuition Programs — https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc313]
2. Whether a contribution earns a STATE income tax deduction or credit depends entirely on which
   state the taxpayer lives in and files a state return in. States with no state income tax
   (commonly cited group: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee,
   Texas, Washington, Wyoming) cannot offer a 529 deduction, because there is no state income
   tax to deduct against.
3. Most states that DO offer a 529 tax benefit require the contribution to go into THAT STATE'S
   OWN 529 plan to qualify for the deduction or credit.
4. Nine states are "tax-parity" states: they offer a state tax deduction or credit for a
   contribution to ANY state's 529 plan, not only their own. Those nine, by name, are: Arizona,
   Arkansas, Kansas, Maine, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Ohio, and Pennsylvania. [Source:
   SavingForCollege.com, "How Much Is Your State's 529 Plan Tax Deduction Really Worth?" —
   https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-much-is-your-state-s-529-plan-tax-deduction-really-worth]
5. Two states offer a tax CREDIT (a dollar-for-dollar reduction in tax owed) instead of a
   deduction, which is generally more valuable per dollar contributed than a deduction: Indiana
   and Vermont. Indiana's CollegeChoice 529 credit is 20% of the contribution, up to $1,500 per
   year for single/joint filers ($750 for married filing separately) for 2025; Indiana's own
   state treasurer has published that the maximum credit rises starting with the 2026 tax year.
   [Source: Indiana Education Savings Authority — https://www.in.gov/tos/iesa/tax-credit/]
6. Example dollar caps confirmed for this run (use ONLY these four named examples — do not
   extend specific dollar figures to any other state not listed here): New York allows a
   deduction of up to $5,000 for single filers / $10,000 for joint filers who contribute to New
   York's own 529 plan. Illinois allows a deduction of up to $10,000 for single filers / $20,000
   for joint filers who contribute to Illinois' own 529 plan. Colorado and Pennsylvania both
   allow residents to deduct their FULL annual 529 contribution with no stated dollar cap.
   [Source: SavingForCollege.com state-benefit research, same URL as fact 4 — this is aggregated,
   frequently-updated industry data, not a single state's own filing, so state each figure as
   "as most recently reported" rather than as a guaranteed-current number, and tell the reader to
   confirm the current-year cap on their own state's 529 plan site before filing.]
7. A 529 state tax deduction is generally available only in the year the contribution is made —
   it is not retroactive, and most states do not allow carrying forward an unused deduction
   indefinitely (state rules vary on carryforward; do not state a specific carryforward period
   for any state not named above).
8. The existing `/529-savings-calculator/` on this site projects account GROWTH (compounding a
   balance and monthly contributions to age 18) and does not model any state tax deduction or
   credit — this new page is the place that answers the tax-benefit question the calculator does
   not.
