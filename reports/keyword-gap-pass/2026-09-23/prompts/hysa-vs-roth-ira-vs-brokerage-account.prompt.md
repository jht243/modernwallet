## CLOSED FACT LIST — anything not on this list, you do not know. Never invent a specific
brokerage's fee, a specific HYSA's APY, or a Roth IRA number not listed here.

1. For 2026, the IRA contribution limit (which applies to Roth IRA) is $7,500, plus an
   additional $1,100 catch-up contribution for savers age 50 and older ($8,600 total for
   50-plus). [Source: IRS, "401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026, IRA limit increases to
   $7,500" — https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500]
2. Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes. For 2026, single filers and heads of
   household can contribute the full amount below $153,000 modified adjusted gross income
   (MAGI), a reduced amount between $153,000 and $168,000, and nothing above $168,000. Married
   couples filing jointly can contribute the full amount below $242,000 MAGI, a reduced amount
   between $242,000 and $252,000, and nothing above $252,000. [Same IRS source as fact 1.]
3. A Roth IRA has no such income limit or contribution cap for a plain taxable brokerage account
   — a brokerage account can hold any amount, from any income level, with no annual contribution
   ceiling.
4. Roth IRA CONTRIBUTIONS (the money the saver put in, not the earnings) can be withdrawn at any
   time, for any reason, with no tax and no penalty, because contributions were already made with
   after-tax dollars. [Source: IRS, Roth IRAs — https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/roth-iras]
5. Roth IRA EARNINGS are a different story: withdrawing earnings before age 59½ AND before the
   account has been open 5 years generally triggers both ordinary income tax and a 10% early
   withdrawal penalty on the earnings portion, with limited exceptions (the IRS lists several,
   including a first-time home purchase up to a $10,000 lifetime limit, certain education costs,
   and a few others). [Same IRS source as fact 4.]
6. A high-yield savings account (HYSA) has NO withdrawal restriction, no age rule, and no 5-year
   rule at all — money can be added or removed at any time, and the entire balance (contributions
   and interest both) was always freely accessible. This is the core liquidity tradeoff against
   both the Roth IRA and, less so, the brokerage account.
7. In a plain taxable brokerage account, selling an investment held for MORE than one year is
   taxed at long-term capital gains rates, which for 2026 are 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on
   taxable income (roughly: 0% up to $49,450 single / $98,900 married filing jointly; 15% up to
   $545,500 single / $613,700 married filing jointly; 20% above that). Selling an investment held
   for ONE YEAR OR LESS is taxed as ordinary income (short-term capital gains), at the investor's
   regular income tax bracket, which is usually a higher rate. [Source: IRS Topic 409, Capital
   Gains and Losses — https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409]
8. HYSA interest is taxed as ordinary income every year it is earned, regardless of whether the
   money is withdrawn — this is already established on this site's `/investing/high-yield-savings-calculator/`
   page.
9. A brokerage account carries market risk: its value can fall, unlike a HYSA's principal, which
   is FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, and does not
   fall in value from market movements. [Source: FDIC — https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance]
10. A narrower, related page already exists on this site — `/compare/custodial-roth-ira-vs-hysa/`
    — but it is scoped specifically to a CUSTODIAL account opened FOR A MINOR CHILD, not this
    page's general adult-saver question. Do not duplicate that page's custodial-specific content
    (age-of-majority transfer rules, kiddie tax); this page is about an adult's own accounts.
