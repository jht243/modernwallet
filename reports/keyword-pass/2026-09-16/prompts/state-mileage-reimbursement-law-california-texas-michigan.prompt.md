## The one thing this page must get right

The IRS standard mileage rate (72.5 cents/mile Jan 1 - Jun 30, 2026; 76 cents/mile Jul 1 - Dec 31, 2026) is a FEDERAL TAX figure for the self-employed Schedule C deduction — it is not a state law and it does not, by itself, require any employer to reimburse anyone anything. Whether a W-2 employee's employer must reimburse mileage at all is a SEPARATE, state-by-state legal question. California has a real statutory reimbursement duty. Texas and Michigan do not have a state-specific mileage reimbursement statute for private employers — say this plainly, it is itself the useful answer for a Texas or Michigan searcher who assumes every state works like California.

## CLOSED FACT LIST — you may state ONLY these facts/numbers. Anything else, you do not know; say so and point the reader to the primary source instead of guessing.

1. 2026 IRS business standard mileage rate: 72.5 cents/mile for miles driven January 1 through June 30, 2026; 76 cents/mile for miles driven July 1 through December 31, 2026. Source: IRS — Standard mileage rates (https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/standard-mileage-rates).
2. 2025 IRS business standard mileage rate (for context/comparison, since several keyword variants ask about "2025"): 70 cents/mile, the whole year. Source: same IRS page above.
3. This IRS rate is the figure self-employed filers use to compute the Schedule C deduction covered elsewhere on this hub. It is not itself an employer-reimbursement law for W-2 employees.
4. California Labor Code Section 2802(a) (exact statutory text you may quote or closely paraphrase): "An employer shall indemnify his or her employee for all necessary expenditures or losses incurred by the employee in direct consequence of the discharge of his or her duties." Section 2802(c) defines "necessary expenditures or losses" as including "all reasonable costs." Source: California Legislative Information, Labor Code 2802 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=LAB&sectionNum=2802.).
5. California courts and the state apply Section 2802 to require employers to cover the necessary cost of an employee's personal-vehicle use for work. Employers may reimburse this in different ways — a per-mile rate (commonly the IRS rate), actual-expense reimbursement, or a lump-sum/stipend — but whichever method is used, it must actually cover the employee's necessary costs; the IRS rate is a common benchmark, not a ceiling written into the statute itself. Do not state a specific court case name or holding — you do not have a verified citation for one; describe this as how the statute's "necessary expenditures" language is applied, not as a named case.
6. Texas has no state statute requiring private-sector employers to reimburse employees for mileage or business vehicle use, and sets no state-mandated reimbursement rate for private employers.
7. Michigan has no state statute requiring private-sector employers to reimburse employees for mileage or business vehicle use, and sets no state-mandated reimbursement rate for private employers.
8. The federal floor that still applies in EVERY state, including Texas and Michigan: under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), an employer cannot require an employee to bear a business expense (a personal vehicle used as a "tool of the trade" is treated this way) if doing so cuts the employee's actual pay below the federal minimum wage of $7.25/hour in any workweek — this is the wage "kickback" / "free and clear" principle. Source: U.S. Department of Labor, Field Assistance Bulletin No. 2009-2 (https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/field-assistance-bulletins/2009-2).
9. The U.S. Department of Labor has confirmed (WHD Opinion Letter FLSA2020-12, August 31, 2020) that the FLSA does NOT require an employer to reimburse at the IRS standard mileage rate specifically — a "reasonable approximation" of the employee's actual vehicle expenses, computed by any appropriate method, satisfies federal law. Source: https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/WHD/opinion-letters/FLSA/2020_08_31_12_FLSA.pdf.
10. The federal minimum wage is $7.25/hour and has been unchanged since July 24, 2009.
11. Texas' own state minimum wage matches the federal floor — Texas has not set a higher state minimum wage.
12. Michigan DOES set its own state minimum wage, which is higher than the federal floor: $13.73/hour effective January 1, 2026, under Michigan's Improved Workforce Opportunity Wage Act. Source: Michigan Department of Labor and Economic Opportunity (https://www.michigan.gov/leo/news/2025/12/08/michigans-minimum-wage-set-to-increase-on-jan-1-2026). Frame this as: in Michigan, the same FLSA-style "does unreimbursed driving cut you below minimum wage" math has more room before it bites, because the wage floor itself sits well above $7.25 — but frame it as the state's OWN minimum wage law providing that floor, not as a mileage-specific mandate; do not conflate the two.
