## The one thing this page must get right — state it EARLY, in the intro

"Lemon law mileage offset" is a CONSUMER AUTO WARRANTY LAW calculation — how much a manufacturer may deduct from a refund/buyback for the use a driver already got out of a defective vehicle before it was fixed. It has NOTHING to do with the IRS standard mileage TAX deduction the rest of this hub covers (the federal per-mile figure a self-employed driver deducts on Schedule C). Say this distinction plainly in the first section so a reader is not confused about which "mileage" topic they landed on, and do not blend the two calculations anywhere on the page.

## CLOSED FACT LIST — state ONLY these facts/numbers. Anything else, you do not know; say so and tell the reader to check their own state's statute.

1. A "lemon" is a new vehicle with a defect covered by warranty that substantially impairs its use, value, or safety, and that the manufacturer has been unable to fix after a reasonable number of repair attempts. State "lemon laws" (all 50 states have some version) give the buyer the right to a refund or a replacement vehicle in that situation.
2. When a refund/buyback is awarded, the manufacturer is allowed to subtract a "mileage offset" (also called a "usage fee") to account for the value the buyer already got from driving the car before the defect was reported/repaired. This is normal and expected — it is not the manufacturer shortchanging the buyer, it is how these buyback laws are designed to work.
3. California's Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act sets out the clearest, most fully documented version of this formula, and California Civil Code Section 1793.2(d)(2)(C) states the mileage offset is calculated by "multiplying the actual price of the new motor vehicle paid or payable by the buyer, including any charges for transportation and manufacturer-installed options, by a fraction having as its denominator 120,000 and having as its numerator the number of miles traveled by the new motor vehicle prior to the time the buyer first delivered the vehicle to the manufacturer or distributor, or its authorized service and repair facility, for correction of the problem that gave rise to the nonconformity." Source: California Legislative Information, Civil Code 1793.2 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1793.2.).
4. In plain terms, California's formula is: offset = purchase price x (miles driven before the FIRST repair visit for that specific defect / 120,000).
5. The critical timing detail: only miles driven BEFORE the first attempt to repair the specific defect count toward the offset — not the total mileage on the odometer at the time of the buyback, and not miles driven during the period the car went back and forth for repeated repair attempts. A car that racked up many more miles after that first repair visit (including miles the dealer or buyer put on it during subsequent attempts) does not have those later miles counted against the buyer.
6. Under California's statute, the mileage offset is the ONLY deduction the manufacturer may subtract from a refund buyback. Everything else the buyer is owed — the vehicle price, sales tax, registration fees, and finance/loan charges — is reimbursed in full, on top of the offset being the sole reduction.
7. Other states have their OWN lemon law statutes with their OWN mileage-offset or usage-fee mechanics, and the exact formula, the mileage divisor, and the cutoff point for which miles count can differ from California's 120,000-mile formula. Do not state a specific number, divisor, or formula for any state other than California — you were not able to verify one for any other state's statute text directly; tell the reader plainly to check their own state's lemon law statute or consult a lemon-law attorney in their state for the exact formula that applies there.
8. Worked example (compute this yourself from the California formula in fact #3-4; use these exact inputs, not a different set): a buyer paid $28,000 for a new vehicle and had driven it 6,000 miles before the first repair visit for the defect that later triggered the buyback. The offset is $28,000 x (6,000 / 120,000) = $1,400. So of a $28,000 purchase price, $1,400 is deducted for use, and the buyer is still owed the remaining $26,600 of the vehicle price (plus tax, fees, and finance charges owed in full separately, per fact #6).
9. This page is explaining the concept and the (California) formula; it is not legal advice, and the exact outcome of any buyback depends on the buyer's own state's statute and the specific facts of their case.
